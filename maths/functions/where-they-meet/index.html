Where they meet

Two plans can swap places. Read where a curve and a line meet from a graph, say the coordinates, then zoom in or halve the gap in a table to pin the crossing down.

Two ways to save: one starts ahead, one starts behind. When does the one behind pull level?

What you should already know:
  • Change a and b in a line · In y = ax + b, a sets how steep the line is and b sets where it starts.
  • Writing the model · A rate of r% a year multiplies by 1 + r/100 each year.

Two plans for saving. Plan A is steady: $2,000 now, and $300 more each year. Plan B starts with $1,000 and grows by 20% a year. The 20% is made up, and much higher than a real savings account, so that the plans swap places in a short time.

How this lesson works: Predict, Try, Investigate, Modify, Practise, Make
  • Predict: Read the problem and decide what you think before you try anything. A wrong guess is fine: it shows you what to look for.
  • Try: Try it on the board and compare the result with your prediction.
  • Investigate: Work out why it happens, one step at a time.
  • Modify: Change the problem on purpose and see what happens.
  • Practise: A few on your own, to check you have it.
  • Make: Make your own problem using what you have learned. Expect a few tries; that is normal.

Explain it to me

Where two graphs meet, the two values are equal. The coordinates of that point tell you both the time and the common value.

To find a crossing exactly enough:

  1. Read a window from the graph.
  2. Zoom in on that window, or make a table of the two values in it.
  3. Find where the one in front changes, and halve the gap again if you need more figures.

For teachers

Students find where an exponential curve and a line meet from a graph, state the coordinates, and refine the answer by zooming in or by halving the gap in a table (AC9M10A05, node 2). About 45 minutes.

Open the teacher guide: answers, solutions, slides and a worksheet.

Students will be able to

  • I can read where two graphs meet and say what the coordinates mean.
  • I can narrow a crossing down by zooming in or by comparing values in a table.
  • I can say which plan is better before and after the crossing.

Before class

Do the three boards yourself first: the full window (Run), the 6 to 10 window (Investigate) and the 8 to 8.4 window (Modify). Students need the exponential model from lesson 6 and where two lines meet from Year 9.

The one idea: the crossing is the decision. Before it one plan wins and after it the other, so the right choice depends on how long you save. The first graph gives a window, not an answer; zooming in or halving the gap narrows it to the accuracy you need.

All numbers are made up: $2,000 and $300 a year for plan A, $1,000 and 20% a year for plan B. 20% is much higher than a real savings account; it is chosen so the plans swap in a short time. Say so in class.

The board marks the crossing with its coordinates (8.2, 4460). The board has no zoom control, so the zoom is three boards with smaller windows.

Running the lesson

  1. Predict: take a show of hands. Those who chose Plan A assumed the plan that starts ahead stays ahead. Don't correct it yet.
  2. Run: ask students to move r and say which way the crossing goes.
  3. Paper: the try-one after the worked example and the dollars gap in Make are worked on paper first. Look for the two values written side by side at each x.
  4. Modify: before the 'swap back' edge case, ask what the line adds each year and what the curve adds.

The hard part (on purpose)

Stopping at one comparison. Ask 'is that true for every year, or just that one?' and have them check a second year on the other side.

When students get stuck

  • A student compares at one time only (Jo's mistake). Ask: Is the same plan ahead at 6 years? Fix: Check one year on each side. If the leader changes, the plans cross in between.
  • A student reads the crossing as a point on one axis only (8.2, or 4460). Ask: What are the two numbers, and what does each one mean? Fix: Write it as a point: 8.2 years is the time, $4,460 is the amount both plans are worth then.
  • A student stops at 'between 8 and 9' when asked for 1 decimal place. Ask: Which of 8.1, 8.2 and 8.3 is closest to equal? Fix: Make a table at those values and compare the two columns.

Stretch and support

Stretch: Find a rate r for which the plans meet at about 5 years. Then: is there a rate for which they never meet in 15 years? Which of the three windows is the board using when you do?

Support: Print the table for 6 to 10 years and highlight which plan is larger in each row. Draw a line where the highlight changes.

What each step is for

  • who is ahead at 10 years?: Committing to who is ahead after 10 years shows who thinks the plan that starts ahead stays ahead (A) and who sees that a share of a growing balance eventually outgrows the same dollars each year.
  • look for the crossing: Where the curve meets the line, the two plans are equal. The graph marks that point and gives its coordinates.
  • zoom in: The first look gives a window, not an answer. Zooming in narrows the window until the crossing is known to the accuracy you need. Comparing at one time says who is ahead then, not where they swap.
  • change the rate: A smaller zoom window gives more figures. A different rate moves the crossing, and a very small rate means the plans may not meet in the window at all.
  • find the crossing: Reading a crossing from two values, from a bracket of years, and (review) finding where two lines meet.
  • back to the plans: Coming back to the savings: the crossing is the decision. Before it the steady plan wins, after it the compounding one wins, so the right choice depends on how long you save.
Now you can
  • Read where two graphs meet and say what the coordinates mean.
  • Narrow a crossing down by zooming in or by comparing values in a table.
  • Say which plan is better before and after the crossing.
