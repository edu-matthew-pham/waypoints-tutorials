Reasoning with angles

Find unknown angles in steps, giving a reason for each: what you know, the rule that links it to the next angle, and the reason in words. Parallel-line rules join straight-line and vertically opposite angles.

A detective does not guess: every step has evidence. Finding an angle is the same. Each angle you find needs a reason, and the reasons are what make the answer trustworthy.

What you should already know:
  • Finding angles with parallel lines · When the lines are parallel, corresponding angles are equal, alternate angles are equal, and co-interior angles add to 180°.

You know the rules: corresponding and alternate angles are equal, co-interior angles add to 180∘180^\circ180∘, and from Year 6, vertically opposite angles are equal and angles on a straight line add to 180∘180^\circ180∘.

Most problems need more than one rule. So you'll find angles in steps, and say which rule you used at each one. Write your working your own way: short notes are fine. The checker marks your answer and the reason at the step that matters; you compare the rest with a model.

How this lesson works: Predict, Try, Investigate, Modify, Practise, Make
  • Predict: Read the problem and decide what you think before you try anything. A wrong guess is fine: it shows you what to look for.
  • Try: Try it on the board and compare the result with your prediction.
  • Investigate: Work out why it happens, one step at a time.
  • Modify: Change the problem on purpose and see what happens.
  • Practise: A few on your own, to check you have it.
  • Make: Make your own problem using what you have learned. Expect a few tries; that is normal.

Explain it to me

Most angle problems take more than one step. For each step:

  1. Know: an angle you already have.
  2. Choose: a rule that links it to a new angle: corresponding or alternate angles are equal, co-interior angles add to 180∘180^\circ180∘, vertically opposite angles are equal, angles on a straight line add to 180∘180^\circ180∘.
  3. Say why: write the rule as the reason.

For teachers
Students find unknown angles at parallel lines in two-step chains, with a reason for each step, writing their working their own way; the platform marks the answer and the reason at the key step, then shows a model to compare with (AC9M7M04, node 3). About 45 minutes.
Open the teacher guide: answers, solutions, slides and a worksheet.
Students will be able to
  • I can find an unknown angle in two or more steps, using parallel-line, straight-line and vertically opposite angles.
  • I can give a reason for each step, naming the rule.
  • I can use angles to show whether two lines are parallel.
Before class
Do the first reasoning task yourself first, then compare with its model. Students need both parallel-lines lessons and Year 6 angle facts (straight line, vertically opposite, at a point).


The one idea: know, choose the rule, say why. Every angle found needs a named reason. The common mistake is linking two angles that only look related (Kai's b and e); it is planted after the routine.


The reasoning tasks do not mark the free working: students write it their own way (notes, abbreviations such as "vert. opp." and "co-int." are fine). The answer is marked, and at one key step students choose the reason from a list, so the misconception gets feedback. Then they compare their working with a model and tick a short self-check. Look at the working in class: it is where reasoning shows.


Running the lesson
  1. Predict: take a show of hands. Students who chose 70° are assuming b and h are linked directly.
  2. Run: ask students to say the two rules that make h = 180° − b true for every crossing angle.
  3. Investigate: model know, choose, say why on the board, writing the reason in short form. Accept any correct route.
  4. Modify: collect two different routes to f and ask which is shorter.
  5. Make: ask students to say their two steps before they turn the line.
  6. Make on paper: ask for the reason at each line before anyone types an answer.
The hard part (on purpose)
Choosing which angle to find first. Ask: which angle can you get from the one you know in one step, and what is the reason?
When students get stuck
  • A student links two angles that only look related (Kai's mistake). Ask: Which rule links them? Name it. Fix: If no rule links them directly, find an angle in between that does.
  • A student gets the right angle with no reasons. Ask: How would you convince someone who did not see the diagram? Fix: Write one reason per line: the angle, then why.
  • A student cannot find a first step. Ask: Which angles can you find straight from the given one? Fix: List every angle one rule away from the given one; the target is usually among those, or one more step on.
Stretch and support
Stretch: Write a three-step puzzle for a partner: give one angle, ask for an angle three rules away, and write the model reasons.
Support: Allow the Run board for checking each step: set the given angle, pick pairs and read the names.
Where it leads
Next: angle sums of triangles and polygons, then Unknown angles in shapes, which combines them with these rules.


Where it leads: geometric proof (Years 8 to 10), where a chain of reasons is the proof.


What each step is for
  • two steps: Committing to h when only b is known shows whether they look for a chain (b to f to h) or assume a link between b and h.
  • check the chain: Setting b to 70° and comparing h shows the two-step answer, and turning the line shows h is always 180° minus b: the chain works for every angle.
  • reasons for every step: Know, choose the rule, state the reason: each step links an angle you know to the next by a named rule. Kai's mistake links two angles that only look related.
  • one change at a time: One change at a time: a different starting angle, a different route, the reverse direction, then lines that are not parallel and showing lines are parallel from the angles.
  • angle chains: Two-step chains with each rule, mixed, with a reason named every time; the review keeps the pair names apart from vertically opposite angles.
  • set it up: Working backwards from a target angle two steps away, then checking on the board, reverses the chain: reasoning first, the board only confirms.
  • a stair cut, on paper: A stair stringer makes the treads two parallel lines and the sloping edge a transversal; the cut needs a two-step chain with a reason at each step.
