Write the algorithm

Turn the pinned-down sprinkler job into numbered steps with a decision and a loop, exact enough for a machine, then run it by hand on two ovals.

Hand the sprinkler plan to a friend who has never seen the job. Could they follow it step by step and get your answer, with nobody to ask?

What you should already know:
  • Pin the problem down · Make a spatial problem exact: what goes in, what comes out, what counts as done.
  • Testing an algorithm: where does it fail? · Good tests include the boundary and the strange cases, not just easy ones.

Last time you pinned the sprinkler job down: an oval that's L long and W wide, a spray that reaches r, and every point within r of a sprinkler. Now the plan has to be written so exactly that a machine could follow it. You found that one sprinkler covers a square a bit under 1.41 × r wide, so the plan uses 1.4 × r to stay on the safe side, and the oval is cut into cells that size or smaller with a sprinkler in the middle of each. The tricky part is deciding how many cells fit, so have a guess at that before the steps.

How this lesson works: Predict, Try, Investigate, Modify, Practise, Make
  • Predict: Read the problem and decide what you think before you try anything. A wrong guess is fine: it shows you what to look for.
  • Try: Try it on the board and compare the result with your prediction.
  • Investigate: Work out why it happens, one step at a time.
  • Modify: Change the problem on purpose and see what happens.
  • Practise: A few on your own, to check you have it.
  • Make: Make your own problem using what you have learned. Expect a few tries; that is normal.

Explain it to me

An algorithm is exact steps that work for any input. Some steps compute a number, one decides whether to go on, and a loop repeats the placing for every cell.

For the sprinklers: find s = 1.4 × r, count the columns and rows with each rounded up, work out the cell size, then put a sprinkler in the middle of every cell. It always covers the oval; it doesn't always use the fewest.

Make your prediction first. Help opens once you've locked it in.

The cell size

Steps 3 to 5 on the junior oval (L = 40, W = 24, r = 8): s = 1.4 × 8 = 11.2, then cols = 40 ÷ 11.2 rounded up. After that, w = 40 ÷ cols. How wide is one cell, in metres?

Steps, decisions and loops

Say which steps compute, which decide, and which repeat. Here are the steps in full.

  1. Read the oval's length L, its width W and the spray's reach r, all in metres.
  2. If L, W or r is zero or less, say "not an oval" and stop.
  3. Set s = 1.4 × r.
  4. Set cols = L ÷ s, rounded up, and rows = W ÷ s, rounded up.
  5. Set w = L ÷ cols and h = W ÷ rows.
  6. For each row j from 1 to rows, and each column i from 1 to cols, put a sprinkler at x = (i − 0.5) × w and y = (j − 0.5) × h.
  7. Say how many sprinklers there are (cols × rows), and where each one is.

For teachers

Students turn the pinned-down sprinkler job into numbered steps with a decision and a loop, run the steps by hand on two ovals, and find a bug where a sprinkler is placed on the edge of its cell instead of the middle (AC9M10SP03, node 2). About 45 minutes.

Open the teacher guide: answers, solutions, slides and a worksheet.

Students will be able to

  • I can write the steps of an algorithm exactly enough for a machine.
  • I can say which steps compute, which decide and which repeat.
  • I can run an algorithm by hand on two different inputs.

Before class

Do the Predict and the senior oval yourself first. Students need Pin the problem down, Pythagoras and the Year 9 idea that an algorithm has computation and decision steps.

The one idea: an algorithm is exact steps that work for any input. Here the steps cut the oval into cells no wider than 1.4 × the reach (the biggest square is about 1.41 × the reach, so 1.4 is on the safe side), with a sprinkler in the middle of each cell. A decision guards the input and a loop places one sprinkler in every cell.

The steps always cover the oval, but they don't always use the fewest sprinklers: the 11.5 m by 4 m oval in Modify needs two when one would do. That is on purpose, to show the gap. Testing awkward ovals (a curved one, a tree in the middle), finding where the algorithm fails and refining it is node 3, the class project, and isn't covered here.

The junior and senior ovals, the reach and the sizes are all made up. A flowchart or pseudocode both work; the numbered steps and the flowchart in the lesson say the same thing.

Running the lesson

  1. Predict: take a show of hands. Most will say 6 or 9. Ask a student to say what happens to the leftover strip.
  2. Paper: the senior oval and the third oval are worked on paper first, a step at a time, with the steps beside you.
  3. Investigate: ask a student to point at the loop and say what would have to be written without it.
  4. Modify: before 11.5 m by 4 m, ask whether the algorithm will place one or two.
  5. Make: ask whether the answer meets the success criterion, and how they know.

The hard part (on purpose)

Rounding a count down, and putting a sprinkler on the edge of its cell. Ask: what is left over if you round down? Where is the middle of the first cell?

When students get stuck

  • A student rounds 3.57 to 4 for one side and 2.14 to 2 for the other. Ask: What happens to the last strip on the short side? Fix: Round both counts up: any part-cell still needs a sprinkler.
  • A student sets the cell size to s, not to L ÷ cols. Ask: Do the cells fill the oval exactly? Fix: Divide each side by the rounded count, so the cells fit with no gaps.
  • A student puts the sprinkler at x = i × w. Ask: Where is the middle of the first cell? Fix: Half a cell in: (i − 0.5) × w.

Stretch and support

Stretch: Write the same algorithm in pseudocode with a loop that reads a list of ovals and prints each count, then try a staggered layout, where every second row is shifted half a cell, and see whether it needs fewer sprinklers.

Support: A printed copy of the seven steps and a blank table with columns for s, cols, rows, w, h and the count.

Where it leads

This covers AC9M10SP03 node 2.

Node 3 (testing, refining and justifying) is left to class: the project is to test this algorithm on the school's own oval, find where it fails, and refine it. Where it leads: the same habit of pinning down, then writing steps, then testing, for any spatial job.

What each step is for

  • how many cells?: Committing to a number of sprinklers shows who rounds a count down or to the nearest whole number. The algorithm's key detail is that a part-cell still needs a sprinkler, and the oval has two different sides to count.
  • the cell size: Following the first steps of the algorithm on a real oval shows that after rounding up the cells come out a little smaller than the biggest square, and that is why they stay covered.
  • steps, decisions and loops: An algorithm for a spatial problem has computation steps, a decision that guards the input, and a loop that repeats one placement for every cell. Zoe's mistake is a wrong formula in the loop: the sprinkler goes on the cell's edge, not its middle.
  • change the oval: Running the algorithm on an oval with an exact fit, on one that is just over, and with a different spray, to see what changes and what the algorithm does when the answer isn't the fewest.
  • run it: Running the algorithm on new ovals, finding a sprinkler's position, spotting a boundary case, and a review of a cell's half-diagonal.
  • the other oval: Returning to the sprinkler job: running the whole algorithm on a new oval, then checking the answer against the success criterion.
