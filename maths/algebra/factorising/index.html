Factorising

Factorising is expanding backwards. Share the terms into equal rows: 6x + 9 is 3 rows of 2x + 3, so 3(2x + 3). Take out the highest common factor, then check by expanding.

6 x-tiles and 9 ones. Can you share them into equal rows, with nothing left over?

What you should already know:
  • Expanding brackets · The number outside a bracket multiplies every term inside it.

In Expanding brackets you turned 3(2x+4)3(2x + 4)3(2x+4) into 6x+126x + 126x+12. Factorising goes the other way: from 6x+126x + 126x+12 back to a bracket.

How this lesson works: Predict, Try, Investigate, Modify, Practise, Make

Explain it to me

Factorising is expanding backwards. Find a number that goes into every term, divide every term by it, and put it outside the bracket.

6x+96x + 96x+9: 3 goes into 6 and 9, so 6x+9=3(2x+3)6x + 9 = 3(2x + 3)6x+9=3(2x+3). Take out the highest common factor: 12x−8=4(3x−2)12x - 8 = 4(3x - 2)12x−8=4(3x−2), not 2(6x−4)2(6x - 4)2(6x−4), which still shares a 2. A letter can be common too: 2x2+4x=2x(x+2)2x^2 + 4x = 2x(x + 2)2x2+4x=2x(x+2).

Check by expanding: you should get back to where you started.

    For teachers

    Students share algebra tiles into equal rows to factorise sx + t, learn to take out the highest common factor (numbers, then letters) and check by expanding (AC9M8A01, node 2). About 45 minutes.

    Open the teacher guide: answers, solutions, slides and a worksheet.

    Students will be able to

    • I can factorise an expression by taking out a common factor.
    • I can take out the highest common factor, including a letter, so the expression is fully factorised.
    • I can check a factorisation by expanding it.

    Before class

    Do the Run board and the Make task yourself first. Students need Expanding brackets (the lesson before) and highest common factors from Year 7 Number.

    The one idea: factorising divides every term by a common factor, and the factor goes outside the bracket. The common mistake is taking out a common factor that is not the highest (12x − 8 = 2(6x − 4)); it is planted after the method, in Ava's working. Her answer is equal to 12x − 8, so it is not wrong, just not finished: that is the conversation to have.

    Answers are marked by form: an equal answer that could still be factorised further is told so ("could take out more") rather than marked right.

    Running the lesson

    1. Predict: take a show of hands. Students who chose 6(x + 9) divided only the first term; those who chose 2(3x + 4.5) found a factor of 6 but not of 9. Do not correct it yet.
    2. Run: ask why 2 rows do not work (9 ones do not share into 2) and why 3 does.
    3. Investigate: model the worked example live, including the check by expanding. Then ask: is Ava's answer wrong?
    4. Modify: before the letter task, ask what goes into both 2x² and 4x. Students often find the 2 and miss the x.
    5. Make: collect students' expressions on the board and ask which ones are fully factorised with 4 outside.
    6. Paper: equal rows is worked in books (HCF, factorised form, check by expanding). The page marks 6 and the reason it is 6 and not 3. Books out, then look at the page for the model.

    The hard part (on purpose)

    Stopping at a common factor that is not the highest, and finding a letter in common (2x² and 4x share a 2 and an x). Ask 'do the terms inside the bracket share anything?' and always check by expanding.

    When students get stuck

    • A student divides only the first term (6x + 9 = 3(2x + 9)). Ask: Expand your answer. Do you get back to where you started? Fix: Expanding is the check: 3 × 9 = 27, not 9.
    • A student stops at a common factor that is not the highest (Ava's mistake). Ask: Look inside the bracket. Do those terms share anything? Fix: Take out what they share as well: 2(6x − 4) = 2 × 2(3x − 2) = 4(3x − 2).
    • A student cannot find a common factor. Ask: List the factors of each number. Which appear in both lists? Fix: Use the board: try k = 2, 3, 4… and see which numbers of rows work.

    Stretch and support

    Stretch: Factorise 6x²y − 9xy² fully. (3xy(2x − 3y).) Then: write three different expressions whose full factorisation has 6 outside.

    Support: Use the tiles board for every two-term question with positive numbers: try each k and read off the bracket.

    Where it leads

    Next: rearranging formulas (node 3), which divides every term by a number in the same way.

    Where it leads: factorising quadratics in Years 9 and 10, which the same tiles show as a rectangle.

    What each step is for

    • fully factorised: Committing to a factorisation of 6x + 9 before using the board tests whether they divide every term, and whether they take out the biggest number.
    • share into rows: Trying k = 1, 2, 3 on the tiles shows which numbers of rows work (the ones that divide both totals) and what each row holds.
    • the highest common factor: Factorising divides every term by a common factor; taking out the highest common factor leaves nothing more to share, and expanding checks it. Ava's mistake takes out a common factor that is not the highest.
    • one change at a time: One change at a time: different numbers on the board, a minus sign, three terms, a letter as the common factor, then the edge case of nothing to take out.
    • factorise and check: Factorising with each sign and full working, mixed; stopping at a common factor that is not the highest is the planted slip, and the review keeps expanding fresh.
    • four rows: Choosing tiles that factorise fully with exactly 4 outside reverses the lesson: from the factor back to the expression.
    • equal rows on paper: Choosing the highest common factor decides how many equal rows there are; the paper task asks why the answer is 6 and not a smaller factor.

    • Factorise an expression by taking out a common factor.
    • Take out the highest common factor, including a letter, so the expression is fully factorised.
    • Check a factorisation by expanding it.
