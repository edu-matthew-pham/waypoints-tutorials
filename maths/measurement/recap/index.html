Area, volume, circles and Pythagoras: recap

Answer each question in your head or on paper first, then check it.

Area

1. Area of parallelograms

Try firstA parallelogram has base 9 cm, slanted side 5 cm and perpendicular height 4 cm. Find its area.

Check your answer

9 × 4 = 36 cm² (the 5 cm slanted side is not used).

A parallelogram is a rectangle pushed over. Cut the triangle off one end along the perpendicular height and move it to the other end: it becomes a rectangle with the same base and height. So:

$$A = b \times h$$

where $h$ is the perpendicular height, at a right angle to the base, not the slanted side. Area is in square units: cm², m².

2. Area of triangles

Try firstA triangle has base 9 cm and height 6 cm. Find its area. A triangle with area 24 cm² has base 8 cm: find its height.

Check your answer

½ × 9 × 6 = 27 cm². ½ × 8 × h = 24, so h = 6 cm.

Two copies of a triangle, one turned round, make a parallelogram with the same base and height. So the triangle's area is half of base × height:

$$A = \tfrac{1}{2} \times b \times h$$

The height is perpendicular to the base. If the top leans past the end of the base, extend the base: the height meets it outside the triangle. Moving the top along a line parallel to the base keeps the area.

3. Area problems

Try firstA shape is a 7 m by 4 m rectangle with a triangle on top (base 7 m, height 2 m). Find its area.

Check your answer

28 + ½ × 7 × 2 = 28 + 7 = 35 m².

Area problems have three moves:

  • Choose the base and the height at a right angle to it; a slanted side is extra information.
  • Convert every length to the same unit first (250 cm = 2.5 m). Area is in square units, and 1 m² = 10 000 cm².
  • Split a shape into rectangles and triangles and add the parts, or subtract a missing part from a bigger shape.

12. Composite shapes

Try firstA rectangle 12 m by 5 m has a 4 m by 2 m corner cut out. Find its area.

Check your answer

12 × 5 − 4 × 2 = 60 − 8 = 52 m².

A composite shape is made of shapes you know. Cut it into pieces and add their areas, or see a big rectangle and take away the missing pieces: both give the same area, if each piece uses its own lengths.

Missing sides are differences of other sides. The perimeter adds every side, in units of length.

13. Irregular shapes

Try firstA shape covers 18 full squares and part of 10 more on a 1 cm grid. Estimate its area.

Check your answer

18 + 10 ÷ 2 = 23 cm².

For an irregular shape, put a grid over it: count the full squares, add half the part squares, and multiply by one square's area. A finer grid gives a better estimate.

For the perimeter, join points on the edge with straight pieces and add them: more pieces get closer (always slightly short). State how precise the estimate is.

Volume

4. Volume in layers

Try firstA box is 5 cm by 4 cm by 3 cm. Find its volume. A box with base 6 cm² has volume 42 cm³: how high is it?

Check your answer

5 × 4 × 3 = 60 cm³. 42 ÷ 6 = 7 cm.

Volume counts unit cubes (cm³, m³). A prism fills up in layers: each layer covers the base, so it has as many cubes as the base has squares. The number of layers is the height:

$$V = A \times h$$

For a box, $A = l \times w$, so $V = l \times w \times h$. The cubes fill the inside, not just the outside you can see.

5. Prisms and capacity

Try firstA prism's triangle end has base 5 cm and height 4 cm; it is 6 cm long. Find its volume and its capacity in mL.

Check your answer

½ × 5 × 4 = 10 cm², × 6 = 60 cm³ = 60 mL.

A right prism has the same cross-section all the way along. Its volume is that slice's area times the length:

$$V = A \times h$$

For a triangular prism, the cross-section is a triangle: halve first ($\tfrac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 4 = 12$ cm², then $\times 10 = 120$ cm³). Capacity: 1 cm³ holds 1 mL, and 1000 cm³ = 1 L.

14. Volume and capacity

Try firstA fish tank is 50 cm by 30 cm by 40 cm. How many litres does it hold?

Check your answer

50 × 30 × 40 = 60 000 cm³ = 60 L.

Volume is in cubic units; capacity is in mL, L or kL. 1 cm³ holds 1 mL, 1000 cm³ holds 1 L, and 1 m³ holds 1000 L.

A cubic metre is 100 cm × 100 cm × 100 cm = 1 000 000 cm³: convert each of the three lengths.

15. Prism problems

Try firstA tank with a 2 m by 1.5 m base holds 4500 L. How deep is the water?

Check your answer

4500 L = 4.5 m³; 4.5 ÷ 3 = 1.5 m.

For any right prism, $V = A \times h$: the area of the end times the length. Find the end's area with its own formula (rectangle, triangle or composite).

To find a missing length, divide: $h = V \div A$. Convert units first, and answer in the units the problem asks for (mL, L or m³).

Circles

6. Parts of a circle

Try firstA circular pond has diameter 9 m. Find its radius. What is a chord?

Check your answer

4.5 m. A straight line joining two points on the circle.

A circle is all the points the same distance (the radius, $r$) from its centre.

  • The diameter ($d$) goes right across through the centre: $d = 2r$, and $r = d \div 2$.
  • A chord joins any two points; the diameter is the longest chord.
  • The circumference ($C$) is the distance all the way round.

7. Discovering π

Try firstWhat is π? Estimate the circumference of a circle with diameter 20 cm.

Check your answer

π is the circumference divided by the diameter, the same for every circle, about 3.14. C ≈ 3.14 × 20 = 62.8 cm.

Roll a circle out flat for one turn and its circumference is a bit more than 3 diameters. For every circle, the circumference divided by the diameter is the same number, π:

$$\pi = \frac{C}{d} = 3.14159\ldots$$

π's digits never end and never settle into a repeating pattern; 3.14 and $\tfrac{22}{7}$ are approximations. A quick estimate: the circumference is a bit more than 3 diameters.

8. Circumference

Try firstFind the circumference of a circle with radius 4 cm. A circle has circumference 12.56 cm: find its diameter. (π ≈ 3.14)

Check your answer

2 × 3.14 × 4 = 25.12 cm. 12.56 ÷ 3.14 = 4 cm.

Because $C \div d = \pi$, the circumference is

$$C = \pi d = 2\pi r$$

Use the diameter (or double the radius). Going backwards, divide by π: $d = C \div \pi$. An exact answer keeps π: a circle with diameter 10 cm has $C = 10\pi$ cm, about 31.4 cm.

16. Area of a circle

Try firstFind the area of a circle with diameter 8 cm.

Check your answer

r = 4 cm; A = π × 16 ≈ 50.3 cm².

Cut a circle into thin slices and lay them top to tail: the shape is nearly a rectangle πr long (half the circumference) and r high. So $A = \pi r^2$.

Check: the area is between $2r^2$ and $4r^2$. Do not mix it up with the circumference $C = 2\pi r$, a length.

17. Circle problems

Try firstA circular pond has an area of 28.3 m². What is its radius?

Check your answer

28.3 ÷ π ≈ 9, and √9 = 3 m.

Choose the formula: circumference $C = 2\pi r$ (a length) or area $A = \pi r^2$ (a space). From an area, $r = \sqrt{A \div \pi}$.

Parts of circles: a semicircle is half the area; its perimeter is half the circumference plus the diameter. Say which value of π you used.

Pythagoras

9. Pythagoras' theorem

Try firstA right-angled triangle has sides 10 cm and 24 cm at the right angle. Find the hypotenuse.

Check your answer

100 + 576 = 676, √676 = 26 cm.

In a right-angled triangle, the hypotenuse $c$ is the side opposite the right angle (the longest). Pythagoras' theorem: the squares on the two shorter sides add to the square on the hypotenuse,

$$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$$

To find $c$: square $a$ and $b$, add, then take the square root. With 6 and 8: $36 + 64 = 100$, $c = 10$.

10. Finding a side

Try firstA right-angled triangle has hypotenuse 17 cm and one side 8 cm. Find the other side.

Check your answer

289 − 64 = 225, √225 = 15 cm.

Pythagoras finds any side of a right-angled triangle:

  • the hypotenuse: add the squares, $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$;
  • a shorter side: subtract, $b^2 = c^2 - a^2$.

Then take the square root (to two decimal places if it is not whole). Check: the hypotenuse must be the longest side.

11. Right, acute or obtuse?

Try firstClassify triangles with sides (a) 8, 15, 17 and (b) 4, 6, 8.

Check your answer

(a) 64 + 225 = 289 = 17²: right-angled. (b) 16 + 36 = 52 < 64: obtuse.

To test a triangle, call its longest side $c$ and compare $a^2 + b^2$ with $c^2$:

  • equal: right-angled;
  • $a^2 + b^2 < c^2$: obtuse (the angle opposite $c$ is over 90°);
  • $a^2 + b^2 > c^2$: acute.

Whole numbers that fit exactly are Pythagorean triples: 3-4-5, 5-12-13, 8-15-17, and their multiples.

Time zones

18. Time zones

Try firstIt is 18:30 Tuesday in Sydney (UTC+11). What time and day is it in Los Angeles (UTC−7)?

Check your answer

Los Angeles is 18 hours behind: 00:30 Tuesday.

A time zone is an offset from UTC. A city with a bigger offset is ahead; a smaller one is behind. Find the difference, add or take it away, and change the day when you cross midnight.

For a flight, add the flight time to the departure (in the departure zone), then change to the arrival zone. Use 24-hour time to avoid am and pm mistakes.
