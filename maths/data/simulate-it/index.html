Simulate it

Build a simulation of the screening test where the second step depends on the first, run it many times, and compare what comes out with the table: close, but not the same, and closer as the trials grow.

Could a computer pretend to test a thousand people in a blink and tell us what the table says?

What you should already know:
  • Tables, Venns and trees · A two-way table, a Venn diagram and a tree can all show the same conditional chance.
  • Run it a thousand times · A computer can run a simulation of two kicks a thousand times.

Back to the screening test from the last lesson. Out of 1000 people, 20 have the condition. The test is positive for 9 in 10 of them and, wrongly, for 1 in 20 of the others. All of these rates are made up. The table said the chance of the condition, given a positive test, is 18 out of 67. A simulation lets you check that without the table: pick imaginary people at random with the right chances, test them, and count.

Explain it to me

A simulation can model a conditional chance if the second step depends on the first.

  • Pick people at random using the first chance, then use the chance that goes with each result for the second step.
  • Run it many times and count each outcome.
  • The chance given a positive is the positives with the condition over all the positives.
  • The simulated chance differs a little from the calculated one, and the gap shrinks as the trials grow.

Also used in

  • Sport. A simulated penalty shootout works the same way: the chance of the next kick depends on how the last one went, and many runs show how often the team wins.
  • Weather and hospitals. Forecasters and planners simulate many runs of a model to see how much results can wander before deciding.
  • Class work. Decide when a simulation supports a real decision, and what it leaves out.

For teachers

Students design a simulation of the screening test where the second step depends on the first, run it many times on the board, and compare the simulated chance with the table. They see that a simulation wanders around the calculated chance and settles as trials grow (AC9M10P02, nodes 1 and 2). About 50 minutes.

Open the teacher guide: answers, solutions, slides and a worksheet.

Students will be able to

  • I can design a simulation where the second step depends on the first.
  • I can run it many times and work out a conditional chance from the counts.
  • I can compare the simulated chance with the calculated one and say why more trials get closer.

Before class

Do the Run board yourself first. Students need the last lessons (tables, Venns and trees) and Year 9's simulation lessons.

The one idea: a simulation picks random people with the right chances, then counts. A conditional chance is read from the counts inside the group, so the Positive rows give the chance given a positive. The board here picks one outcome in proportion to its count (the tree in the last lesson), so one trial is one imaginary person. The two-step design with digits is the same idea in a form a student could do with dice, a spreadsheet or code.

The board has no field for the chance given a positive: students read the count table and divide. That is deliberate. Results are random, so no check depends on a particular count: the board checks wait for a number of trials, and the number questions use counts given in the question.

Every rate and count is made up: the screening test, the simulated counts and the counters. A simulation of a made-up test says nothing about any real test.

Running the lesson

  1. Predict: show of hands before the board. Most choose exactly 67.
  2. Run: ask each group to report its own simulated chance after 1000 trials. They will differ: put them on the board.
  3. Paper: the counters design and the Make comparison are worked on paper first. Look for a different second step.
  4. Modify: before the last board, ask 'what is the chance there are no positives in 10 trials?' (about half).

The hard part (on purpose)

Forgetting that the second step changes with the first. Ask: 'Is the chance of a positive the same for everyone? Who has a different chance?'

When students get stuck

  • A student gives every person the same chance of a positive. Ask: Is a positive equally likely for someone who has the condition and someone who doesn't? Fix: Make the second step depend on the first: 9 in 10 for people with it, 1 in 20 for the rest.
  • A student divides by the number of trials. Ask: Given a positive test, which trials are you looking at? Fix: Only the trials with a positive result: add the two Positive rows.
  • A student says the table is wrong because the simulation gave a different number. Ask: If you ran it again, would you get exactly the same number? How many trials was it? Fix: Run more trials and see whether the result settles near the table's number.

Stretch and support

Stretch: Change the board's rates so 100 people in 1000 have the condition, the test is positive for 9 in 10 of them and for 1 in 20 of the rest. Predict the chance given a positive, then simulate it. What did a more common condition do?

Support: A printed table of the four outcomes with a space for tallies, and the calculation written as a fraction to fill in: ___ ÷ (___ + ___).

What each step is for

  • how close will it be: A simulation uses random picks, so each run is a little different. Students who expect the exact counts from the table are treating a sample as if it were the whole.
  • a thousand imaginary people: Each trial picks one imaginary person from the tree in proportion to the counts. 1000 trials give a count for each outcome. The positives with the condition, over all the positives, estimate the chance given a positive.
  • the design, and the comparison: A simulation of a conditional chance must make the second step depend on the first: the chance of a positive is different for people with and without the condition. A simulated result differs from the calculated one by chance, and the gap shrinks as the trials grow.
  • more trials, a different test: More trials bring the simulated chance closer to the table. A different test changes the calculated chance, and the simulation follows it. With very few trials, a simulation may not give any positives at all.
  • compare the numbers: Reading a simulated chance from its counts, describing the gap from the table, and saying what more trials do. Review: counting a compound outcome from Year 9.
  • back to the test: Returning to the opening: the simulation is checked against the table. A good comparison gives both chances, says how far apart they are and why that is expected.
