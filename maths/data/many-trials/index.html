Many trials

Over a few trials the relative frequency jumps about; over many it settles close to the probability. That is why more trials give a better estimate, and why a run of tails does not make heads 'due'.

Flip a coin ten times and you might get 7 heads. Is the coin unfair, or are ten flips just not enough to tell?

What you should already know:
  • Predict and test · The expected number of times an outcome happens is its probability × the number of trials: 60 rolls should give about 10 sixes.

A short experiment rarely matches the expected count, as you saw with the dice. So what happens as the number of trials grows?

How this lesson works: Predict, Try, Investigate, Modify, Practise, Make

Explain it to me

Over a few trials, the relative frequency (count ÷ trials) jumps about. Over many trials it settles close to the probability: the law of large numbers. One more head moves 7/10 a lot, but 501/1000 hardly at all.

It is not because results balance out: a coin has no memory, and after 5 tails heads is still exactly 1/2. Large experiments are how we estimate probabilities we cannot work out, such as a drawing pin landing point-up.

Reviews on paper

Do this one in your book. The review counts are made up, but a shop page really can show five reviews or five hundred. Work out both relative frequencies, type the fall, then choose the reason you trust the bigger run.

Also used in

  • Product reviews. Five 5-star reviews are weaker evidence than five hundred averaging 4.6, because small samples swing a lot.
  • Next year. Year 8 samples show the same thing: how much a sample varies depends on how big it is.
  • Coin flips. A run of heads is only a short run, and the coin is not 'due' a tail.

Sources: every count in this lesson is made up for the task (the flips, the drawing pin and the spinners). Nothing here comes from a real experiment.

For teachers

Students run hundreds and thousands of trials and watch the relative frequency settle towards the probability (the law of large numbers), use large experiments to estimate unknown probabilities, and reject the idea that outcomes are 'due' (AC9M7P02, node 2). About 40 minutes.

Open the teacher guide: answers, solutions, slides and a worksheet.

Students will be able to

  • I can describe how the relative frequency changes as the number of trials grows.
  • I can use a large experiment to estimate a probability.
  • I can explain why a run of one outcome does not make another one 'due'.

Before class

Do the Run and Make boards first. Students need Predict and test.

The one idea: over many trials the relative frequency settles close to the probability, because each new trial changes the fraction less, not because results balance out. The common mistake is the gambler's fallacy (a coin is 'due' a head); it is planted in Zara's reasoning and again in Practise.

The mystery bag hides its contents: students estimate the make-up from 1000 draws (with replacement). It has 3 red and 7 yellow counters.

Running the lesson

  1. Predict: take a show of hands; many students choose 'equally likely'.
  2. Run: ask students to press Start again and compare the first ten flips of different runs.
  3. Investigate: show the 7/10 → 8/11 and 501/1000 → 502/1001 calculation on the board.
  4. Modify: collect the class's estimates for the mystery bag; reveal 3 red, 7 yellow.
  5. Make: collect how many flips each student needed.
  6. Paper: the review task is worked in books; the page marks the fall (0.4) and the reason for trusting 500. Books out, and ask which number sat nearest the long-run share.

The hard part (on purpose)

Thinking a short run shows the true chance, or that a coin is due a tail. Ask: how would the coin know what came before?

When students get stuck

  • A student thinks heads is 'due' (Zara's mistake). Ask: How would the coin know what happened before? Fix: Each flip is still 1/2; long runs happen, and get swamped by many more trials.
  • A student thinks large experiments give the probability exactly. Ask: After 1000 flips, is it exactly 0.5? Fix: Close, not exact: closer with more trials.

Stretch and support

Stretch: How many flips are needed, roughly, to be within 0.01 of 0.5 most of the time? Experiment with the board (about 10 000).

Support: Use the board's graph: point to where the line is after 10, 100 and 1000 trials.

Where it leads

This completes the Year 7 chance descriptors (AC9M7P01, AC9M7P02).

Where it leads: complementary events, two-step experiments and simulations of compound events (Year 8).

What each step is for

  • 10 or 1000?: Committing to which experiment lands closer to half shows whether they expect larger samples to be more reliable.
  • watch it settle: Running 10 flips, then a thousand, on one graph shows the early jumps and the settling: relative frequency approaches the probability as trials grow.
  • settling, not balancing: Relative frequency settles because each new trial counts for less as the total grows, not because earlier results are balanced out. Zara's mistake thinks the coin remembers.
  • one change at a time: One change at a time: a dice instead of a coin, a mystery bag estimated from data, the size of the error, then the edge case of a long run.
  • many trials: Relative frequency, estimating from experiments and judging sample size, mixed; the gambler's slip planted again; the review keeps expected counts fresh.
  • how many is enough?: Running trials until the estimate is within 0.01 of the probability makes the law of large numbers concrete: closeness costs many trials.
  • reviews on paper: A product with few reviews shows a short run swinging far from where many trials settle; the paper task makes them compute two relative frequencies and say which to trust.
