Scatterplots

A scatterplot turns each person into one dot, using two measurements: the first goes across and the second goes up. Plot a dot, read a dot, and see whether the dots sit near a line.

Is your arm span the same as your height? Measure a whole class and you get a lot of numbers. A picture can show them all at once.

What you should already know:
  • Plotting a rule · Plot each ordered pair from a rule's table as a point, across first and then up.
  • Shape: symmetric or skewed · A distribution's shape says where the values pile up and where the long tail runs.

A class measured their height and their arm span, in centimetres. Each student gives two numbers, so each student can be one dot: the arm span goes across, the height goes up. The measurements in this lesson are made up. Have a guess where Tom's dot goes, then we'll plot the whole class.

Explain it to me

A scatterplot shows two measurements for each person at once. Each person is one dot.

  • Across: the first measurement (arm span here).
  • Up: the second measurement (height here).
  • A reference line such as height = arm span shows where the two numbers are equal: dots above it have the bigger second number, dots below it have the smaller.

For teachers

Students plot a dot from two measurements (first across, second up), read a dot's coordinates, and compare a dot with the line height = arm span. The context is a class's arm span and height with made-up numbers (AC9M10ST03, node 1). About 45 minutes.

Open the teacher guide: answers, solutions, slides and a worksheet.

Students will be able to

  • I can plot a dot from two measurements, putting the first across and the second up.
  • I can read the two numbers of a dot from a scatterplot.
  • I can say whether a dot is above, on or below the line where the two measurements are equal.

Before class

Do the Run board yourself first. P snaps to whole numbers; the arrow buttons under the graph move it a step at a time.

The one idea: one student is one dot, with the first measurement across and the second up. A scatterplot shows every pair at once, and a reference line (here height = arm span) gives something to compare each dot against.

Every number is made up: Tom, the twelve students and the five on paper. Nothing here is a real class. If your class measures itself first (tape measures; the library's suggestion) use the real data in place of the twelve dots: the Make step then answers your own question. Measure to the nearest centimetre and agree how to measure an arm span (fingertip to fingertip, arms straight out).

Hidden: measuring errors (a slumped arm span, shoes on). They show up as dots far from the line, which is where the next lesson goes.

Running the lesson

  1. Predict: take a show of hands before the Run. Students who chose 'below' usually swapped the two numbers; ask them which axis arm span is on.
  2. Run: ask students to say 'across, then up' aloud as they drag P.
  3. Paper: the five-student try-one is worked on paper first, with the worked example beside them. Look for a dot placed on the line being counted as above.
  4. Modify: before the metres question ask 'about how many cm is a student tall?' and then 'where is 1.62 on that scale?'.

The hard part (on purpose)

Putting the two numbers the wrong way round, or reading a dot's height off the wrong axis. Ask 'which measurement did we name first, and which axis is that?'

When students get stuck

  • A student puts the bigger number across (Mia's mistake). Ask: Which measurement did the question name first? Which axis is it on? Fix: Label the axes on the page, then say 'across to arm span, up to height' as each dot is placed.
  • A student counts dots by whether they look close to the line, not by comparing the two numbers. Ask: What is the height and what is the arm span? Which is bigger? Fix: Write the two numbers beside the dot and compare them. A dot is above the line when the second number is bigger.
  • A student says two students with the same arm span make one dot. Ask: Do they have the same height too? Fix: One dot per student. The dots share their first number, so they sit one above the other.

Stretch and support

Stretch: Draw a scatterplot on paper for any two measurements you can take in the classroom (hand span and foot length, say), then decide where a line like height = arm span could go, or whether none makes sense.

Support: Give a printed grid with the axes labelled and a row of pairs to plot one at a time, ticking each pair off. Mark 'across' and 'up' on the grid.

Where it leads

Next: Describing association: how the dots slope, how tightly they cluster, and whether a straight line fits.

Where it leads in class: students measure their own class (hand span against foot length, or arm span against height) and make the scatterplot in a spreadsheet or on paper. Any real class data replaces the made-up numbers.

Sources

What each step is for

  • where is Tom's dot?: Placing a dot for a student who is taller than their arm span shows whether students put the first number across and the second up, and whether they see the dashed line as 'the same'.
  • one dot at a time: Each student is one dot: arm span across, height up. A dot above the dashed line means a student whose height is more than their arm span.
  • reading and plotting: A scatterplot is read one dot at a time: across for the first variable, up for the second. Swapping the two puts every dot in the wrong place, and the dashed line helps check.
  • when dots share or break the pattern: Two students can share a number, and a slip in the units shows at once on a plot. Dragging P to these cases shows what a scatterplot does with them.
  • reading and checking: Reading a height from an arm span on the plot, and comparing a pair with the line, over and over until across-then-up is automatic.
  • is arm span the same as height?: Back to the opening question: counting how close the twelve dots are to the dashed line gives a first answer, and a careful sentence says what the plot shows and what it doesn't.
