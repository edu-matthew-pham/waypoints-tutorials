Data and chance: recap

Answer each question in your head or on paper first, then check it.

Data

1. Counting and measuring

Try firstClassify: (a) number of apps on a phone, (b) battery temperature, (c) screen size in inches. Explain one.

Check your answer

(a) discrete (counted), (b) continuous (measured), (c) continuous (measured, though sold in common sizes).

Discrete data is counted: it takes separate values, such as 0, 1, 2 siblings. Continuous data is measured: it can be any value in a range, such as a 100 m time of 14.37 s.

Measured values are always rounded: 152 cm to the nearest centimetre means from 151.5 cm up to 152.5 cm. Decimals alone do not make data continuous: shoe sizes (7, 7.5, 8) are discrete.

2. Mean, median, mode and range

Try firstFind the mean, median, mode and range of 6, 2, 9, 6, 7, 12.

Check your answer

Mean 42 ÷ 6 = 7; median (6 + 7) ÷ 2 = 6.5; mode 6; range 10.

Four numbers sum up a data set:

  • mean: add the values, then divide by how many there are (3, 5, 5, 6, 11: 30 ÷ 5 = 6);
  • median: sort the values and take the middle one; for an even count, halfway between the two middle values;
  • mode: the most common value (there can be two, or none);
  • range: highest minus lowest (11 − 3 = 8), a measure of spread.

3. Which measure?

Try firstSalaries in a small company ($ thousands): 50, 52, 55, 58, 400. Which measure would you report as typical, and why?

Check your answer

The median, 55: the 400 is an outlier that pulls the mean up to 123, more than four of the five salaries.

The mean, median and mode can disagree. Look for outliers first (values far from the rest):

  • An outlier pulls the mean towards it, so with one the median usually describes a typical value better ($5, 6, 6, 7, 8, 40$: mean 12, median 6.5).
  • With no outliers, the mean is a fair summary because it uses every value.
  • For "which is most common?", use the mode.

4. Stem-and-leaf plots

Try firstDraw a stem-and-leaf plot of 17, 25, 22, 41, 18, 29, 26 and find its median.

Check your answer

1 | 7 8, 2 | 2 5 6 9, 3 | (empty), 4 | 1. Median: the 4th of 7 values, 25.

A stem-and-leaf plot keeps every value and shows the shape. For two-digit numbers the stem is the tens digit and the leaf is the units digit; leaves go in order along their stem's row. Include every stem from the smallest to the largest, even with no leaves, and give a key: $3 \mid 5$ means $35$.

Read the median by counting leaves to the middle; the range is the last value minus the first.

Chance

6. Sample spaces and probability

Try firstA bag has 2 red, 3 green and 5 yellow counters. List the sample space and give each probability.

Check your answer

{red, green, yellow}: P(red) = 2/10 = 1/5, P(green) = 3/10, P(yellow) = 5/10 = 1/2. They add to 1.

The sample space is the list of every possible outcome. If the outcomes are equally likely,

$P(\text{event}) = \dfrac{\text{favourable outcomes}}{\text{total outcomes}}$, so $P(\text{even on a dice}) = \tfrac{3}{6} = \tfrac{1}{2}$.

If they are not (a half-red spinner, a bag with more of one colour), use the sizes or the counts: $P(\text{red}) = \tfrac{1}{2}$. The probabilities of all the outcomes add to 1; 0 means impossible, 1 means certain.

7. Predict and test

Try firstA spinner has P(blue) = 0.4. How many blues do you expect in 50 spins? You get 23. Is the spinner unfair?

Check your answer

20 expected. 23 is close to 20, so there is no reason to think it is unfair.

A probability predicts what happens over many trials: expected count = probability × number of trials. In 60 rolls of a fair dice, expect about $60 \times \tfrac{1}{6} = 10$ sixes.

Then test it: run the experiment and record the counts. The relative frequency is count ÷ trials. In a short experiment the results vary: 7 or 12 sixes in 60 rolls is normal, so do not judge a dice on a few rolls.

8. Many trials

Try firstAfter 40 spins a spinner gave 18 reds; after 4000 spins, 1230 reds. Which estimate of P(red) is better, and what is it?

Check your answer

The 4000-spin one: 1230 ÷ 4000 ≈ 0.31. More trials give a more reliable estimate.

Over a few trials, the relative frequency (count ÷ trials) jumps about. Over many trials it settles close to the probability: the law of large numbers. One more head moves 7/10 a lot, but 501/1000 hardly at all.

It is not because results balance out: a coin has no memory, and after 5 tails heads is still exactly 1/2. Large experiments are how we estimate probabilities we cannot work out, such as a drawing pin landing point-up.

10. Complementary events

Try firstP(it snows tomorrow) = 0.08. What is P(it does not snow)? Then: two coins are tossed; what is P(at least one tail)?

Check your answer

0.92. And 1 − P(HH) = 1 − 1/4 = 3/4.

The complement of an event A, written not A, is every other outcome. A and not A cover every outcome exactly once, so P(A) + P(not A) = 1, and P(not A) = 1 − P(A).

This makes at least one easy: count the outcomes with none, and subtract from 1. Two events that just can't happen together are not complementary unless they cover every outcome too.

12. Grids and tree diagrams

Try firstA spinner shows 1, 2 or 3 (equal parts) and a coin is tossed. List the outcomes. What is P(an odd number and heads)?

Check your answer

1H, 1T, 2H, 2T, 3H, 3T: 6 outcomes. 1H and 3H, so 2/6 = 1/3.

Two events one after the other make pairs. A tree diagram splits each first outcome into every second outcome; a grid puts one event down the side and the other across. Both list every pair once, so each is equally likely.

The number of outcomes is the two counts multiplied. Order matters: HT and TH are different outcomes. Then P(event) = outcomes in the event ÷ all outcomes.

13. And, or and not

Try first40 students: 25 like maths, 18 like art, 10 like both. Find P(maths or art) and P(neither).

Check your answer

25 + 18 − 10 = 33, so 33/40. Neither: 7/40.

A and B: both happen (the overlap). A or B: at least one happens, both included (everything inside the circles). Not A: everything outside A. Neither: outside both.

Count each outcome once. From totals: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B), because the overlap is in both totals.

15. Census, sample, experiment or observation

Try firstA cereal company wants to check the weight of its boxes. Census or sample? Why?

Check your answer

A sample: weighing every box costs time; checking a random sample of boxes each hour is enough to spot problems.

The population is everyone the question is about. A census measures all of them (exact, but often slow and costly); a sample measures some (quicker and cheaper, an estimate).

An experiment changes something on purpose and measures the effect; an observation watches without changing anything. Choose the technique that fits the question, the cost and the time.

16. Bias in collecting data

Try firstA gym surveys its members on how often people exercise. Name the bias and which way it pushes the result.

Check your answer

Sampling bias: gym members exercise more than most people, so the result is too high.

Bias is anything in how data is collected that pushes the result one way. Sampling bias: the sample isn't like the population (only people at a gym). Response bias: the question or setting pushes answers (leading questions, no privacy). Measurement bias: the tool or method is off.

A bigger sample doesn't fix bias. Random choice, neutral questions, anonymous answers and checked tools do.

17. Ways to choose a sample

Try firstA school has 400 Year 9 and 200 Year 10 students. Describe how to take a stratified sample of 30.

Check your answer

In proportion: 20 Year 9s and 10 Year 10s, each chosen at random from its year's list.

Simple random: every member has the same chance. Systematic: every kth member of a list from a random start (k = population ÷ sample size). Stratified: split into groups and choose at random within each, in proportion, so each group gets its fair share. Convenience: whoever is easiest, often biased. Quota: fixed shares, but the collector chooses, so it can be biased too.

19. How samples vary

Try firstTwo random samples of 10 students give 0.4 and 0.7 for the proportion who walk to school. Is one wrong? What would you do?

Check your answer

Not necessarily: small samples vary a lot. Take bigger samples (or more of them) for a more reliable estimate.

Sampling variation: random samples from the same population give different results, because each holds different members. It isn't a mistake.

Bigger samples vary less, so their results are more reliable. Fair samples scatter around the population's value; bias pushes them all to one side.

Year 10: conditional chance

42. Given

Try firstOf 60 students, 24 play football and 9 play football and tennis. What is the chance a football player plays tennis? What is the chance a tennis player plays football if 18 play tennis?

Check your answer

9/24 = 0.375, then 9/18 = 0.5. The group changes, so the answer changes.

A chance "given" something is found inside the group where that thing is true.

  • Spot the given: "given", "knowing that", "if … then" and "of the" point at the group.
  • Shrink the whole: only the days or people in that group are counted.
  • Divide: count what you want inside the group, then divide by the size of the group.
  • Swapping the events changes the group, so P(A | B) is usually not P(B | A).

Try firstA test is positive for 8 of the 10 people who have a condition and for 5 of the 90 who don't. Given a positive test, what is the chance a person has it?

Check your answer

8 out of 13 positives, 8/13 = 0.62.

A conditional chance is read from a table, Venn diagram or tree by looking only at the group that is given.

  • Start from a group of 1000 and turn each rate into a count.
  • "Given a positive test" means the group is everyone who tested positive, with and without the condition.
  • The chance is the condition's positives over all the positives.
  • A rare condition means most of the positives can be false alarms, even from a good test.

44. Independent or not?

Try firstOf 100 students, 50 play basketball. Of the 20 students who are left-handed, 10 play basketball. Are playing basketball and being left-handed independent in this survey?

Check your answer

Yes: 10/20 = 0.5 and 50/100 = 0.5, the same.

Two events are independent when the chance of one stays the same whether or not the other happened.

  • Compare P(A | B) with P(A), both as rates.
  • Equal chances: independent. Different chances: not independent.
  • Counts mislead because the groups are different sizes.
  • Events that cannot both happen are not independent: if one happens, the other's chance drops to 0.

45. Simulate it

Try firstA simulation gave 12 people with the condition and a positive test, and 36 without it and a positive test. What is the simulated chance of the condition, given a positive test?

Check your answer

A simulation can model a conditional chance if the second step depends on the first.

  • Pick people at random using the first chance, then use the chance that goes with each result for the second step.
  • Run it many times and count each outcome.
  • The chance given a positive is the positives with the condition over all the positives.
  • The simulated chance differs a little from the calculated one, and the gap shrinks as the trials grow.

57. Linked, but not the cause

Try firstSales of sunscreen and cases of sunburn rise together. Does that show sunscreen causes sunburn? Name a third thing.

Check your answer

50. Misleading graphs

Try firstA bar chart's axis starts at 50. The bars show 80 and 60. How many times as tall does the first bar look as the second?

Check your answer

(80 − 50) ÷ (60 − 50) = 3 times, though 80 is only about 1.3 times 60.

A graph can mislead without a single wrong number. A vertical axis that doesn't start at zero, a time window picked to suit the story, or a picture scaled in two directions all change what the eye sees.

53. Boxplots

Try firstA boxplot's five numbers are 5, 8, 12, 20 and 41. What is the upper fence, and is 41 an outlier?

Check your answer

IQR = 12; fence = 20 + 18 = 38; 41 is beyond it, so it's an outlier.

A boxplot draws the five numbers as a picture. The box runs from the lower quartile to the upper quartile, the bar inside is the median, and the whiskers reach the lowest and highest values that aren't outliers.

A value is an outlier if it is more than 1.5 times the interquartile range beyond the box. It is marked with a cross.
