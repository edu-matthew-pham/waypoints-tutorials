## The idea in plain words

The **population** is everyone a question is about. A **census** measures all of them: exact, but slow, costly, and sometimes impossible (testing every battery leaves none to sell). A **sample** measures some, and gives an **estimate**. An **experiment** changes something on purpose and measures the effect; an **observation** watches without changing anything.

**Bias** pushes a result one way: **sampling bias** (who is asked: people at a gym, phone-in callers), **response bias** (how they are asked: leading questions, no privacy) and **measurement bias** (a faulty tool). A bigger sample doesn't fix bias.

Fair ways to choose: **simple random** (every member the same chance), **systematic** (every kth from a random start, k = population ÷ sample size) and **stratified** (at random within each group, in proportion). **Convenience** and **quota** samples let a person choose, so they can be biased.

Fair samples still differ by chance: **sampling variation**. Their results scatter around the population's value, and **bigger samples vary less**. So an **inference** about the population says "about", names the sample's size and method, stays within the population sampled, and is collected ethically (consent, anonymity, neutral questions).

## Why it matters

Polls, medical trials, product testing, the ABS: almost every number in the news comes from a sample. Knowing how it was chosen and how big it was is how to judge it.

## What students get wrong

- **A census is always best**, even when it destroys or costs too much.
- **A big sample can't be biased** (the phone-in poll).
- **Random means picking without a plan.**
- **A sample's mean is the population's exact value.**
- **Two different samples mean one is wrong.**
- **Inferring beyond the population sampled** (one school to all teenagers).
