## The idea in plain words

Numerical data is **discrete** when it is counted (siblings, goals) and **continuous** when it is measured (height, time), and measured values are always rounded. A data set is summed up by its **centre** (mean: total ÷ count; median: the middle value once sorted; mode: the most common) and its **spread** (range: highest − lowest).

Which centre is most useful depends on the data: an **outlier** drags the mean, so the median usually describes a typical value better; with no outliers the mean uses every value; for "most common" use the mode. A **stem-and-leaf plot** keeps every value and shows the **shape** (symmetrical or skewed, clusters, gaps, outliers). To **compare** two data sets, compare centre and spread, with numbers and in context.

## Why it matters

Statistics in the news, in sport and in science all rest on choosing a fair summary and comparing fairly. Year 8 builds sampling and variation on these ideas.

## What students get wrong

- **Treating any decimal as continuous** (shoe sizes, prices).
- **Taking the median of the unsorted list.**
- **Trusting the mean whatever the data.** One outlier can put the mean above most values.
- **Leaving empty stems out of a stem-and-leaf plot**, hiding gaps.
- **Comparing single values** (the top score) instead of centre and spread.

## A statistical investigation (AC9M7ST03)

The lesson *Statistical investigations* gives the shape of one (question, plan, sample and bias, display, summary, report), with sleep as the example. The investigation itself is best done in class too, using the tools from these lessons: pose a question about the class (for example, "Do we sleep less on school nights?"), plan what to measure and how, collect the data, display it (dot plot or stem-and-leaf), find the centre and spread, compare groups, and report a conclusion with its limits (who was asked, how many, how it was measured).
