## The idea in plain words

The **sample space** lists every possible outcome. When outcomes are equally likely, $P(\text{event}) = \dfrac{\text{favourable outcomes}}{\text{total outcomes}}$; when they are not (a spinner with a bigger sector, a bag with more of one colour), use the sizes or counts. Probabilities run from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain) and all the outcomes' probabilities add to 1.

A probability predicts: **expected count = probability × trials**. An experiment gives a **relative frequency** (count ÷ trials), which varies a lot over a few trials and settles close to the probability over many (the **law of large numbers**).

## Why it matters

Large experiments and simulations are how probabilities are estimated when no sample space of equal outcomes exists: a drawing pin landing point-up, a medicine working, a machine failing.

## What students get wrong

- **Assuming listed outcomes are equally likely** (three colours, so 1/3 each).
- **Expecting the predicted count exactly**, and calling a fair dice unfair after a short experiment.
- **The gambler's fallacy**: thinking a coin is "due" a head after a run of tails. Each trial is independent.
