## The idea in plain words

An **expression** is a calculation with a letter in it, such as $2x + 3$. It has a value once you know $x$, but it does not say anything is true.

An **equation** puts an = sign between two expressions, such as $2x + 3 = 11$. It is a statement: "these two things are equal". The **solution** is the value of the letter that makes the statement true. Here it is $x = 4$, because $2 \times 4 + 3 = 11$.

**Solving** means finding that value. The unit teaches three ways, in order:

1. **Try and check** (substitution): put a value in place of the letter, work out both sides, and see if they match.
2. **Backtracking**: think of the equation as a machine ($x$, then $\times 2$, then $+ 3$, gives 11) and undo each step, last step first.
3. **The balance method**: do the same thing to both sides until $x$ is on its own. This one also works when $x$ is on both sides.

Whatever the method, the answer is **checked** by putting it back into the first equation.

## Why it matters

- Equations turn word problems into something you can solve: "4 tickets and a $5 booking fee cost $41" is $4x + 5 = 41$.
- Formulas in science and money are equations with one unknown: speed, area, cost per item.
- Checking by substitution is how you know an answer is right without asking anyone.

## An everyday picture, and where it breaks

A balance scale. Two bags with the same unknown number of blocks, plus 3 loose blocks, balance 11 blocks. Take 3 blocks from **each** side and it still balances: two bags balance 8 blocks. Share both sides into 2 equal groups: one bag balances 4 blocks.

Where it breaks: a scale cannot show taking away more than is there, so equations with subtraction ($7x - 3 = 32$) or negative answers need the written method. That is why the balance lesson moves from the board to lines of algebra, and why subtraction comes in a later lesson.

## Try it yourself (5 minutes)

1. Solve $3x + 5 = 20$ by trying values: $x = 4$ gives 17, $x = 5$ gives 20. So $x = 5$.
2. Solve it by backtracking: $20 - 5 = 15$, then $15 \div 3 = 5$.
3. Solve it by balancing: take 5 from both sides ($3x = 15$), divide both sides by 3 ($x = 5$).
4. Check in the first equation: $3 \times 5 + 5 = 20$ ✓.

Three methods, one answer. Students meet them in this order because each one explains the next.

## Questions students ask

- **"Isn't 2x just 2 and x next to each other?"** No: $2x$ means $2 \times x$. With $x = 4$ it is 8, not 24. This is the most common slip in the unit.
- **"Which step do I undo first?"** The last thing that was done to $x$. In $2x + 3$, the 3 was added last, so take it away first.
- **"Why do I have to do it to both sides?"** Because the equation says both sides are equal. Change only one side and they are not equal any more.
- **"Do I have to check?"** Yes. A check in the first equation catches almost every mistake, and it takes ten seconds.

## How it connects

The Equations unit builds on writing expressions (the Expressions unit) and leads to graphing linear relations in Year 8, where the solution of an equation is where a line crosses a value. It also links to coding: a function machine is a function in Python, and backtracking is running it in reverse.

**Mistakes this unit plants on purpose** (so students learn to spot them): reading $2x$ as 24, undoing steps in the wrong order, and changing one side of an equation only.
