## The idea in plain words

The number outside a bracket multiplies every term inside: $3(2x + 4) = 6x + 12$. With algebra tiles, $3(2x + 4)$ is 3 rows, each of two $x$-tiles and four ones, so 6 $x$-tiles and 12 ones. Signs come with the terms: $4(3x - 2) = 12x - 8$, and a negative outside flips every sign: $-2(x - 5) = -2x + 10$.

After expanding, collect like terms: $6x + 12 - x = 5x + 12$.

**Factorising** goes backwards: share the terms into equal rows. $6x + 9 = 3(2x + 3)$. Take out the highest common factor, letters included ($2x^2 + 4x = 2x(x + 2)$), and check by expanding.

**Changing the subject** undoes what was done to a letter, last step first, on both sides: $y = 3x + 5$ gives $x = \dfrac{y - 5}{3}$. Check by substituting numbers.

## Why it matters

Expanding is how expressions are simplified, how equations with brackets are solved, and, backwards, how expressions are factorised. It is the distributive law students already use in mental arithmetic: $3 \times 24 = 3 \times 20 + 3 \times 4$.

## What students get wrong

- **Multiplying only the first term**: $4(x + 3) = 4x + 3$. Count the tiles: every row has the ones too.
- **Signs**: $4(3x - 2) = 12x + 8$, or $-2(x - 5) = -2x - 10$. Each row takes away; a negative number of rows flips every tile.
- **A common factor that is not the highest**: $12x - 8 = 2(6x - 4)$ is equal but not fully factorised; the bracket still shares a 2.
- **Dividing only one term** when rearranging: $y = 4x - 3$ becomes $\frac{y}{4} = x - 3$. Undo the $- 3$ first.
- **Stopping before collecting**: $6x + 12 - x$ is expanded but not simplified. The answer checks tell students which.

## The words

**expand**: write without brackets. **term**: a part of an expression joined by + or −. **like terms**: terms with the same letters and powers ($6x$ and $-x$). **distributive law**: $a(b + c) = ab + ac$. **factorise**: write as a product, usually with a bracket. **highest common factor (HCF)**: the biggest number (and letters) that divides every term. **subject**: the letter on its own on one side of a formula.
