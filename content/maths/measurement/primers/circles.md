## The idea in plain words

A circle is all the points the same distance from its **centre**: the **radius** $r$. The **diameter** goes right across through the centre, so $d = 2r$. A **chord** joins any two points (the diameter is the longest); the **circumference** $C$ is the distance all the way round.

Roll any circle out flat for one turn and the track is just over 3 diameters. For every circle, $C \div d$ is the same number, **π** (pi) $= 3.14159\ldots$, whose digits never end or settle into a repeating pattern. So $C = \pi d = 2\pi r$, and backwards $d = C \div \pi$. Use $\pi \approx 3.14$ or $\tfrac{22}{7}$, or keep π for an exact answer ($10\pi$ cm).

## Why it matters

Wheels, tracks, pipes and anything round. π comes back in the area of a circle (Year 8), cylinders (Year 9) and well beyond.

## What students get wrong

- **Radius for diameter.** A wheel of radius 30 cm given C = π × 30. Double first: C = π × 60.
- **π as exactly 3.14 or 22/7.** Both are close; π never ends.
- **Multiplying by π to go backwards.** The diameter is smaller than the circumference: divide.
- **A bigger π for a bigger circle.** C and d grow together, so C ÷ d stays π.

## Year 8: the area of a circle

Slices of a circle laid top to tail make a near-rectangle πr long and r high, so A = πr²; it is between 2r² and 4r². To find the radius from an area, divide by π and take the square root. Parts of circles (semicircles, quarters, rings, tracks) are fractions of circles added to or taken from other shapes. The common slips: 2πr for the area, and stopping at r².

## The words

**centre**, **radius**, **diameter**, **chord**, **circumference**, **π** (pi), **sector**, **semicircle**.
