## The idea in plain words

A solid can be drawn flat in three ways, and each shows something different.

A **net** unfolds the solid: every face is drawn at its true size, so a net gives the surface area. A cube's net is six squares that cover six different faces; six squares alone is not enough. A triangular prism's net is two triangles and three rectangles.

An **isometric drawing** shows what the solid looks like in 3D, but squashes the faces and hides what is behind.

**Views** (top, front and side) are what you would see looking from one side. A front or side view shows the tallest pile in each column, so cubes behind it are hidden. A top view with the height written on each pile shows every cube.

## Why it matters

Engineers, builders and designers pass solids around as drawings. Choosing the drawing that shows what you need is a reasoning skill: a net for cutting and costing, views for building.

## What students get wrong

- **Any six squares make a cube net.** Test by folding: every face must be covered once.
- **Adding every pile to get a view.** A view shows only the tallest pile in each column.
- **Mixing up front and side views.** Say which way you are looking.

## Year 8: points in 3D

A third axis, z, points up from the x-y plane, so a point in space is (x, y, z): along x, along y, then up. The order is always x, y, z, and points on the floor have z = 0. A box from the origin to (x, y, z) has edges x, y and z long. Real systems do the same with other names: east, north and up for a drone, metres up in a building, latitude, longitude and altitude for a plane. The common slip: writing the height first.
