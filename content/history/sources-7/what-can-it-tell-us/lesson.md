You now have four questions to ask of any source.

**Origin**: who made it, when and where. **Content**: what it shows or says. **Context**: what was going on. **Purpose**: why it was made.

On their own they just describe the source. Together, they help you decide **what it can tell you**, and what it can't. That depends on what you want to find out.

## Predict: what can it tell us?

```step
stage: predict
insight: "Committing first shows whether students treat a source as simply true or untrue instead of asking what it can tell us about this question."
check:
  type: predict
  q: "The First Emperor had an inscription cut into stone saying everyone lived in peace under his rule. Can it tell us whether everyone really did?"
  options: ["Not reliably: it praises him, so it mostly shows how he wanted to be seen", "Yes: it was carved in stone for everyone to read, so it must be true", "No: a source that praises a ruler can tell us nothing at all", "Yes: it was made at the time, so it shows what really happened"]
  actual: "Not reliably. Historians think it was made to win support, so it is better evidence of how he wanted to be seen than of what everyone thought. That is still useful."
```

## Try: match the questions to Source C

```step
stage: run
insight: "Matching each of the four questions to its answer for one source shows what each question adds, before the answers are joined into a conclusion."
check:
  type: match
  q: "Match each question to its answer for Source C, the First Emperor's stone inscription."
  pairs:
    - ["Origin: who, when, where?", "Set up by order of the First Emperor, around 219 BCE, on a mountain"]
    - ["Content: what does it say?", "He brought peace, and one set of laws, weights and measures"]
    - ["Context: what was going on?", "The Qin had just defeated six rival kingdoms"]
    - ["Purpose: why was it made?", "Probably to show his power and win people's support"]
  explain: "Each question gives a different piece. You can ask them in any order that suits the source. What matters is that you have all four before you say what the source can tell you."
```

You can ask the four questions in whatever order suits the source. The conclusion comes once you have all four answers, because it is built from them.

```step
      explain: "A good conclusion names what the source can tell us and what it cannot."
```

## Vary: the same questions on a new source

```step
stage: modify
insight: "Changing the source and the question shows that the same four questions give different conclusions."
tasks:
  - check:
      type: question
      q: "You want to know: What rules did Qin officials have to follow? Which source is best, B (laws on bamboo slips) or C (the inscription)?"
      options: ["Source B: it was written for officials, to tell them the rules", "Source C: it was set up for everyone to read", "Source C: it says there was one set of laws", "Neither: both were made by the Qin state, so both are biased"]
      answer: 0
      feedback: [null, "Being read by everyone does not make it list the rules. Think about its purpose.", "It mentions laws, but only to praise him. The slips list the rules.", "Made by the state does not make a source useless. Rules written by officials are just what this question needs."]
      explain: "Source B was made to tell officials the rules, so it fits the question. Source C was made to show power."
  - check:
      type: question
      q: "Source D, the 2019 museum label, says the warriors were found by farmers in 1974. Which question is Source D good evidence for?"
      options: ["How the warriors were found in the 1970s", "Why the First Emperor wanted a clay army", "How the craftsmen felt about their work", "What the warriors were made of"]
      answer: 0
      feedback: [null, "It was written in 2019 by people who were not there. It can only repeat what historians think.", "It was written in 2019 by museum staff, who did not meet the craftsmen.", "For that, the warriors themselves are better evidence than a label about them."]
      explain: "The label is a secondary source about the warriors. It is good for how they were found and how museums explain them today, not for what the emperor thought."
```

## Practise: say what a source can tell us

```step
stage: practise
insight: "Using all four questions on a fresh case, and writing the conclusion in a form that names its limit."
tasks:
  - check:
      type: question
      q: "A clay warrior was made for the First Emperor's tomb. Which is the best conclusion about what it can tell us?"

      answer: 0
      feedback: [null, "It was made for one man's tomb. It cannot tell us what everyone believed.", "Figures standing in a pit show equipment, not how battles were fought.", "Different faces do not prove each is a portrait of a real person. Historians still debate that."]
      explain: "Its content shows armour and weapons. Its purpose and context show how much effort went into the emperor's tomb."
  - check:
      type: reflect
      text: "I took one source, asked who made it, what it shows, what was going on and why it was made, and then said one thing it can tell us and one thing it cannot."
```

```step
  answer: 0
  feedback: [null, "Neither has to be wrong. They were made for different jobs.", "Both were made during the Qin, only a couple of years apart.", "Source B was written by Qin officials. It shows punishments because it was written to tell officials the rules."]
```
