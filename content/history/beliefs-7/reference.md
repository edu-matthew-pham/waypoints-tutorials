These lessons use **ancient China in the Qin and Han period** as the worked example. If your class studies a different society, use its card with the same questions: **What did many people believe? What did those beliefs lead them to do? What evidence shows it? What does it reveal about what the society valued, and what set it apart?**

Each card is a short summary to start from, not a full account. Check it against your class text.

## Ancient China (Qin and Han)

- **Beliefs:** many people believed ancestors' spirits lived on and could help their families; Confucian teaching on filial piety (duty to parents, in life and after death); the Mandate of Heaven (a ruler rules only while he rules well); an afterlife much like this life.
- **Practices:** offerings to ancestors; careful funerals; tombs stocked with food, clothes, dishes and models of houses, granaries and farms; advisers reading floods and droughts as Heaven's warnings to the emperor.
- **Evidence:** the Mawangdui tombs (2nd century BCE), with their bamboo lists of grave goods and painted silk banner; Han tomb models; the First Emperor's terracotta army (he died in 210 BCE); the *Analects* of Confucius.
- **Sources to look for:** museum pages on Mawangdui (Hunan Museum) and on Han tomb models; the *Analects*.

## Ancient Egypt

- **Beliefs:** many gods; an afterlife in which the preserved body was needed (texts describe the *ba*, part of the spirit, as unable to survive without it); a judgement after death, shown in texts as the heart weighed against the feather of Maat.
- **Practices:** mummification for those who could afford it; tombs with goods for the afterlife; funerary texts placed with the dead.
- **Evidence:** mummies and coffins; tomb paintings; Book of the Dead papyri (from about 1550 BCE).
- **Sources to look for:** museum pages on mummification and on the Book of the Dead, such as the Papyrus of Hunefer.

## Ancient Greece

- **Beliefs:** many gods, who could be asked for advice; heroic ideals described in the *Iliad*.
- **Practices:** asking the oracle of Apollo at Delphi, where the priestess (the Pythia) was believed to speak for the god; lawmakers, colonists and cities asked it for guidance, including about planned wars; both burial and cremation of the dead.
- **Evidence:** remains of the sanctuary at Delphi; inscriptions; writers such as Herodotus, who report oracle answers (written later, so read with care).
- **Sources to look for:** museum or encyclopedia pages on the Delphic oracle; Herodotus.

## Ancient Rome

- **Beliefs:** many gods, including household gods (the Lares) believed to watch over the family and home.
- **Practices:** a household shrine (lararium), often in the main hall or near the kitchen hearth, where families prayed and made offerings at family festivals; public festivals and sacrifices at temples.
- **Evidence:** household shrines and statuettes of the Lares; wall paintings; tomb inscriptions.
- **Sources to look for:** museum pages on Roman household gods; tomb inscriptions.

## Ancient India

- **Beliefs as ancient texts describe them:** the Vedas, among the oldest religious texts of India, centre on offerings to the gods through a sacred fire (Agni); Brahmins served as priests. Later traditions, often called Brahmanism and then Hinduism, grew from this.
- **Practices:** fire offerings; rites marking stages of life (samskaras), the last of them the funeral rites; cremation, with a Rigveda hymn asking Agni to carry the dead to the ancestors.
- **Evidence:** the Vedic hymns themselves, passed down by memory and written much later; later law and religious texts. Practice varied by period, region and group.
- **Sources to look for:** encyclopedia pages on Vedic religion and on antyeshti (funeral rites); translations of the Rigveda.
