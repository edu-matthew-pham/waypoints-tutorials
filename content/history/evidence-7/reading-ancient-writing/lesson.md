An **inscription** is writing cut or marked onto a surface such as stone. Laws, letters and accounts can also be written sources. Reading an ancient text can involve damaged words, unfamiliar meanings and translation. Even when the words can be read, we still ask who made the text, why and in what circumstances.

These source cards are **made up**, set in an invented ancient town. They are not translations of a real Qin inscription.

- **Written card:** a ruler's stone inscription praises a new storehouse and calls the town prosperous. One damaged word could mean “gift” or “payment”.
- **Physical card:** an excavation records a storehouse floor below burnt roof wood.

Written, physical and oral sources need questions appropriate to them. Writing is not automatically the most reliable kind. Comparing different sources on a question is sometimes called **triangulation**.

## Worked example: follow the reasoning

```step
stage: investigate
insight: Test the main inference, then repair the planted misconception before carrying it into a new case.
tasks:
  - check:
      type: question
      q: Which investigation would help most?
      options:

        - Check the dates, purpose and find records of both cards
        - Decide from which source is longest
      answer: 1
      explain: Compare what each source supports and whether they describe the same time.
      feedback:
        - Written form does not guarantee accuracy.
        - null
        - Length does not establish reliability.
  - check:
      type: question
      q: "Nib says: “It was carved in stone, so the town must have been prosperous for everyone.” Which reply is strongest?"
      options:
        - Stone writing can never be used
        - A physical find always tells the whole story
        - The inscription shows a claim; its purpose and other evidence need checking
      answer: 2
      explain: An inscription is evidence of what was recorded. Assessing the wider claim needs more evidence.
      feedback:
        - A source can still be useful when its claim needs checking.
        - Physical evidence also has limits.
        - null
      fault: logic
      misconception: writing-wins-automatically
    smaller:
      check:
        type: question
        q: Does a ruler praising a town record every resident’s experience?
        options:
          - Yes, because it survives
          - No, its purpose can limit what it says
        answer: 1
        explain: Survival and written form do not make an account complete.
        feedback:
          - Look at what the evidence actually records.
          - null
    hint:
      - Separate “the ruler said this” from “this was true for everyone”.
      - Look for evidence produced for a different purpose.
```

For “What did the ruler want readers to think?”, the inscription helps. For “Was there a fire after the floor was used?”, the layer record helps. The evidence can answer different parts of the story. A boast of prosperity does not prove everyone was prosperous; burnt wood does not prove the inscription was false when it was made.
