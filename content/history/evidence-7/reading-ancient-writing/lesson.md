An **inscription** is writing cut or painted onto something hard, such as stone or metal. Laws, letters and accounts are written sources too. Reading ancient writing is not always easy: words can be broken off, a word can have more than one meaning, and it has to be translated. Even when we can read every word, we still ask who wrote it, why, and what was going on at the time.

These two source cards are **made up**, set in an invented ancient town. They are not translations of a real Qin inscription.

- **Written card:** a ruler's stone inscription praises a new storehouse and says the town is prosperous. One damaged word could mean “gift” or “payment”.
- **Physical card:** a dig finds a storehouse floor with burnt roof wood lying on top of it.

Written sources, objects and spoken accounts each need their own questions. Writing is not always the most reliable kind. Checking a claim against different kinds of source is sometimes called **triangulation**.

## Predict: which source wins?

```step
stage: predict
insight: Students commit to whether one kind of source is automatically more reliable before comparing the two cards.
check:
  type: predict
  q: Which source is automatically more reliable?
  options:
    - The inscription, because it is written
    - The dig, because it is physical
    - Neither; ask what each can show about the question
  actual: Neither wins on its own. It depends on the question, who made each source, why, and what each one leaves out.
```

## Worked example: the storehouse

```step
stage: investigate
insight: Students plan a fair comparison of the two cards, then repair the planted misconception that writing wins automatically.
tasks:
  - check:
      type: question
      q: What would help you most to compare the two cards?
      options:
        - Believe the inscription and stop there
        - Check the dates, purpose and dig records of both cards
        - Go with whichever card says more
      answer: 1
      explain: Find out what each source supports, and whether they are even about the same time.
      feedback:
        - Being written down does not make a claim true.
        - null
        - Saying more does not make a source more reliable.
  - check:
      type: question
      q: "Nib says: “It was carved in stone, so the town must have been prosperous for everyone.” Which reply is best?"
      options:
        - Writing in stone can never be used as evidence
        - The burnt floor tells you the whole story instead
        - The inscription shows what the ruler claimed; check why it was made and what other evidence says
      answer: 2
      explain: An inscription is good evidence of what someone chose to record. Whether it was true for everyone needs more evidence.
      feedback:
        - A source can still be useful when its claim needs checking.
        - Objects have limits too. The floor cannot tell you how people lived.
        - null
      fault: logic
      misconception: writing-wins-automatically
    smaller:
      check:
        type: question
        q: When a ruler praises a town, does that tell you how every person in the town lived?
        options:
          - Yes, because the inscription has survived
          - No, because the ruler had reasons to praise it
        answer: 1
        explain: Surviving, and being written down, do not make an account complete.
        feedback:
          - Lasting a long time does not make a source tell the whole story.
          - null
    hint:
      - Split “the ruler said this” from “this was true for everyone”.
      - Look for a source that was made for a different reason.
```

If your question is “What did the ruler want people to think?”, the inscription helps. If your question is “Was there a fire after the floor was used?”, the dig helps. Each answers a different part of the story. A boast about prosperity does not prove everyone was well off. Burnt wood does not prove the inscription was false when it was carved.

## Vary: two readings of one word

Now change one thing. One reader translates the damaged word as “gift”. Another translates it as “payment”. Both explain their reasons. The card itself does not settle it.

```step
stage: modify
insight: Students keep an uncertain reading visible instead of choosing the one that suits their story.
check:
  type: question
  q: How should you use the damaged word?
  options:
    - Say it is uncertain, and look for other texts or records that could help
    - Choose the translation that makes the better story
    - Write it as if the word were clear
  answer: 0
  explain: Keep both readings in view, and look for evidence that could decide between them.
  feedback:
    - null
    - A better story is not a reason to pick a translation.
    - The damage is still there. Pretending otherwise hides a real limit.
```

## Practise: an order to repair a road

```step
stage: practise
insight: Students apply the same reasoning to a new written source on their own.
check:
  type: question
  q: A second made-up inscription orders a road to be repaired. What does it show most directly?
  options:
    - Every part of the road was repaired at once
    - No one used the road before the order
    - Someone gave an order to repair it; whether it was done needs other evidence
  answer: 2
  explain: An order is not proof that it was carried out. The remains of the road, or later records, could help.
  feedback:
    - An order does not show the work was finished.
    - The order says nothing about who used the road before.
    - null
```

## Typical: one text in three scripts

```step
stage: make
tier: typical
insight: Students see how comparing versions of the same text can help with translation.
check:
  type: question
  q: "The British Museum describes the Rosetta Stone as carrying versions of the same decree in three scripts: hieroglyphs, demotic and ancient Greek. Why would comparing the versions help?"
  options:
    - All three scripts use the same signs
    - Reading one version can help you work out another
    - It means translation no longer needs any judgement
  answer: 1
  explain: Matching versions can help test a reading. It does not remove every translation or context problem.
  feedback:
    - The three scripts look quite different from each other.
    - null
    - Comparing helps with judgement. It does not replace it.
```
