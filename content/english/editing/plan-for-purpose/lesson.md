**What you're learning.** Good writing starts before the first sentence. You decide what your text is for and who will read it, then choose the structure and the words that will work for that reader. A plan that lists only *what* you'll say is half a plan. A full plan also says *how* you'll say it, and why.

## Purpose and reader

**You will:** read a writing task and say, in one sentence each, who the text is for and what it should do for them (inform, persuade, entertain, explain, reflect).

**Good looks like:** a named reader ("Year 3 students", not "people") and a purpose with a verb ("persuade the principal to fund it").

```step
stage: predict
insight: "Naming the reader and purpose first is the decision every later planning choice depends on (the hinge in this node)."
check: { type: reflect, text: "I wrote who my text is for and what it should do for them." }
```

## See a model

**You will:** look at a finished plan and find where it names its reader, its purpose, its structure and its language choices, and the reason given for each.

**Good looks like:** you can point to one structure choice and one language choice and say which reader each one is for.

```step
stage: run
insight: "Seeing a plan that records how as well as what shows the difference between content-listing and purposeful planning."
check: { type: reflect, text: "I found the reader, the purpose, a structure choice and a language choice in the model plan, and the reason for each." }
```

## Learn the strategy

**You will:** learn four planning questions to ask every time.

1. **Who** is reading?
2. **What** should it do for them?
3. **Which shape** suits that? (the text type and its stages)
4. **Which words** suit that? (formal or friendly, technical or everyday, short or long sentences)

For questions 3 and 4, add **because…** and name the reader.

**Good looks like:** every choice in your plan has a *because* that names the reader or the purpose.

```step
stage: investigate
insight: "A named, repeatable strategy (four questions with a because) is the strongest-evidence way to teach planning."
check: { type: reflect, text: "I can say the four planning questions and why each choice needs a because." }
```

## Try it with support

**You will:** plan a given task using a frame. Copy it into your own document and fill it in.

| What should it do for them? | | |

| Which words suit that? | | |

**Good looks like:** all four rows are filled, and rows 3 and 4 have a *because* that names the reader.

```step
stage: modify
insight: "Using the strategy on a given brief with a frame makes the four questions a habit before students plan their own text."
check: { type: reflect, text: "I filled in all four rows for the given task, with a because for the shape and the words." }
```

## Make yours

**You will:** plan a text you are writing for class, in your own document, using the four questions. Keep it short: a plan, not a draft.

**Good looks like:** your plan names a reader and a purpose, and at least one structure choice and one language choice, each with a reason.

```step
stage: make
insight: "Planning the student's own text with the strategy is the node's evidence: purpose, audience, and a structure and language choice with reasons."
check: { type: reflect, text: "I planned my own text with the four questions, and gave a reason for at least one shape choice and one word choice." }
```

## Check

**You will:** check your plan against what good looks like, then swap with a partner. Can they tell who your text is for just from your plan?

- ☐ It names a reader, not "people".
- ☐ It says what the text should do, with a verb.
- ☐ It has at least one structure choice with a reason.
- ☐ It has at least one language choice with a reason.

```step
stage: info
insight: "Self-checking against the criteria, then a partner reading the plan cold, is the self-regulation part of the strategy."
check: { type: reflect, text: "I checked my plan against the list, and a partner could tell who it is for." }
```
