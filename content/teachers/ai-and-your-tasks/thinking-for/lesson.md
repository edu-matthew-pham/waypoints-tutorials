In this course you redesign **one real task**: one you're setting in the next fortnight. Have it beside you (the instructions you'd give students). No AI tool is needed, and every activity works on paper.

This lesson starts where every AI decision should: **what is this task for?** The course's five example tasks are on the [reference page](../reference/).

## Think first

```step
stage: predict
insight: "Teachers who say a task is for all of its parts find it hardest to decide where AI fits; naming one or two kinds of thinking makes the decision possible."
check:
  type: predict
  q: "A Year 9 science task: plan a fair test of which antacid neutralises the most acid, run it, and write a report. Which part is the task really for?"
  options: ["Planning the fair test", "Writing the report in the right format", "Explaining what the results show", "All of it equally"]
  actual: "Usually the planning and the explaining: those are the thinking a practical report exists to build. The format is how students show it. Teachers who say \"all of it\" have the hardest time deciding where AI fits, because every part then looks equally important to protect."
```

## The idea

```step
stage: investigate
insight: "A task is a vehicle for some thinking; the parts that are that thinking stay with the student, and the rest can be helped."
```

A task is a vehicle. The essay, the report, the folio are how students do some **thinking** you want them to get better at. Name that thinking in one sentence: *what should students be able to do after this task that they couldn't before?*

Then sort the task's parts into three groups (the same words students use in *AI at our school*):

- **Keep the thinking:** this part *is* the learning. If AI did it, the student would miss the point of the task.
- **AI can help:** not the goal of this task. Help here can free time for the thinking (a spellcheck on a science poster).
- **Depends on the goal:** help for one student or one goal, a crutch for another. A hint when stuck keeps a student going; a worked answer can take the thinking away. Translating a word helps in science; in a language class it may be the whole point.

**Age and stage matter.** A part can move groups between Year 7 and Year 10: once a skill is learned, help with it stops costing learning.

Notice what isn't in this lesson: what AI can or can't do. That's next lesson. Decide what the task is for first, or you'll design around the tool instead of the learning.

## Worked example

```step
stage: investigate
insight: "Naming the thinking as a sentence about what students can do is what makes the sort possible."
check:
  type: question
  q: "The Year 10 persuasive essay: \"Should our school start later? About 800 words.\" Which sentence best names the thinking it's for?"
  options:
    - "Building an argument from reasons and evidence, and answering the strongest objection, in your own voice."
    - "Writing an 800-word persuasive essay."
    - "Knowing the arguments for and against later school start times."
    - "Using persuasive language features correctly."
  answer: 0
  feedback:
    - null
    - "That's the task, not the thinking. What should students be able to do after it?"
    - "That's content. Students could know every argument and still not be able to build one."
    - "Part of it, maybe, but language features without an argument behind them aren't persuasion."
  explain: "The first names a skill students can get better at, in a sentence. With it, the parts sort: choosing a position and reasons is the thinking; checking spelling isn't."
```

The persuasive essay, sorted:

| Part | Group | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Choosing a position and the reasons for it | Keep the thinking | That's the argument. |
| Answering the strongest objection | Keep the thinking | The hardest part, and the most valuable. |
| Finding a statistic to support a reason | Depends on the goal | Fine if research isn't the goal; not if it is. |
| Feedback on a draft | Depends on the goal | Helpful if the student decides what to change. |
| Spelling and punctuation | AI can help | Unless conventions are what you're assessing. |

## Check

```step
stage: practise
insight: "Sorting parts of other subjects' tasks builds the habit before teachers judge their own."
check:
  type: sort
  q: "Sort these parts of the example tasks. Where does AI fit?"
  options: ["Keep the thinking", "AI can help", "Depends on the goal"]
  items:
    - ["Maths problem set: choosing which method to use for each problem", "Keep the thinking", "Choosing the method is what lets students do the next one alone."]
    - ["Maths problem set: checking the arithmetic after you've finished", "AI can help", "A check after the thinking, like a calculator; unless the arithmetic is the skill being learned."]
    - ["History source task: working out who made the cartoon, when and why", "Keep the thinking", "Origin is the thinking the task is for."]
    - ["History source task: background on the 1916 conscription debate", "Depends on the goal", "Background helps if the goal is judging the source; less so if researching the context is the skill."]
    - ["Design folio: deciding what a younger student needs from a phone stand", "Keep the thinking", "Design decisions come from the user's needs."]
    - ["Design folio: tidying the layout of the folio pages", "AI can help", "Presentation isn't the design thinking."]
    - ["Science report: explaining why one antacid worked better", "Keep the thinking", "Explaining results is what the report is for."]
    - ["Science report: turning a table of results into a graph", "Depends on the goal", "In Year 7, drawing the graph may be the skill; by Year 10 it's probably not."]
```

## Apply

```step
stage: make
insight: "Writing the thinking down and sorting their own task's parts is the decision every later lesson builds on."
tasks:
  - check:
      type: reasoning
      q: "Your task: write the thinking it's for in one sentence, then sort its main parts into the three groups."
      placeholder: "The thinking: students get better at … Keep the thinking: … AI can help: … Depends on the goal: …"
      models:
        - title: "Science (the example report)"
          text: "The thinking: planning a fair test and explaining what the results show. Keep the thinking: the plan, the explanation of results. AI can help: spelling, the layout of the report. Depends on the goal: drawing the graph (Year 7 yes, Year 10 no), background on acids."
        - title: "History (the example source task)"
          text: "The thinking: asking where a source came from separately from what it shows, then judging its usefulness. Keep the thinking: origin and purpose, the judgement of usefulness. AI can help: checking spelling. Depends on the goal: background on the 1916 debate."
      criteria:
        - "The thinking is a sentence about what students can do, not the task's name."
        - "Each part is in a group, with a reason."
        - "I've said where the answer would change for a younger or older class."
  - check: { type: reflect, text: "I've written the thinking my task is for where I'll find it next lesson." }
```

### Name the thinking and sort the parts

Write one sentence: *after this task, students are better at …*. Then list the task's main parts (three to six) and sort them.

### Keep it

Keep what you wrote (on paper, or copy it into your planning). Lessons 2 to 4 use it.

```teacher
summary: "Teachers name the thinking a task is for, see a persuasive essay sorted into keep the thinking, AI can help and depends on the goal, then sort their own task's parts."
time: 20
timing: "Think first 3 min · The idea and worked example 6 min · Check 4 min · Apply 7 min"
goals:
  - "I can name the thinking a task is for, as a sentence about what students can do."
  - "I can sort a task's parts into keep the thinking, AI can help and depends on the goal."
  - "I can say where that sort would change for a younger or older class."
before: |
  **For a facilitator** (a head of department, or the AI committee's staff lead). Ask everyone, a few days ahead, to bring **a task they're setting in the next fortnight**: the instructions they'd give students. A real, near task keeps the course practical; a hypothetical one stays hypothetical. No AI tool is needed and nothing is shared beyond the room.

  This course closes the know-how gaps that *AI at our school* teaches a committee to notice. It lives in the **task instructions** layer: what each teacher decides for each task. It's about the task, not the tool; products appear only in dated teacher notes.

  **A 50-minute staff meeting** (lessons 1 and 2): 5 min why we're here (most AI use can't be seen or caught, so the task is where we have most influence); 20 min this lesson; 5 min pairs read each other's "thinking" sentence; 15 min lesson 2's idea and check; 5 min pairs swap tasks for lesson 2's paper test. Teachers finish lesson 2's Apply on their own.

  Keep the room off "which tools should we allow". That's the committee's work (*AI at our school*) and the termly guidelines. Here, the question is what this task is for.
running:
  - "Think first: everyone locks in. Take a quick show of hands: who chose \"all of it\"? Don't argue yet."
  - "The idea: read the three groups aloud. Ask for one 'depends on the goal' example from someone's subject."
  - "Worked example: do the question, then look at the table. The statistic row usually starts a useful argument: research skill or not?"
  - "Check: individual. Discuss the graph item: it's where year level changes the answer."
  - "Apply: individual, then pairs read each other's thinking sentence. Is it a sentence about what students can do?"
struggle: "Teachers name the task or the content (\"the essay\", \"the causes of WWI\") instead of the thinking. Ask: what should students be able to do after it that they couldn't before? Let them rewrite it; don't supply it."
stuck:
  - see: "A teacher says every part of their task is 'keep the thinking'."
    ask: "If AI checked the spelling, or drew the graph, what would the student not learn?"
    fix: "Usually something moves to 'AI can help'. If nothing does, the task may be tightly focused, which is fine; keep the sort."
  - see: "A teacher didn't bring a task."
    ask: "What's the next thing you'll set?"
    fix: "Use that, from memory, or pick the example task closest to their subject from the reference page."
  - see: "The conversation turns to which AI tools are allowed."
    ask: "Whatever's allowed, what is this task for?"
    fix: "Note the tools question for the committee and come back to the task."
stretch: "Sort the same task for a class two years younger or older. What moves, and why?"
support: "Start from the example task closest to their subject (reference page), sort it with a partner, then do their own."
leads: "Lesson 2 (Could AI do it now?) tests each part against what AI can do today. The parts AI could finish alone, that are also 'keep the thinking', are where lesson 3's redesign goes."
exit:
  q: "Why decide what a task is for before thinking about AI?"
  a: "Because whether AI helps or harms depends on the thinking the task exists to build: the same help frees time on one part and skips the learning on another."
```
