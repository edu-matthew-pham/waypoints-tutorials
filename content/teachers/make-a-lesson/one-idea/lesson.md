Every lesson on this site teaches **one idea**: one thing students can do or explain by the end. In this lesson you'll find the first fault in a deliberately weak lesson, then write the one idea for your own primer.

## Think first

```step
stage: predict
insight: "Teachers who have just sat the weak lesson usually say it covered seasons and the Moon: two topics, and no idea a student could say back."
check:
  type: predict
  q: "Do the example lesson, Seasons and the Moon, as a student would (3 minutes). Then: what would a student say they learned?"
  options: ["Why we have seasons", "Seasons and the Moon", "That the Earth's axis is tilted 23.5 degrees", "Not sure"]
  actual: "Most likely \"seasons and the Moon\": two topics. Or the one number the checks asked for. Neither is an idea a student could use: the lesson never decided what students should be able to explain."
```

Open [Example to improve: Seasons and the Moon](../example-seasons/) in a new tab and do it as a student would. Then come back and lock in a guess.

## The idea

```step
stage: investigate
insight: "One idea is a sentence about how something works or how to do something, small enough to check with one good question."
```

A **topic** names an area: *seasons*, *fractions*, *sources*, *persuasive writing*. An **idea** says something about it that a student can use: *the half of the Earth tilted towards the Sun has summer, because its sunlight is more concentrated and its days are longer.*

Three tests for one idea:

1. **It's a sentence**, not a heading. If it has no verb, it's a topic.
2. **A student could say it back**, in their own words, at the end.
3. **One good question could check it.** If you'd need two unrelated questions, it's two ideas.

A primer is short (four steps), so one idea is all it can carry. A second idea is a second primer.

## Worked example

```step
stage: investigate
insight: "Narrowing is choosing what to leave out; the curriculum waypoint's success criteria help choose."
check:
  type: question
  q: "Which is the best one idea for a primer narrowed from the example lesson?"
  options:
    - "The half of the Earth tilted towards the Sun has summer, because its sunlight is more concentrated and its days are longer."
    - "Seasons and the phases of the Moon."
    - "The Earth's axis is tilted by 23.5 degrees."
    - "Students will learn about seasons."
  answer: 0
  feedback:
    - null
    - "That's two topics, the fault you just found."
    - "A true fact, but not an idea: a student could recite it and still not know why summer is hot."
    - "That names the topic, not what students can explain."
  explain: "The first one is a sentence, a student could say it back, and one good question (\"It's summer in Sydney: which half of the Earth leans towards the Sun?\") would check it."
```

Narrowing the example lesson, one step at a time:

1. **Topic:** seasons and the Moon. Two topics: keep one. Seasons.
2. **Still a topic:** seasons. What should students be able to *explain*? Why it's hotter in summer.
3. **One idea:** the half of the Earth tilted towards the Sun has summer, because its sunlight is more concentrated and its days are longer.

The Moon's phases are a fine primer too. They're just a different one.

The same narrowing in History: *sources* → *working out where a source came from* → *a source's origin (who made it, when, where) and its content (what it shows) are separate questions.* That's the one idea in lesson 1's primer.

## Check

```step
stage: practise
insight: "Sorting other people's statements is quicker than judging your own, and builds the eye for it."
check:
  type: sort
  q: "One idea, a topic, or two ideas?"
  options: ["One idea", "A topic", "Two ideas"]
  items:
    - ["Fractions", "A topic", "No verb: it names an area."]
    - ["A fraction's denominator says how many equal parts the whole is cut into", "One idea", "A sentence a student could say back, checked by one question."]
    - ["Equivalent fractions name the same amount, and to add fractions you need a common denominator", "Two ideas", "Two moves joined by and: two primers (the second needs the first)."]
    - ["A source's origin and its content are separate questions", "One idea", "The idea from lesson 1."]
    - ["Persuasive writing", "A topic", "What should students be able to do with it?"]
    - ["Photosynthesis makes glucose, and respiration releases its energy", "Two ideas", "Two processes: two primers."]
    - ["A loop repeats the steps inside it", "One idea", "One sentence, one move."]
    - ["Ancient Rome", "A topic", "An area, not an idea."]
```

## Apply

```step
stage: make
insight: "Writing the idea down, then checking each step against it, is the decision Create's design panel keeps beside the lesson."
check:
  type: reasoning
  q: "Write the one idea for your primer, as a sentence a student could say back."
  placeholder: "Students leave able to say: …"
  models:
    - title: "History (lesson 1's primer)"
      text: "A source's origin (who made it, when and where) and its content (what it shows) are separate questions, and you answer both before deciding what the source means."
    - title: "Maths"
      text: "Multiplying by a number between 0 and 1 makes the answer smaller, because you're taking part of the number, not adding more copies of it."
  criteria:
    - "It's a sentence with a verb, not a heading."
    - "A student could say it back in their own words."
    - "One good question would show whether a student has it."
  explain: "If your sentence has \"and\" joining two different things, try dropping one: it's probably a second primer."
```

Choose which primer to keep working on:

- **Keep lesson 1's History primer.** In Create, choose **Open existing work**, then **Resume saved workspace**.
- **Or start one on a topic you teach.** In Create's **Start with a lesson idea**, choose your **Subject** and **Lesson structure: Primer: one idea**, type your topic, and press **Add a lesson starter**. If your subject is under **Curriculum waypoints (optional)**, choose your waypoint first, as in lesson 1.

Write your one idea here. Then put it in Create too: under the editor, **Your design decisions** › **The one idea**. Read your lesson's steps and tick *Does every step serve this one idea?* only when they all do.

```teacher
summary: "Teachers find the first fault in the weak example (two topics, no idea), narrow a topic to one idea, and write the one idea for their own primer."
time: 20
timing: "Think first (do the example) 5 min · The idea and worked example 5 min · Check 3 min · Apply 7 min"
goals:
  - "I can tell a topic from one idea."
  - "I can narrow a topic to one idea a student could say back."
  - "I can write the one idea for my own lesson."
before: |
  **For a facilitator.** Everyone needs the topic they brought (lesson 1's notes ask for one they teach next week). Teachers who don't have one can keep lesson 1's History primer.

  The example lesson (*Seasons and the Moon*) is weak on purpose, with one fault per design decision; this lesson is about the first. Its science is correct, which is the point: it looks fine, and it still teaches nothing a student could say back.

  In the 50-minute session (lesson 1's notes), this is the second half: 15 minutes for the lesson, then 5 for pairs to read each other's one idea against the three tests.
running:
  - "Think first: everyone does the example lesson as a student, quietly, then locks in. Ask a few people what a student would say they learned."
  - "The idea: read the three tests aloud. Try one on a topic from the room."
  - "Check: individual. Discuss the fractions and photosynthesis items: both sound like one lesson, and both are two ideas."
  - "Apply: pairs read each other's sentence against the three tests before typing it into Create."
struggle: "Teachers write the topic with a verb attached (\"Students will learn about fractions\"). Ask what a student could say at the end, not what the lesson is about, and don't supply the sentence for them."
stuck:
  - see: "A teacher's idea has two parts joined by 'and'."
    ask: "Would one question check both halves?"
    fix: "Keep the half their waypoint or unit needs first; the other half is a second primer."
  - see: "The idea is a fact (a date, a definition, a number)."
    ask: "Could a student recite it and still not understand it?"
    fix: "Add the because: what the fact explains, or what it lets students do."
  - see: "Resume saved workspace isn't there."
    ask: "Same browser and device as lesson 1?"
    fix: "Create keeps work in the browser it was made in. Start a new primer on their own topic instead."
stretch: "Look at a lesson you taught this week. Write its one idea. If you can't, what were the two (or more) it tried to teach?"
support: "Give the three tests as a printed card, and let them narrow out loud with a partner first: topic, then what students should be able to explain, then the sentence."
leads: "Lesson 3 (The misconception and the opening move) finds the next fault in the example: it never brings out the wrong idea students arrive with."
exit:
  q: "What's the difference between a topic and one idea?"
  a: "A topic names an area (seasons). One idea is a sentence a student could say back and one question could check (the half of the Earth tilted towards the Sun has summer)."
```
