The example lesson opens by explaining the tilt. Its students never say what they think first, so the most common wrong idea about seasons never comes up. In this lesson you'll choose the misconception your primer tackles and write an opening question that brings it out.

## Think first

```step
stage: predict
insight: "Teachers usually know the distance misconception exists; the point is that the example lesson leaves it untouched."
check:
  type: predict
  q: "Ask a class why it's hotter in summer. What's the answer you'll hear most?"
  options: ["The Earth is closer to the Sun in summer", "The Earth's axis is tilted", "The Sun gives out more heat in summer", "There are fewer clouds"]
  actual: "\"We're closer to the Sun.\" It's the classic misconception, held by many adults too. The example lesson never asks, so a student can read about the tilt, get every check right, and still believe it's distance."
```

## The idea

```step
stage: investigate
insight: "The opening move is chosen for the misconception: a question where the wrong idea gives a definite, wrong answer."
```

Students don't arrive empty. If a lesson explains the right idea without first bringing out the wrong one, many students keep both: the right one for the quiz, the wrong one for everything else.

So the lesson's first move is a question students answer **before** being taught, where **the misconception gives a definite wrong answer**. Students lock in their answer, then see what really happens. The surprise is what makes the idea stick.

A good opening question:

- can be answered with no teaching (a prediction, a puzzle, a choice);
- has the misconception as one of its options, worded the way students would say it;
- has an answer that clearly contradicts the misconception, so it can't be quietly kept.

**Where to find the misconception:** students' wrong answers in your own classes; and, when you start from a curriculum waypoint, Create's **Possible misconceptions** list (from Waypoints Kit's hinge reasons and response modes), each with **Use this**.

## Worked example

```step
stage: investigate
insight: "A question about the other hemisphere turns the misconception against itself."
check:
  type: question
  q: "Which opening question would bring out the distance misconception best?"
  options:
    - "In early January the Earth is at its closest to the Sun. What season is it in London then?"
    - "What causes the seasons?"
    - "How many degrees is the Earth's axis tilted?"
    - "Draw the Earth going around the Sun."
  answer: 0
  feedback:
    - null
    - "Students who already read the answer will say tilt; students who don't know will guess. Neither shows you the misconception."
    - "A recall question: it can't bring out what students think causes seasons."
    - "Students draw without committing to a cause, so the misconception stays hidden (though the drawing might hint at it)."
  explain: "Students who think distance causes seasons will say summer. It's winter in London: closest to the Sun, and cold. The misconception gives a definite wrong answer, and the real answer contradicts it."
```

Rewriting the example lesson's opening, one step at a time:

1. **The misconception:** summer is when the Earth is closer to the Sun.
2. **A question where it gives a definite answer:** the Earth is closest to the Sun in early January. If distance caused seasons, January would be summer everywhere.
3. **Make it bite:** ask about the other hemisphere. In Australia, January *is* summer, so asking about Sydney would seem to confirm the misconception. Ask about London instead: it's winter there.
4. **What students see after locking in:** "Winter. In January the Earth is at its closest to the Sun, but it's winter in the north. Distance can't be the cause."

The site's own seasons lesson, [Why is summer hot?](../../../science/earth-7/seasons/), opens the same way and then lets students test it on a model of the Earth.

## Check

```step
stage: practise
insight: "The same move works in any subject: find the wrong idea, then the question where it gives a definite answer."
check:
  type: question
  q: "Many students believe multiplying always makes a number bigger. Which opening question brings that out?"
  options:
    - "Without working it out: which is bigger, 8 × 0.5 or 8?"
    - "What is 8 × 0.5?"
    - "Write the rule for multiplying decimals."
    - "Is multiplying the same as repeated adding?"
  answer: 0
  feedback:
    - null
    - "Students who can calculate get 4 without facing what they believe. The comparison makes them commit first."
    - "Recall: it doesn't ask students what they think happens."
    - "A fair discussion question, but it doesn't make the misconception give a definite wrong answer."
  explain: "A student who believes multiplying makes bigger picks 8 × 0.5, commits, and then sees it's 4: half of 8."
```

## Apply

```step
stage: make
insight: "Writing the misconception and the opening move down, side by side, shows whether the question really targets the mix-up."
check:
  type: reasoning
  q: "For your primer, write the misconception (as a student would say it) and your opening question with its options."
  placeholder: "Misconception: … Opening question: … Options: …"
  models:
    - title: "History (lesson 1's primer)"
      text: "Misconception: the people in a source made it (the soldiers in a photo made the photo). Opening question: A photograph shows soldiers marching through a city. Who made this source? Options: The soldiers / Whoever took the photograph / You can't tell from the picture alone / The city council."
  criteria:
    - "The misconception is worded the way a student would say it."
    - "A student could answer the question before being taught."
    - "One option is the misconception, and the real answer clearly contradicts it."
  explain: "If no option is the misconception, the question can't bring it out. If the real answer could fit the misconception too (as Sydney in January would), choose a different case."
```
