## The idea in plain words

A computer's **specifications** say how much of each part it has. The **processor** works through instructions: its speed (GHz) is how many billion steps each core takes a second, and its **cores** are how many things it can work on at once. **RAM** is how much it can work on at once; it empties when the power goes off. **Storage** keeps everything, even with the power off: its size (GB, TB) is how much it holds, and its type sets how fast it reads (an SSD is far faster than a hard disk, HDD). **Network** bandwidth (Mb/s) is how much data can arrive each second. A **graphics card** has thousands of small processors that do the same simple sum at once, for pictures, games and AI.

A job is only as fast as its slowest part, the **bottleneck**. Upgrading anything else barely helps. Different jobs have different bottlenecks, so choosing hardware starts from the job: what does it need most, and what can you afford?

## The kitchen

Processor = the chefs (how fast, how many). RAM = the bench (what you are working with now). Storage = the pantry (how much it holds, how far away). Network = the delivery van. Graphics card = a team of helpers who all chop at once.

## The model on the board

Times add up part by part, and when RAM is too small the computer swaps to storage (slow on a hard disk). Real computers overlap these steps and have caches and other tricks; the board keeps one idea: the slowest part decides.

## What students get wrong

- **RAM and storage are the same thing** ("my phone has 256 GB of memory"). RAM is working space; storage is what it keeps.
- **A bigger drive is a faster drive.** Size is capacity; the type (SSD or HDD) is speed.
- **A faster processor fixes everything.** Only if the processor is the bottleneck.
- **The biggest numbers are the best buy.** The best choice depends on the job and the budget.
