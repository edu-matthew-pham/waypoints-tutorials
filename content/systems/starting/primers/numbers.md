## The idea in plain words

A computer stores everything as **numbers**. Each letter has a number, its **code**: capital A is 65, B is 66, and the codes go up in order; small a is 97. A word is a list of codes, one per character, and a space and a digit have codes too (space is 32, the character 7 is 55).

**Text and numbers do different jobs.** Stored as numbers, 12 and 3 can be added (15). Stored as text, they can only be joined (123). That's why a form treats a phone number as text: nobody adds phone numbers, and a leading 0 must stay.

Underneath, every number is stored with **switches** that are only on (1) or off (0). One switch makes 2 patterns, two make 4, three make 8: each extra switch doubles the patterns. The **dot cards** (8, 4, 2, 1 dots) turn a pattern into a number: add the dots on the cards that are face up. With seven cards (64 down to 1), 65 is the 64 card and the 1 card: the letter A.

## Why it matters

This is the primary half of the Digital Technologies Knowledge and understanding strand (AC9TDI6K03 and 6K04). Every Years 7–8 lesson on data assumes it: Everything is numbers, Text as numbers, Bits and binary numbers. Coverage in primary school varies, so check before you start Year 7 work.

## Teaching it

- Real dot cards first (CS Unplugged's binary cards: free to print); the board is the same cards.
- Keep the words plain: "on or off", "face up", "dots", "code". The words bit, binary and byte come in Year 7; don't hold students back from using them, but nothing here needs them.
- Year 7–8 teachers using this as catch-up: lessons 1 and 4 are the two to check. A student who can find D's code (68) and make 72 on the cards can go straight to Everything is numbers.
