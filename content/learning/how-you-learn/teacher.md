## What this course is for

An optional, discussion-led introduction to **self-regulated learning**, which students meet as **being your own learning coach**: understanding and adjusting your own thinking, motivation, feelings and actions as you learn. It's for any class, Years 6 to 8, and takes five workshops of about 40 minutes each, plus a short "did it help?" follow-up a week or two after the last one. It gives students the ideas and a shared language. The habit itself is built afterwards, in every subject, with the strategy cards and occasional prompts. See For Learning skills teachers for how to do that.

The core idea, in the students' words: **"Sometimes you choose the task, sometimes someone else does. Either way, you're your own learning coach."** Who chose the task is a feature of the task; coaching yourself through it applies either way.

## The five workshops

1. **What do you believe about learning?** Beliefs like "fast means smart" and "familiar means known", and what they make people do. Names self-regulated learning as being your own learning coach.
2. **Your learning coach.** Who chose the task versus who coaches (always you); the coach's three jobs (plan, check, look back), feelings included; thinking out loud as the coach's voice.
3. **Why do you do it?** Reasons that push you and reasons you own; the goals grid (getting better, not slipping back, looking good, not looking bad); marks as the scoreboard; beating your own best and goals about what you'll do.
4. **When easy fools you.** Familiar isn't known; why testing yourself, spreading practice and explaining usually work better than rereading.
5. **Knowing it vs using it.** One strategy taught with NEMO-T; having a strategy versus using it; the dip when a new strategy doesn't help yet; an if-then plan for a real task in another subject.

## How the workshops run

- **Primers, loosely explicit teaching.** Each workshop is a `primer`, the site's own lesson shape (Think first, The idea, Check, Apply, Vary, Try), using only the stages it needs, in the order it needs them. Primer isn't a named research model. Its steps are consistent with explicit teaching: draw out what students already think, explain with a worked example, check, then apply it to real content (Bransford; Rosenshine; Sweller; the EEF). The discussions and self-reflection in the class plans add the inquiry part.
- **Openers vary.** Workshop 1 starts with a position on belief statements, 2 and 4 with a prediction about made-up learners, 3 with a made-up learner's reasons, and 5 with a task before the strategy is named.
- **Discussion carries it.** Every class plan has a discussion or debate; several questions have more than one good answer.
- **Nib makes the mistakes.** Nib, the site's novice, makes one slip per workshop for students to find.
- **The last workshop is the transfer,** and the follow-up matters more than anything in the lessons.

## Personal reflection

Students are encouraged to apply each idea to themselves, and to **share only what they're comfortable with**. Discussion centres on made-up learners, the way pastoral lessons on bullying do. The personal parts are private: a think, a tick, or a writing box that stays in the student's browser. **Nothing is collected or marked**, and no step needs a personal answer to be finished. If something a student says suggests real distress (about marks, pressure at home, feeling not good enough), follow your school's wellbeing process.

## Grades

Workshop 3 names marks openly: they pull on how students study, and they're the scoreboard, worth knowing but not the same as getting better. Students in a grade-driven system are self-regulating, often toward the grade (doing the moves for the teacher rather than for themselves).

Your school may want to assess self-regulated learning alongside the subject. If so, keep it **formative**: self-assessment, learning journals, think-alouds, conferences about goals, or a school-level diagnostic. Marking it tends to invite surface compliance, where students perform the moves for the mark.

## The research behind it

**The spine is Shyam Barr's work** (*Educate to Self-Regulate*, 2024, his blog and podcast): his definition of self-regulated learning (thinking, motivation, emotions and behaviour); NEMO-T for teaching a strategy (Name, Explain, Model, Opportunity, Transfer); having a strategy versus using it (availability and production deficiencies: a production gap needs prompts, not reteaching); strategy stripping (teaching a strategy without its how and why); surface compliance versus genuine self-regulated learning; mastery versus performance goals, growth goals, process before outcome goals, and task value; the teacher shifting to coaching and fading; SEE (Setting, Exchanges, Events: where support for self-regulation sits in a classroom); and learning journals and think-alouds for seeing it. The five workshops follow five lenses on what students need to know about self-regulated learning: what it is, beliefs about learning, motivation and goals, strategy knowledge, and strategy use.

**Other sources fill gaps,** under their own names:

- **Who chose the task:** self-directed versus externally directed learning (Loyens, Magda and Rikers 2008; Jossberger and colleagues 2010; Knowles).
- **The learning coach** is the site's own analogy, after self-instructional training (Meichenbaum and Goodman 1971) and cognitive apprenticeship (Collins, Brown and Newman 1989).
- **The cycle:** Zimmerman (forethought, performance, self-reflection), which the EEF simplifies as plan, monitor and evaluate. The EEF also finds these strategies work best taught explicitly and applied to normal subject content: that's why this course is short and optional, and the cards are the part to keep using.
- **Beliefs:** Schommer; Bjork. Growth mindset with caution: large reviews find small average effects (Sisk and colleagues 2018; Yeager and colleagues 2019).
- **Motivation and goals:** the motivation continuum in self-determination theory (Ryan and Deci; Vansteenkiste and colleagues 2009 on mixes); the achievement goals 2×2 (Elliot and McGregor 2001); expectancy, value and cost (Eccles); criteria compliance (Torrance).
- **Study and strategies:** illusions of competence and desirable difficulties (Bjork); Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) on study techniques; what, how, when and why knowledge (Paris, Lipson and Wixson; Schraw); utilisation deficiency, the dip (Miller); if-then plans (Gollwitzer).

The lessons simplify these ideas for Years 6 to 8 and say "usually" and "often" where the evidence has limits.
