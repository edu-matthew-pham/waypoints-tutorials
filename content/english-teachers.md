## What is here

**This is a pilot.** Three courses are built. The first is [Holding writing together](../english/cohesion/): nine lessons on cohesion for Years 6 to 8, not yet tried with a class. It follows the cohesion thread of the Language strand through the years:

- **Warm-up (optional, Years 4–5 ideas):** pointing words such as it, they and this (AC9E4LA04), joining words (AC9E4LA04) and where a sentence starts (AC9E5LA04). Skip these with a class that's secure.
- **Words that hold it together:** repetition on purpose and word chains (AC9E6LA04).
- **Guiding the reader:** topic sentences, overviews and conclusions (AC9E7LA04).
- **Inside a paragraph:** claim, evidence and explanation (AC9E8LA04).
- **Challenge:** repair a report that has the right facts but is hard to follow.

The second course is [How texts are built](../english/texts/): nine lessons on text structure for Years 6 to 8, also not yet tried with a class.

- **Warm-up (optional, Years 4–5 ideas):** a recount's stages, and features that fit imaginative, informative and persuasive texts, including reports versus explanations (AC9E4LA03, AC9E4LY03, AC9E5LY03).
- **Stages and phases:** explanations and reviews, and how purpose decides the stages and the language (AC9E5LA03, AC9E6LA03, AC9E7LA03).
- **Organising ideas:** cause and effect, compare and contrast, problem and solution, claim and support, and what a structure puts first or leaves out (AC9E7LY04, AC9E8LY04).
- **Built for a reader:** structure and language chosen for an audience, and hybrid texts (AC9E6LY03, AC9E8LA03).
- **Challenge:** build an argument, following the genre teaching and learning cycle (take a model apart, build one together, then write).

The third course is [Building sentences](../english/sentences/): eleven lessons on how sentences are built, for Years 6 to 8, also not yet tried with a class. Grammar is taught in use, so each lesson ends in a short piece of a real text type (a match report, a note home, a notice, a diary entry, a review).

- **Warm-up (optional, Years 4–5 ideas):** one main idea in a sentence (AC9E4LA06), direct and reported speech (AC9E4LA07), what a dependent clause adds (AC9E5LA05) and noun groups (AC9E5LA06).
- **Detail:** a clause inside a clause (AC9E6LA05) and precise verbs, tense and adverb groups (AC9E6LA06).
- **Shapes:** simple, compound, complex and compound-complex sentences, and choosing the shape for the job (AC9E7LA05), steady tense (AC9E7LA06), and embedded clauses that say more in fewer words (AC9E8LA05).
- **Challenge:** rework a choppy report by folding, steadying the tense and choosing shapes.

The board is a small sentence on paper: students click tiles into order, pick the mark for each slot, or write in a text box that shows rough counts (words, sentences, joining words, who or which). The counts are aids. They never say whether the writing is good; a partner or you do that, and each step says so. The plain idea is taught first and the term (such as embedded clause) is named once, with ACARA's term in the teacher notes. Where an answer is defensible, the board marks it correct or leaves the item out.

The Years 9–10 text-structure descriptors (judging when breaking a pattern works) are left for later Years 9–10 work rather than added as harder steps. The optional "wider" steps add other contexts at the same level.

We'd like colleagues to try these with a class before we build more, so please tell us what worked and what didn't.

## How it teaches

- **A text to mark up.** Each lesson has a short text on the screen. Students draw arrows from a pointing word back to what it points to, colour word chains, choose joining words in gaps, and label or reorder sentences and paragraphs.
- **One move per lesson**, then the same move in their own writing on the board, then (optionally) in a piece of class writing.
- **No AI marking, anywhere.** The board checks only what can be checked: where a planted word points, which joining word does the right job, which sentence is the topic sentence, whether the paragraphs follow the overview's order. Students' own writing is checked by them against a model, by a partner, or by you. The board says so on those steps.
- **Every text is written for the lesson.** Nothing is copyrighted, so the texts can be quoted, projected and printed, and every planted link is exactly where the lesson needs it.

## What it isn't

The lessons are for introducing a move or revisiting one. They sit beside the reading, writing and talk in your class, not in place of it. They don't cover interpreting a whole literary text, speaking and listening, or sustained drafting over weeks: those need discussion and your feedback.

## What is planned

These are plans, not promises. We'll propose each one for review before building it.

1. **Your own text on the board.** The boards already treat text as data. Next, a teacher could paste a passage from the class novel and students could trace and colour it (self-checked, since the board can't know the answers for a text it hasn't seen). Using the passage would fall under your school's own copyright arrangements; the site would store nothing.
2. **More Language strand courses:** How sentences work, and Choosing words.
3. **More of How texts work:** images and multimodal texts, and literary ideas with short public-domain extracts.
4. **An open-response mode**, still with no AI marking: students place their answer among model answers of different quality, tick criteria and highlight their evidence, or take it to class discussion.

## For you

Each lesson's **Teacher guide** link is at the bottom of the course page, with goals, what to say, where students get stuck and an exit ticket. Most schools teach a paragraph frame such as TEEL or PEEL: the lessons use plain labels (claim, example, quotation, explanation) that map onto any frame, so use yours in class.
