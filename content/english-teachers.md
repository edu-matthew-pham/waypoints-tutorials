## What is here

To see where a course sits across Foundation to Year 10, read [How English skills grow](../teach/english/threads/): each thread, what ACARA intends at each stage in plain words, and the lessons here that practise it.

**This is a pilot.** Seven courses are built. The first is [Holding writing together](../english/cohesion/): nine lessons on cohesion for Years 6 to 8, not yet tried with a class. It follows the cohesion thread of the Language strand through the years:

- **Warm-up (optional, Years 4–5 ideas):** pointing words such as it, they and this (AC9E4LA04), joining words (AC9E4LA04) and where a sentence starts (AC9E5LA04). Skip these with a class that's secure.
- **Words that hold it together:** repetition on purpose and word chains (AC9E6LA04).
- **Guiding the reader:** topic sentences, overviews and conclusions (AC9E7LA04).
- **Inside a paragraph:** claim, evidence and explanation (AC9E8LA04).
- **Challenge:** repair a report that has the right facts but is hard to follow.

The second course is [How texts are built](../english/texts/): nine lessons on text structure for Years 6 to 8, also not yet tried with a class.

- **Warm-up (optional, Years 4–5 ideas):** a recount's stages, and features that fit imaginative, informative and persuasive texts, including reports versus explanations (AC9E4LA03, AC9E4LY03, AC9E5LY03).
- **Stages and phases:** explanations and reviews, and how purpose decides the stages and the language (AC9E5LA03, AC9E6LA03, AC9E7LA03).
- **Organising ideas:** cause and effect, compare and contrast, problem and solution, claim and support, and what a structure puts first or leaves out (AC9E7LY04, AC9E8LY04).
- **Built for a reader:** structure and language chosen for an audience, and hybrid texts (AC9E6LY03, AC9E8LA03).
- **Challenge:** build an argument, following the genre teaching and learning cycle (take a model apart, build one together, then write).

The third course is [Building sentences](../english/sentences/): eleven lessons on how sentences are built, for Years 6 to 8, also not yet tried with a class. Grammar is taught in use, so each lesson ends in a short piece of a real text type (a match report, a note home, a notice, a diary entry, a review).

- **Warm-up (optional, Years 4–5 ideas):** one main idea in a sentence (AC9E4LA06), direct and reported speech (AC9E4LA07), what a dependent clause adds (AC9E5LA05) and noun groups (AC9E5LA06).
- **Detail:** a clause inside a clause (AC9E6LA05) and precise verbs, tense and adverb groups (AC9E6LA06).
- **Shapes:** simple, compound, complex and compound-complex sentences, and choosing the shape for the job (AC9E7LA05), steady tense (AC9E7LA06), and embedded clauses that say more in fewer words (AC9E8LA05).
- **Challenge:** rework a choppy report by folding, steadying the tense and choosing shapes.

The board is a small sentence on paper: students click tiles into order or pick the mark for each slot. Nothing on the site takes or checks a student's own writing. Each Make step gives the brief, the criteria and an example, the student writes in their own document (Google Docs or OneNote), and a partner or you read it. The plain idea is taught first and the term (such as embedded clause) is named once, with ACARA's term in the teacher notes. Where an answer is defensible, the board marks it correct or leaves the item out.

The fourth course is [Punctuation for meaning](../english/punctuation/): six lessons for Years 6 to 8, also not yet tried with a class.

- **Warm-up (optional, Years 3–5 ideas):** apostrophes and speech marks (AC9E3LA11, AC9E4LA12), and commas after opening phrases and apostrophes for shared owners (AC9E5LA09).
- **Marks that change meaning:** the three jobs of a comma (AC9E6LA09), colons and brackets (AC9E7LA09), semicolons and dashes (AC9E8LA09).
- **Challenge:** punctuate a school newsletter that has no punctuation, then check the commas in a piece of their own writing.

Students choose a mark for each slot in a sentence, so every mark is tied to a job in the sentence. The board only marks choices with one defensible answer. Dashes, brackets and the comma before the last *and* of a list are style choices, so the board leaves them out and a partner or you judge them in the student's own writing.

The fifth course is [Language for people and situations](../english/words/): eight lessons for Years 6 to 8, not yet tried with a class.

- **Warm-up (optional, Years 4–5 ideas):** the same message in different words (AC9E4LA01), shades of meaning (AC9E4LA11, AC9E5LA08), and words that signal roles (AC9E5LA01).
- **Core:** slang to formal, polite is not formal (AC9E6LA01); familiarity and power (AC9E6LA01); how words show who we are (AC9E7LA01); how words build roles and include or leave out a newcomer (AC9E8LA01).
- **Challenge:** rewrite a notice that has the right news in the wrong voice.

Students place words on a scale or choose a word in a sentence and see where it sits. All examples are made up; no student is asked about their own family, language or background. The cultural-conventions nodes (AC9E4LA01 n4, AC9E5LA01 n3) are left to class with your school's local guidance, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander education staff or community members, and AIATSIS; the site does not supply protocols. The board only marks choices with one defensible answer, accepting two bands where a careful student could argue either.

The sixth course is [Judging and persuading](../english/persuade/): nine lessons for Years 6 to 8, not yet tried with a class.

- **Warm-up (optional, Years 4–5 ideas):** fact and opinion words (AC9E4LA02), how sure a claim sounds and what backs it up (AC9E5LA02 n1–n2), and who says so (AC9E5LA02 n3).
- **Core:** emotive words and bias (AC9E6LA02), judging and backing it up (AC9E7LA02), language features that shape meaning (AC9E7LY03), and irony, exaggeration and understatement (AC9E8LA02).
- **Challenge:** judge a made-up film review for its audience and sources, and write a verdict (AC9E8LY03).

Every text is made up and low-stakes (a phone rule, a film, a canteen); no real, contested topics. The board labels, colours and places planted words; the student's own writing stays in their own document, and a partner or you judge whether the evidence is good, whether a word takes a side and which device was used.

The seventh course is [Speaking to an audience](../english/speaking/): nine lessons for Years 6 to 8, not yet tried with a class.

- **Warm-up (optional, Years 4–5 ideas):** planning a talk with an aim, an opening, points, details and a close (AC9E4LY07 n1, AC9E5LY07 n1), and writing a script to be heard (AC9E5LY07 n2).
- **Prepare:** cutting a script to cue cards on a new board, and choosing a register and a stance for the listeners (AC9E6LY07 n1, AC9E4LY07 n1, AC9E8LY07 n1). The register lesson is the required step for AC9E8LY07 n1.
- **Voice, visuals and feedback:** using pace, volume, pitch and tone on purpose (AC9E5LY07 n3, AC9E6LY07 n2, AC9E7LY07 n2), visuals that add something (AC9E5LY07 n4), judging a spoken text from its transcript (AC9E7LY02 n2), and rehearsing with a partner and changing one thing (AC9E4LY07 n2, AC9E8LY07 n2).
- **Challenge:** prepare a one-minute talk for the class, then give it (AC9E4LY07 n3, AC9E6LY07 n3, AC9E8LY07 n2).

**The site plans, scripts and checks. Rehearsing and presenting happen in class.** Nothing on the site listens to a talk, records a voice or judges how anyone sounds. For the nodes about delivering and rehearsing, the site covers the preparing and the self-check, and you tick the criteria from what you see and hear in class (each lesson's teacher notes say which). Voice and spoken texts are judged from made-up transcripts, where pace, pitch and volume are only on the page when someone has marked them. Peer feedback is a short checklist in the rehearsal lesson for now; a fuller card is planned.

The cue board shows a script in sections. Students click the key words they would need and see one card per section, with a word count and the key words as a share of the script. **Print cue cards** makes index cards two across a page, and **Print highlighted script** prints the whole script with the key words marked. The board counts; it never says whether the words are the right ones. A student tests that by covering the script and speaking.

The Years 9–10 text-structure descriptors (judging when breaking a pattern works) are left for later Years 9–10 work rather than added as harder steps. The optional "wider" steps add other contexts at the same level.

We'd like colleagues to try these with a class before we build more, so please tell us what worked and what didn't.

## How it teaches

- **A text to mark up.** Each lesson has a short text on the screen. Students draw arrows from a pointing word back to what it points to, colour word chains, choose joining words in gaps, and label or reorder sentences and paragraphs.
- **One move per lesson**, then the same move in their own writing in their own document (the Make step is the direction), then (optionally) in a piece of class writing.
- **No AI marking, anywhere.** The board checks only what can be checked: where a planted word points, which joining word does the right job, which sentence is the topic sentence, whether the paragraphs follow the overview's order. Students' own writing lives in their own document and is checked by them against a model, by a partner, or by you. Each Make step says so.
- **Every text is written for the lesson.** Nothing is copyrighted, so the texts can be quoted, projected and printed, and every planted link is exactly where the lesson needs it.

## How you might use these

You know your units and your classes, so treat this as a few ideas, not a plan. Each lesson teaches one move on its own, so you can use it wherever it helps: to introduce an idea, to revisit it, or as a short task inside a unit you're already teaching (word chains while students redraft a persuasive piece, say). How you weave it in is your call.

*Judging and persuading* and *Speaking to an audience* lean towards reading and presenting whole texts, so they may sit most naturally alongside a unit on that, with a text or task of your own. We'd be glad to hear how you actually use them.

## What it isn't

The lessons are for introducing a move or revisiting one. They sit beside the reading, writing and talk in your class, not in place of it. They don't cover interpreting a whole literary text, delivering a talk (the site prepares it; you hear it), discussion, or sustained drafting over weeks: those need discussion and your feedback.

## What is planned

These are plans, not promises. We'll propose each one for review before building it.

1. **More Language strand lessons:** images and layout, and subject vocabulary and nominalisation (Years 7 and 8).
2. **A worked unit,** perhaps starting with a persuasive composition unit: one example of how the lessons, a text or task and the curriculum could fit together, with a prompt to adapt it to your own text or theme. It would be an illustration to change, not a script. A text-based unit might follow.
3. **Later, only if teachers ask:** your own passage on the board (self-checked; your school's copyright arrangements would apply) and an open-response mode with no AI marking.

## For you

Each lesson's **Teacher guide** link is at the bottom of the course page, with goals, what to say, where students get stuck and an exit ticket. Most schools teach a paragraph frame such as TEEL or PEEL: the lessons use plain labels (claim, example, quotation, explanation) that map onto any frame, so use yours in class.
