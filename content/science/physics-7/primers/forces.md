## The idea in plain words

A **force** is a push or a pull. It has a **size** (in newtons, N) and a **direction**, so we draw it as an **arrow**: longer arrow, bigger force. A **force diagram** shows every force on one object, each named (weight, support, friction, push, air resistance…).

- **Balanced** forces cancel out: the **net force** is zero. Balanced does **not** mean no forces. An object with balanced forces keeps doing what it was doing: stays still, or keeps moving at a steady speed in a straight line.
- **Unbalanced** forces (a net force) change the motion: speeding up, slowing down or changing direction, in the direction of the net force.
- **Mass** (kg) is how much matter is in an object; it is the same everywhere. **Weight** (N) is the force of gravity on that mass: weight = mass × g, where g is about 9.8 N/kg on Earth (often rounded to 10), 1.6 N/kg on the Moon.

## Where the picture breaks

- The board's object moves left and right only; up-and-down forces are shown but not run.
- Friction is a fixed size once something slides; real friction changes a little with speed, and air resistance grows a lot.
- On the board, arrows start at the object's centre (the usual convention for a force diagram); real forces act at surfaces or through the whole object.

## Common misconceptions

- A moving object must have a force pushing it forward (constant speed needs balanced forces, not a forward force).
- Balanced means no forces.
- Heavier objects fall faster (without air resistance, all fall together).
- Mass and weight are the same thing; weight is measured in kilograms.

## Class task: force, mass and motion (node 5)

Node 5 brings the unit together, so it is a class task, not a tutorial. The checkpoint: students can say which of two objects speeds up more under the same force, and read the pattern in force data.

**Investigation (about 30 minutes).** Pull a trolley along the bench with a stretched rubber band (one band, then two side by side) and time it over 1 m from rest; then add a mass to the trolley and repeat. Keep the surface, distance and starting point the same. Groups record the time for each run and say what changed.

**Predict first.** "The same rubber band pulls an empty trolley and a loaded one. Which speeds up more?" (The empty one: same force, less mass, bigger change in motion.)

**Data to read** (example figures, not real measurements): time to roll 1 m from rest.

| Trolley | One band | Two bands |
|---|---|---|
| Empty (0.5 kg) | 1.4 s | 1.0 s |
| Loaded (1.0 kg) | 2.0 s | 1.4 s |

Questions: Which pull changed the motion most quickly, and how can you tell? What did adding mass do? Which two runs are the fairest test of mass alone? (The same column.) Why do two bands on the loaded trolley match one band on the empty one?

**Success criteria (from the progression):** states the relationship in words (a bigger unbalanced force gives a bigger change in motion; a bigger mass gives a smaller change for the same force); predicts which object speeds up more under the same force; identifies the pattern in data. Keep it qualitative: F = ma waits for Year 10.

**Watch for:** "heavier objects need a force to keep moving" (from node 2), and mixing up mass and weight (node 4) when students explain why the loaded trolley is slower.

**On screen:** the short tutorial *Same push, different mass* covers node 5 (the qualitative relationship and the data pattern); the investigation above still comes first.

## Investigate in class

Optional ideas if you run a practical for this unit: pick one, adapt it to your equipment, or use your own.

- **Does the surface change how hard you must pull to slide a block?** Change: the surface (bench, carpet, sandpaper, towel). Measure: the force meter reading while the block slides steadily. Keep the same: the block, a level pull. *(Average three pulls.)*
- **Does doubling the mass double the weight?** Change: the number of 100 g masses on a force meter (one to five). Measure: weight in newtons. Keep the same: the meter, zeroed first. *(Expect about 1 N per 100 g; the gradient of the graph is g.)*
- **Does a heavier object fall faster?** Change: how much sand is in a sealed container (empty, half, full). Measure: which lands first when two are dropped together from 2 m. Keep the same: containers, height. *(Listen for one thud or two.)*

**Safety and ethics:** keep feet clear of falling masses, and drop from standing height, not from chairs or desks.
