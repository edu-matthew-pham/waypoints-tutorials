title: Pure substances and mixtures in 5 minutes

## The idea in plain words

A **pure substance** contains only **one kind of particle** (distilled water, copper, sugar). A **mixture** contains two or more kinds (salt water, air, milk). You cannot tell by looking: salt water looks like one substance but is a mixture.

A mixture can be **separated** because its parts differ in a **physical property**:

| Property difference | Technique |
|---|---|
| Magnetism | Magnet |
| Particle size | Sieving, filtration |
| Boiling point | Evaporation (keep the solid), distillation (keep the liquid) |
| Density | Let it settle, pour off the top |
| How each substance dissolves and sticks | Chromatography |

Separating does not change the particles: you get back the same substances.

## Why it matters

Water treatment, recycling, mining and food processing all rely on these property differences. Choosing a technique means naming the difference first.

## Where the picture breaks

- "Pure" on a food label means nothing added, not one kind of particle.
- Here "one kind of particle" covers both elements and compounds (water is one kind of particle). Atoms and compounds come in Year 8.
- Salt dissolves as charged particles; the tutorials draw it as one kind of particle.

## Common misconceptions

- Clear or even-looking means pure.
- A dissolved substance has disappeared.
- Distillation is the same as evaporation.
- Dyes separate by weight in chromatography.
