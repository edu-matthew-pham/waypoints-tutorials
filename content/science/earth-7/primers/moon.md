## The idea in plain words

- The sun always lights **half** of the moon. As the moon goes round the Earth (about 29.5 days from new moon to new moon), we see different amounts of that lit half: the **phases**. They are not caused by Earth's shadow.
- Order: new moon, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full moon, waning gibbous, last quarter, waning crescent. From Australia a waxing moon is lit on the **left**; from the Northern Hemisphere, on the right.
- **Solar eclipse:** at new moon, the moon is between the sun and Earth and its shadow falls on part of Earth. **Lunar eclipse:** at full moon, the Earth is between the sun and moon and the moon passes through Earth's shadow. The **umbra** is the dark central shadow (total eclipse); the **penumbra** is the outer partial shadow.
- Eclipses don't happen every month because the moon's orbit is **tilted about 5°**: most new and full moons pass above or below the line to the sun. Eclipses happen only in "eclipse seasons" about every six months, when the line where the orbits cross points at the sun.
- **Tides:** the moon's gravity pulls the oceans into two bulges (one facing the moon, one on the far side); the Earth spins through both, giving most coasts **two high tides a day**. The sun adds a smaller effect: **spring tides** (biggest range) at new and full moon when the sun and moon line up; **neap tides** (smallest range) at the quarters.

## Where the picture breaks

- Distances and sizes are not to scale; in the eclipse side view the tilt and the shadows are exaggerated so that they can be seen.
- The ocean bulges are hugely exaggerated, and real tides are shaped by coastlines and seafloor (some places have one tide a day).
- The board's tide chart is for an idealised coast: real tide tables also vary with the moon's distance.

## Common misconceptions

- Phases are caused by Earth's shadow.
- A lunar eclipse is just a new moon (or a moon phase).
- Eclipses should happen every month.
- There is one tidal bulge, under the moon, so one high tide a day.
- "Spring tides" happen in spring.

## Investigate in class

Optional ideas if you run a practical for this unit: pick one, adapt it to your equipment, or use your own.

- **Does where the moon is in its orbit change how much of its lit half we see?** A model in a dark room: a lamp (sun), a ball on a pencil (moon), a student's head (Earth). Change: the ball's position around the head (eight places). Record: the lit shape seen. Keep the same: the ball's height.
- **Does the moon's shape change over two weeks?** Change: the date. Record: the shape and which side is lit. Keep the same: the time of night and the place you look from. *(A home task.)*
- **Is the tidal range bigger at full and new moon?** Secondary data: Bureau of Meteorology tide predictions for a nearby port. Change: the day of the month. Measure: high tide minus low tide. Keep the same: the port.

**Safety and ethics:** never look straight at the sun when spotting the moon by day; the lamp gets hot.
