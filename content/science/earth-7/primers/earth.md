## The idea in plain words

- **Rotation:** the Earth spins on its axis once a day (anticlockwise seen from above the North Pole), so the sun rises in the east and sets in the west. Day is the half facing the sun.
- **Revolution:** the Earth goes round the sun once a year (about 365¼ days), spinning about 365 times on the way.
- **Tilt:** the axis is tilted 23.4° and keeps pointing the same way in space all year (towards the Pole Star). So for half the year the Southern Hemisphere leans towards the sun, and for the other half it leans away.
- **Seasons** come from the tilt, not from distance. Leaning towards the sun means the sun is higher at noon (its light is concentrated on a smaller area) and the days are longer: summer. The Earth is actually *closest* to the sun in early January, in Australia's summer and the Northern Hemisphere's winter.
- The two hemispheres have **opposite seasons** at the same time. Near the equator, day length and the noon sun change little, so there is no strong summer and winter.

## Where the picture breaks

- Sizes and distances on the board are not to scale: the sun is about 109 times wider than the Earth and about 12 000 Earth-widths away.
- The side view shows only the tilt towards or away from the sun; the rest of the tilt is towards or away from you.
- Day lengths ignore the bending of light by the air, so real days are a few minutes longer.

## Common misconceptions

- Seasons happen because the Earth is closer to the sun in summer.
- The sun goes round the Earth each day, or the Earth goes round the sun once a day.
- The axis wobbles towards the sun in summer (it doesn't change direction; the Earth moves to the other side of the sun).
- Summer days are hotter because the sun is "bigger" or "stronger".
