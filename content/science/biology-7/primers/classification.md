## The idea in plain words

Scientists sort living things into groups by **features they can observe**. The biggest groups are **kingdoms**. A common five-kingdom scheme for Year 7:

| Kingdom | Observable features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Animals | many cells; move; eat other living things | kangaroo, bull ant, sea star |
| Plants | many cells; make their own food from sunlight (green) | gum tree, moss, grass |
| Fungi | absorb food from dead or living material; grow from spores; not green | mushroom, bread mould, yeast |
| Protists | mostly one cell, with a nucleus | amoeba, paramecium, Euglena |
| Bacteria | one tiny cell, no nucleus | yoghurt bacteria, E. coli |

A **dichotomous key** identifies an organism by a series of two-way choices ("Does it have a backbone?"), each about an **observable feature**. You follow the choices that match the specimen until the key names it. One wrong choice leads to a wrong name, so each step needs checking against the specimen.

## Where the picture breaks

- Kingdoms sort by features, not relatedness, and are not the endpoint of classification. Schemes change as evidence changes (node 5): two kingdoms, then five, and now three domains (bacteria, archaea, eukaryotes) from DNA evidence. Protists are not one natural group, and fungi are closer relatives of animals than of plants. The Kingdoms lesson makes both points explicit.
- Some organisms sit awkwardly: Euglena moves like an animal and makes food like a plant.
- The key tool shows specimens as written descriptions; with real specimens or photos, students must observe the features themselves.

## Common misconceptions

- Fungi are plants (they are not green and can't make their own food).
- Organisms in the same kingdom must be close relatives (kingdoms group by features; relatedness comes from shared ancestors).
- Only animals are living things that move; bacteria aren't living.
- Keys are about names, not features (students guess the animal, then follow the key backwards).

## Why classify (a short opening lesson)

Classification helps us identify organisms, communicate with one shared name and see relationships. It groups by shared features (unlike a list), and every system is built for a purpose, including First Peoples' systems tied to Country.

## Investigate in class

Optional ideas if you run a practical for this unit: pick one, adapt it to your equipment, or use your own.

- **Does a key with measurable features give more correct names than one with vague words?** Change: the key's wording ("legs: 6 or 8" or "big or small"). Measure: correct identifications out of 10 specimens. Keep the same: specimens, time, group size. *(Photos or school-ground leaves work well.)*
- **Does a hand lens change how many features we can use to tell leaves apart?** Change: eye alone or a hand lens. Measure: features recorded per leaf. Keep the same: leaves, time.
- **Does bread mould need light to grow, like a plant?** Change: light or dark cupboard. Measure: squares of a grid covered after a week. Keep the same: bread, water added, temperature. *(Set up in one lesson, check a week later.)*

**Safety and ethics:** seal mould bags with tape and never open them (spores can trigger asthma and allergies); dispose of them sealed. Collect fallen leaves only, from school grounds.
