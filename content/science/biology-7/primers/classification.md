## The idea in plain words

Scientists sort living things into groups by **features they can observe**. The biggest groups are **kingdoms**. A common five-kingdom scheme for Year 7:

| Kingdom | Observable features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Animals | many cells; move; eat other living things | kangaroo, bull ant, sea star |
| Plants | many cells; make their own food from sunlight (green) | gum tree, moss, grass |
| Fungi | absorb food from dead or living material; grow from spores; not green | mushroom, bread mould, yeast |
| Protists | mostly one cell, with a nucleus | amoeba, paramecium, Euglena |
| Bacteria | one tiny cell, no nucleus | yoghurt bacteria, E. coli |

A **dichotomous key** identifies an organism by a series of two-way choices ("Does it have a backbone?"), each about an **observable feature**. You follow the choices that match the specimen until the key names it. One wrong choice leads to a wrong name, so each step needs checking against the specimen.

## Where the picture breaks

- Classification schemes change as evidence changes (node 5). Many scientists now use three domains above kingdoms, and protists are not one natural group.
- Some organisms sit awkwardly: Euglena moves like an animal and makes food like a plant.
- The key tool shows specimens as written descriptions; with real specimens or photos, students must observe the features themselves.

## Common misconceptions

- Fungi are plants (they are not green and can't make their own food).
- Only animals are living things that move; bacteria aren't living.
- Keys are about names, not features (students guess the animal, then follow the key backwards).
