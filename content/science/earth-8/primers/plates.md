## The idea in plain words

**Layers:** a thin solid **crust** (about 35 km under continents, 7 km under oceans), a thick solid **mantle** that flows very slowly, a liquid **outer core** and a solid **inner core** (iron and nickel). Temperature and pressure rise with depth. The **lithosphere** is the crust plus the rigid top of the mantle; below it the **asthenosphere** is solid but soft enough to flow.

Earth's outer shell, the **lithosphere** (the crust and the top of the mantle), is broken into large rigid pieces called **tectonic plates**. They move a few centimetres a year, up to about twice as fast as fingernails grow. Australia moves about 7 cm north each year.

The plates sit on the hot **mantle**. The mantle is solid rock, but it is so hot that over millions of years it flows very slowly, like extremely thick putty. Heat from the core warms the bottom of the mantle, and radioactive elements in the mantle rock heat it from within (about half of Earth's inner heat comes from radioactive decay); hot rock is less dense, so it rises; near the top it cools, becomes denser and sinks. This loop is a **convection current**, and it drags the plates along. Where currents rise, plates move apart; where they sink, plates move together.

Scientists now think two pulls also matter: old, cold plate sinking into the mantle at subduction zones drags the rest of the plate (**slab pull**), and plates slide away from high mid-ocean ridges (**ridge push**). Both are part of the same convection system.

## Where the picture breaks

- The simulation shows the mantle as moving particles, like a liquid. The real mantle is solid rock that flows over millions of years.
- In the model hot particles are pushed up directly; in reality hot rock rises because it has expanded and is less dense.
- Real convection cells are thousands of kilometres across and far less tidy than one loop.

## Common misconceptions

- The mantle is a sea of liquid magma (it is solid rock that flows very slowly; only small pockets melt).
- The plates float on a sea of liquid magma.
- Earthquakes move the plates (earthquakes are caused by plates moving).
- The continents have always been where they are.

## Investigate in class

Optional ideas if you run a practical for this unit: pick one, adapt it to your equipment, or use your own.

- **Does heating one end of a tray of water make floating "plates" move?** A model: a shallow tray of water with cork or foam pieces, a beaker of hot water under one end. Change: heated or not. Measure: the direction and distance the pieces drift in 2 minutes. Keep the same: water depth, the pieces.
- **Are earthquakes more common near plate boundaries?** Secondary data: Geoscience Australia's earthquake records. Change: the area (north of Australia near the plate edge, or the middle of the continent). Measure: the number of magnitude 4+ earthquakes in a year. Keep the same: area size, time span, magnitude.
- **Can a solid flow if you give it time?** An analogue: silly putty. Change: how fast you pull it. Measure: how far it stretches before it snaps. Keep the same: the size of the piece. *(Slow pulls stretch; sharp tugs snap.)*

**Safety and ethics:** use warm tap water, not boiling.
