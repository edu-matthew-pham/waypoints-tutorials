## The idea in plain words

The particles of Years 7 and 8 have parts. An atom has a tiny, dense **nucleus** of **protons** (charge +1, mass 1) and **neutrons** (no charge, mass 1), with **electrons** (charge −1, mass about 1/1840) around it.

- The **atomic number** is the number of protons. It decides the element.
- The **mass number** is protons plus neutrons. Neutrons = mass number − atomic number.
- A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons; an **ion** has gained or lost electrons.
- The model changed because of evidence: Thomson's electrons, Rutherford's gold foil (a tiny positive nucleus), Chadwick's neutrons.

## Where the picture breaks

- The board's Bohr model is not to scale: the nucleus would be about 100 000 times smaller than the atom.
- Electrons are drawn as dots on rings; they are better described as clouds where they are likely to be.

## Common misconceptions

- The mass number is the number of neutrons.
- Electrons carry a lot of the mass because they fill the atom.
- Adding neutrons changes the element (it makes an isotope).

## Isotopes (node 2)

Atoms of one element always have the same number of protons, but the neutrons can differ: these are **isotopes** (carbon-12, carbon-13, carbon-14). They have the same electrons, so they react alike. Some are stable; others are **unstable** (radioactive). The build board says which, for elements 1 to 20.

## Radioactive decay (node 3)

Some nuclei are unstable. They decay by giving out radiation, at random for one atom but predictably for many.

- **Alpha**: 2 protons and 2 neutrons leave (a helium nucleus). Atomic number −2, mass number −4. Stopped by paper or a few centimetres of air.
- **Beta**: a neutron becomes a proton and a fast electron, and the electron leaves. Atomic number +1, mass number the same. Stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium.
- **Gamma**: energy only, usually straight after alpha or beta. No change to either number. Reduced by thick lead or concrete.

Decay chains (node 5) are a class task, with the decay board set to `isotope: uranium-238`.

Misconceptions: gamma makes a new element; the beta electron comes from the shells; alpha is always the least dangerous (it is the most damaging inside the body).

## Half-life (node 4)

Each unstable nucleus decays at random, but a large sample halves in a fixed time, the **half-life**: 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 … Half-life is not changed by heat or chemistry. Short half-lives suit medicine (fluorine-18, about 2 hours); long ones suit dating (carbon-14, 5730 years).

Misconceptions: the sample is all gone after two half-lives; each nucleus decays at exactly the half-life; heating speeds decay up.

## Investigate in class

Optional ideas if you run a practical for this unit: pick one, adapt it to your equipment, or use your own.

These are models: school radioactive sources are tightly controlled, and none are needed.

- **Does the number of nuclei you start with change the half-life?** Change: starting number of dice (50, 100, 200). Measure: throws until half remain, removing every six as a "decay" each throw. Keep the same: the removal rule. *(Expect about 4 throws whatever the start: half-life does not depend on amount.)*
- **Does the chance of decay change the half-life?** Change: rule (remove sixes, or remove evens with coins: tails decays). Measure: throws until half remain. Keep the same: starting number. *(Coins halve each throw; dice take about 4.)*
- **Does a bigger hidden target deflect more marbles?** Change: size of a shape hidden under a board. Measure: share of rolled marbles that bounce off course. Keep the same: number of rolls, roll speed. *(A model of Rutherford's gold foil: a small nucleus deflects few.)*

**Safety and ethics:** collect stray marbles from the floor.
