## The idea in plain words

In Year 7 every particle was just "a particle". Now the particles get an identity: they are **atoms**, and atoms come in about 118 **types**, one for each **element** (hydrogen, oxygen, carbon…). All atoms of one element are the same type.

- An **element** is made of one type of atom only. Its particles can be single atoms (helium, neon) or atoms of the same type joined together (oxygen, O₂).
- A **compound** is two or more types of atom **chemically joined** in a fixed ratio (water, H₂O: always 2 hydrogen to 1 oxygen). It is a new substance with new properties: sodium is a metal that reacts violently with water and chlorine is a toxic gas, yet sodium chloride is table salt.
- A **mixture** is two or more substances together but **not joined**: each keeps its own particles and properties.

A formula tells you the atom types and how many of each: H₂O has 2 H and 1 O. The small number belongs to the atom before it.

## Where the picture breaks

- Atoms are drawn as coloured balls. Real atoms have no colour, and they have parts inside (Year 9).
- Sodium chloride is not made of separate NaCl molecules: it is a lattice of sodium and chloride ions (Year 10). The formula still gives the ratio, 1 to 1.
- The simulations are 2D and much more spread out than a real liquid or solid.

## Common misconceptions

- A compound is a mixture of its elements (water is "hydrogen and oxygen mixed").
- A molecule of an element must be a single atom (so O₂ "is a compound").
- The subscript multiplies the whole formula (H₂O read as "2 water").
- Atoms of a substance have the substance's properties (a copper atom is "orange", a sulfur atom "smells").

## Elements (the new first step)

An **element** is a pure substance of only one type of atom; its particles may be single atoms (He) or joined (O₂, O₃). Symbols are one capital letter or a capital and a small letter (Co is cobalt, CO is carbon monoxide). Misconceptions: pure means element; an element's particles must be single atoms.

## Mixtures and representations (nodes 4 and 5)

A mixture is substances mingled, not joined: properties kept, any amounts, separated physically. A formula gives atom types and numbers (the small number counts the atom before it; a big number counts particles); structural formulas show joins, models show shape. No representation shows everything.

## Investigate in class

Optional ideas if you run a practical for this unit: pick one, adapt it to your equipment, or use your own.

- **Does the share of iron filings in an iron and sand mixture change how much a magnet recovers?** Change: grams of iron in 20 g of mixture (2, 5, 10 g). Measure: mass of iron pulled out. Keep the same: magnet, number of passes, total mass. *(Wrap the magnet in cling wrap so filings come off easily. Any amounts mix, and the iron keeps its magnetism: it is mixed, not joined.)*
- **Does adding more salt to crushed ice change the lowest temperature it reaches?** Change: salt per 100 g of ice (0, 5, 10, 20 g). Measure: lowest temperature in 5 minutes, stirring. Keep the same: mass of ice, cup, stirring. *(Pure ice stays near 0 °C; the mixture's melting point depends on how much salt, falling well below 0 °C.)*

**Safety and ethics:** salt and ice can drop below −10 °C; handle the cup, not the slush, and keep filings away from eyes.
