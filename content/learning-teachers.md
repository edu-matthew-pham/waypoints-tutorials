## What is here

**Self-regulated learning** is how students plan, check and look back on their own learning. It's a skill to keep practising in every subject, like spelling, rather than a topic to finish. Most of it happens in students' heads, so the work is making it visible.

Two things are built so far, as a pilot (not yet tried with a class):

- [How you learn best](../learning/how-you-learn/): an optional introduction for Years 6 to 8, four discussion workshops (about 40 minutes each). Students meet the ideas through made-up tasks with a trap in them, tell steering their learning apart from choosing it, see why studying that feels easy can fool them, and plan one strategy to try on a real task in another subject.
- [Strategy cards](../learning/how-you-learn/reference/): one screen per strategy, for before, during and after a task, with what it looks like in maths, science, English and Code. Plus four cards for Code (say what it should do, test a small piece, read the error, trace it).

The course is optional. The cards and the way you prompt them in your own lessons are what build the habit.

## The cycle

One shared language across subjects:

- **Before:** What is it asking? Make a plan.
- **During:** Predict first. Check your confidence. Explain it to yourself. When you're stuck, name the kind of stuck.
- **After:** Look back. Did it work? What would I change?

This is Zimmerman's cycle (forethought, performance, self-reflection), which the EEF simplifies as plan, monitor and evaluate.

## Bringing it into your lessons

The research is clear on one thing: these strategies work best taught **inside** normal subject content, not as separate thinking-skills lessons. So use them on whatever you're teaching.

**Teach a strategy once, properly (NEMO-T).** Shyam Barr's sequence (*Educate to Self-Regulate*, 2024) works for any strategy card:

1. **Name** the strategy ("Today: predict first").
2. **Explain** what it is, and when and why it helps.
3. **Model** it with a think-aloud (below).
4. **Opportunity** to practise it on today's task.
5. **Transfer:** ask where else it would help, in this subject or another.

**Model it with a think-aloud.** Talk through your own thinking on a real task from your subject, out loud. Plan four or so stopping points, each doing one job (plan, check, fix). Show real wobbles: start down the wrong path, notice it, and recover. A polished performance teaches less than an honest one. Then have pairs think aloud to each other, and later, students do it quietly in their heads.

**Then prompt it lightly, and fade.** Knowing a strategy isn't the same as using it when it matters (Barr calls this having an umbrella but leaving it in the bag). Once a class knows a strategy, re-teaching it can bore them. Instead, ask an open question at the right moment: "What might help you here?" rather than "Use the planning strategy". Over the term, prompt less often, until students do it unprompted.

**How often.** Not every lesson. A prompt on every slide becomes a routine students tune out. Roughly a third of a unit's lessons or fewer: teach one strategy early, model it at the first genuinely hard lesson, nudge before challenge or independent work, and look back at the end.

**Where in a lesson.** One moment per lesson is plenty:

- **Opening:** "What's your plan?" or "Predict first."
- **Middle, before the hard step:** "How do you know it's working?" or a think-aloud on that step.
- **Close:** "What would you do differently next time?"

## Cautions

- **It depends on subject knowledge.** Students can't check their understanding of something they know nothing about. Teach the content; use the strategies on it.
- **Keep it light during hard tasks.** Reflection prompts compete for the same working memory as the task. One prompt, not a checklist.
- **Feelings matter.** Frustration is often when students stop checking and start guessing. "This is the hard part, and that's normal" can do more than a strategy.
- **It's the student's own record.** Plans and reflections are for the student, not something to collect or mark.

## Planned, not built

These are plans, not promises:

- Strategy cards for maths, science and English in each subject's own words.
- Optional slides to drop into a lesson deck: *Teach it* (a short NEMO-T insert), *Show it* (a think-aloud moment) and *Nudge it* (one prompt).
- A "try a strategy first" link at the moment a student is stuck in a lesson, before the hints.

## The research

Zimmerman (the self-regulated learning cycle); Winne and Hadwin (understanding the task); Dunlosky, and Nelson and Narens (monitoring and calibration); Bjork (illusions of competence, desirable difficulties); Dunlosky and colleagues (2013, a review of study techniques); Harris and Graham (SRSD, strategy instruction); Veenman (what, when, why and how); Loksa, Ko and Prather (novice programmers' problem solving); Knowles (self-directed learning); Shyam Barr (NEMO-T; availability and production of strategies); and the EEF's Metacognition and self-regulation guidance. The course simplifies these for Years 6 to 8.
