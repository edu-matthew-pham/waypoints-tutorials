title: Choosing AI tools for learning

*Last checked: 18 October 2026.*

This guide helps teachers make informed choices about AI tools. It complements [AI Foundations](../ai/foundations/) and [Does AI Help Us Learn?](../ai/does-ai-help-us-learn/), but it is **not** another student course, a vendor ranking or an endorsement.

## Start with the learning purpose

Before naming products, decide what students need to learn and what evidence would show it. A tool that makes a task faster may not help students learn to do it independently.

Ask two questions throughout:

1. **Does it work?** Does the experience improve the outcome we actually value: thinking, understanding, independent transfer, teacher time, or useful information about learning?
2. **Can we trust it?** Are the conditions safe, permitted, equitable and transparent enough for this use?

A promising answer to the first does not make the second optional.

## Compare against a well-prompted general LLM

A fair trial should not compare a specialised education product only against a general chatbot given vague instructions. A structured prompt may reproduce much of the *interaction behaviour* at little extra complexity.

[Waypoints Kit](https://github.com/edu-matthew-pham/waypoints-kit) offers reusable formats for different purposes:

| Interaction | What the student does | Kit format |
|---|---|---|
| **Coach** | Learns through hints, questions, practice and reflection | [coach.md](https://github.com/edu-matthew-pham/waypoints-kit/blob/main/coach.md) |
| **Draft** | Develops work with an explicit, permitted level of AI help | [draft.md](https://github.com/edu-matthew-pham/waypoints-kit/blob/main/draft.md) |
| **Defend** | Explains decisions in already completed work, without the AI supplying answers | [defend.md](https://github.com/edu-matthew-pham/waypoints-kit/blob/main/defend.md) |
| **Discussion** | Articulates and tests ideas in a question-led conversation without needing completed work | [discussion.md](https://github.com/edu-matthew-pham/waypoints-kit/blob/main/discussion.md) |

These are **examples of purposeful prompting**, not attempts to recreate an entire commercial platform. Test the prompt in an AI service that your school allows. If it cannot open the Kit URLs, use [the bundled Kit file](https://github.com/edu-matthew-pham/waypoints-kit/blob/main/waypoints-kit-all.md) and supply the relevant curriculum node or task information. Check that both conditions receive the same learning material.

A prompt can shape questioning and assistance, but cannot supply product-level controls such as a locked session, school-managed permissions, independent transcripts, data agreements or verified identity. Compare **behaviour** and **controls** separately.

## Choose a small trial

Use existing practice as a benchmark when possible, as well as a thoughtfully prompted general LLM. Not every trial needs three conditions.

| Decide | Practical question |
|---|---|
| Goal | What precisely should improve, and for whom? |
| Comparison | Existing teaching practice, a structured general-LLM prompt, or another tool? |
| Fairness | Are the task, waypoint, time, initial information and permitted assistance comparable? |
| Participants | Which students or teachers participate, with what approvals and accessible alternatives? |
| Immediate evidence | What behaviours, work samples, explanations, time saved or corrections will we observe? |
| Later evidence | After one or two weeks, can students handle a comparable **new task without the tool**? |
| Experience | What did students and teachers find useful, frustrating, inaccessible or misleading? |
| Decision | What would justify adopting, changing, repeating or ending the trial? |

Avoid interpreting engagement, fluent answers or confidence alone as proof of learning. Consider order effects: a student who has already practised defending an answer may do better in the next condition simply because it is their second attempt. Record important differences and keep claims proportionate to a small trial.

## Can we trust it?

Check these **before student use**, using dated vendor information, the relevant school policies and appropriate approval. Mark unknowns as unknowns rather than treating them as permission.

| Area | What to verify |
|---|---|
| **Privacy and data** | What is uploaded, where it goes, retention/deletion, training use and any relevant agreements |
| **Safety** | Age-appropriate safeguards, behaviour when things go wrong, teacher escalation |
| **Terms of use** | Minimum age, education versus consumer terms, permissions for minors and assessment use |
| **Teacher oversight** | Visibility, settings, accountability, ability to review and override decisions |
| **Fair access** | School-managed accounts, accessibility, language support, devices and equitable alternatives |
| **Practical fit** | Reliability, teacher workload, export/interoperability, costs and ongoing maintenance |

Even if a prompt and a product yield equally good questions, a dedicated service may be preferable because of genuinely useful oversight or protections. Conversely, extra controls do not by themselves prove better learning.

## Three kinds of tools to evaluate

### Help tools

Tutors, study modes and assistants should be examined using the habits from **AI Foundations, Unit 4: Getting good help from AI**: asking for the right sort of help, checking claims and deciding which thinking to retain.

A useful comparison is a specialised study mode against a school-approved chatbot using Kit's **Coach** prompt on the same waypoint. Observe hint-first behaviour, teacher control and whether students can later solve a new problem independently.

### Evidence tools

Tools that question students about completed work should be examined using **Does AI Help Us Learn?, Unit 4: Who decides what learning looks like?** (especially its second lesson, *How can we tell what we have learned?*). Ask what the conversation actually reveals, whether judgments are fair to quieter or multilingual students, and what a teacher can inspect or challenge.

A useful comparison is a teacher's short discussion of the same work, the specialised evidence workflow and Kit's **Defend** prompt in a school-approved general chatbot. The prompt is only an *unlocked rehearsal*: do not treat a self-selected chat record as verified assessment evidence. Investigate whether speaking fluently is being mistaken for understanding.

### Teacher tools

For planning, resource generation, feedback drafts and rubrics, examine time saved **after correction**, factual and curriculum accuracy, how much editing is required, and whether the teacher can explain and own the final decisions. A carefully specified general prompt is again a useful baseline.

## Example trial questions

- **A study mode versus Coach:** Are students more capable on a new, unaided task a week later, or do they simply finish the supported task faster?
- **A defence platform versus a teacher conversation and Defend:** Does it uncover useful reasoning that the teacher would otherwise miss? Which students are disadvantaged by the interaction? Which additional controls matter?
- **A planning tool versus an ordinary prompted assistant:** How much verified teacher time does each save once corrections and review are counted?

For all three, separate the quality of the interaction from trust, permissions, workload and access. Record the results before deciding whether any dedicated product is worth adopting.

## Make a decision, not a leaderboard

After the trial, record:

- What worked, and what evidence supports that conclusion.
- What remains uncertain, including any trust or fairness concerns.
- Which features a dedicated product adds beyond a well-prompted general LLM.
- Whether to adopt, revise and retrial, or not proceed — and who owns the decision.

Keep conclusions local to the task, age group and setting tested. Product features and terms change; verify them at the time of each trial. A teacher's professional judgement remains essential.
