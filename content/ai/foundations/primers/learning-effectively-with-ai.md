title: Getting good help from AI in 5 minutes
---
## The idea in plain words

Three habits: **ask for the right kind of help** (a hint, feedback or a worked example, depending on what you need to practise), **check what AI tells you** against evidence you can inspect, and **decide what to hand over** (keep the thinking that is the skill you're learning; hand over jobs on the edge of it, then check them).

## Why this is useful

A finished answer can skip the thinking a student came to practise, and a fluent claim can be wrong. These habits keep the learning with the student without banning help.

## Where the picture breaks

- The boards use prepared hints, claims and situations, not a live AI.
- Worked examples are not "bad": beginners often learn well from them, if they then explain and try a new question.
- "Supported by these measurements" is narrower than "always true".

## Common misconceptions

- "A hint is always better than an answer."
- "If it sounds confident, it's right."
- "Anything boring can be handed over." Only if it isn't the skill you're learning.

## Try it yourself (5 minutes)

On lesson 1's board, do the fractions challenge once with one hint and once with the worked example, then try the new question each time. Which run left more of the thinking to you?

## Questions students ask

**Can I use AI for homework?** That is your school's rule to set. These lessons help you decide which parts of a task to keep, whatever the rule.

**How do I check an AI claim?** Look for the key word (every, always, average) and test it against the evidence, or a source you trust. Asking the same AI again isn't checking.

## How it connects

Does AI Help Us Learn? tests these habits: does using them actually help students learn, and when?
