## Purpose

The committee refines the AI lines inside the school's policies, and the AI statement and map that hold them together (lesson 4), using the evidence from Units 2 and 3. The product is **version 2 of the statement and map**, with each proposed AI line sent to that policy's owner.

## The lesson

11. **Refine the AI rules.** Sort draft lines into too vague, about right and too prescribed. Move fast-changing detail out of the policy into termly guidelines or task instructions. Rewrite lines that rely on catching people so they work through task design and honest acknowledgement, and lines that rely on what AI can't do yet.

## Five balances

- **Vague vs prescribed.** Too vague can't decide a case; too prescribed (named apps, versions, every allowed use) is out of date within weeks and removes teacher judgement. Layers solve most of it: **AI lines in each policy** (stable), **guidelines** (updated each term: approved tools, settings, year-level expectations), **task instructions** (set by the teacher: how much AI this task allows).
- **Checkable vs policed.** A school can check that its policy works (learning holds up without AI, reports come in, nobody is treated unfairly). It mostly can't catch individual AI use: home use is invisible and detectors are unreliable, wrongly flagging second-language writers most. A workable policy relies on task design, rewarding honesty, and students understanding why the thinking matters, with enforcement kept for clear cases.

- **One big policy vs scattered lines.** A big AI policy duplicates rules other policies own and drifts from them; scattered lines fragment unless someone holds the map. The answer: a thin statement that points, AI lines in each policy, and a named person who keeps them in step and passes changes along.
- **Today's limits vs tomorrow's.** Lines built on what AI can't do yet ("it makes maths mistakes", "it can't fill in a quiz") break as it improves: many tools now check sums by running code, and some act for you. Test every line with "what if AI could do this next year?", and add a line for AI that acts: a person sends, submits or changes anything on a school account.

- **Rule vs know-how.** When something goes wrong, check whether the rule is wrong (fix it) or people don't yet know how to apply it (guidance, examples, a briefing). New rules don't fix know-how gaps.

## Everyday picture

A road: the law says "drive to the conditions" (policy), the signs change with roadworks (guidelines), and the driver judges this corner (task). Police can't watch every corner, so roads are designed to make the safe choice easy.

## Where the picture breaks

Students don't need a licence, and a school's "road design" is mostly the tasks teachers set.

## Cautions

Keep real detector product names out of student discussion. If students raise their own experience of being accused, keep it general and follow up privately.

## Staff committee

This is the drafting session for staff: work through each policy's AI lines with their owners in the room, and agree who holds the map.
