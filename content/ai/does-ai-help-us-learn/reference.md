# Does AI Help Us Learn? — Concept and evidence reference

Use this reference to clarify ideas and question claims. It does **not** supply a single correct answer to the course's driving inquiry.

## Learning and performance

| Term | Meaning | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| **Performance** | What someone achieves under particular conditions, including available help | What can this result establish? |
| **Learning** | A developing change in knowledge, skills, understanding, judgement or participation | What has changed for the learner? |
| **Transfer** | Applying an idea in a new situation | Can the learner use it beyond the practised example? |
| **Retention** | What remains available over time | What would a later attempt show? |
| **Meaningful learning** | Depends partly on educational purpose: independent competence, creative work and participation may all matter | What is worth learning here, and why? |

## Thinking and assistance

| Term | Meaning | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Cognitive offloading** | Using a tool or resource to handle part of a mental task | Saving effort can free capacity or bypass intended learning |
| **Central and peripheral thinking** | Thinking more or less directly related to the *current* learning goal (Nakamura's word for central is *constitutive*) | A sliding scale, not two boxes; it changes with the goal and learner |
| **Metacognition** | Monitoring and reflecting on one's understanding | Confidence can differ from demonstrated ability |
| **Calibration** | How well a confidence judgement matches available evidence of performance | One attempt is an imperfect indicator |
| **Self-regulated learning** | Setting goals, monitoring progress, choosing strategies and adjusting actions | Learners may need help judging what they do not yet understand |
| **Scaffolding** | Support that helps someone work towards a developing capability | Hints are not automatically superior to worked examples |
| **Fading support** | Gradually reducing guidance as capability develops | A well-designed sequence is not proof of durable learning |

## What makes an activity educationally useful?

- **Behavioural interaction:** What the student visibly does, such as clicking, answering or talking.
- **Cognitive engagement:** What the student thinks about, recalls, explains, predicts, tests or revises.
- **Instructional responsiveness:** How an explanation, hint or activity responds to the learner's answer or need.
- **Evidence of learning:** What an appropriate new attempt, explanation or delayed check suggests about developing capability.

More interaction, more responsiveness or harder struggle does **not automatically** mean more learning. A worked example, retrieval quiz, interactive tutorial, teacher or AI tutor may each be useful for different goals.

## How to read AI-learning research

1. **Claim:** What precisely is being suggested?
2. **Comparison:** Compared with what? Consider effective non-AI tools, not just no assistance.
3. **Outcome:** Assisted task performance, immediate test, independent transfer, retention, confidence or another educational goal?
4. **Population and conditions:** Which learners, tasks, tools and time period?
5. **Inference:** What does the study support, and what remains uncertain?

### Evidence encountered in Unit 2

| Source | What the course uses it to examine | Important limit |
|---|---|---|
| [Bastani et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122) | Mathematics practice gains with AI and differing later unaided test results depending on assistance design | Does not settle performance against the strongest non-AI interactive tutors |
| [Kestin et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6) | Positive short-term results for an AI tutoring package relative to instructor-led active learning | Each student tried both, in random order (a crossover design); different instructional features changed together; longer-term learning was not established |
| [Fernandes et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108779) | AI-assisted reasoning performance can coexist with overestimated competence | Not a direct study of long-term school-course learning |
| [Nakamura (2026), *Augmentation or Erosion?*](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-026-10224-6) | A theoretical explanation for why outcomes might depend on goals, expertise and offloading choices | **A theoretical framework with testable propositions, not a validating experiment** |

The research landscape changes. This is a reference to the studies examined in the course **as of October 2026**, not an exhaustive or permanent verdict.

## Assessment, decisions and agency

- **Formative assessment:** Uses evidence to guide what to learn or do next.
- **Summative assessment:** Uses evidence to judge achievement at a particular point.
- **Validity:** Whether evidence adequately supports the particular interpretation or claim.
- **Learner agency:** Being able to make and act on informed choices about learning.
- **Assessment incentives:** Ways that assessment expectations affect what and how students practise.

One task may serve formative and summative purposes. AI assistance could be useful for learning yet alter what a submitted result can establish about independent capability.

## A reusable questioning routine

When deciding whether an AI interaction helps learning, ask:

**Learning what? For whom? Compared with which alternative? With which thinking retained? Under what conditions? What evidence would change my judgement?**

This is a reasoning guide, **not a mandatory checklist or a rule that AI is always helpful or harmful**.
