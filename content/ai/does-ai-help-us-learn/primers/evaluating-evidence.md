---
title: Unit 2 — What does the evidence show?
---
## Purpose

Students now have several competing understandings of learning. They investigate what empirical studies actually measure, how confidence may differ from performance, and why even credible experiments cannot answer every educational question.

## Distinctive teaching moves

1. **Evidence confrontation (lesson 1):** Compare Bastani's high-school mathematics trial with Kestin's university physics tutoring trial. Emphasise genuinely different treatments, comparisons and assessment outcomes rather than a simple good-versus-bad framing.
2. **Metacognitive calibration (lesson 2):** Experience the difference between help, confidence and a new attempt. The activity is **not** an AI experiment; Fernandes and colleagues' reasoning research supplies separate empirical evidence.
3. **Methodological redesign (lesson 3):** Design a better comparison between existing instruction, effective interactive tools and an added AI feature. Nakamura's theoretical proposal is an optional Elaborate extension: design a study that could test or refute it.

## Sources for teachers

- Bastani et al. (2025), *Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics*: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
- Kestin et al. (2025), *AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning*: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6
- Fernandes et al. (2026), *AI makes you smarter but none the wiser*: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108779

- Nakamura (2026), *Augmentation or Erosion? Regulating Cognitive Offloading in Generative AI-Supported STEM Learning*: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-026-10224-6. A theoretical review offering seven testable propositions, **not** a directly validating experiment.

These sources have different populations, interventions, outcomes and limitations. Avoid flattening them into a single consensus.

## Conceptual checkpoints and dialogue

Check understanding of comparison groups, outcome measures, transfer, calibration, alternative explanations and validity. Leave the larger question open: how should we value unaided test performance alongside creativity, collaboration and productive tool use?

Use selective, honest discussion prompts. A productive dialogue need not end in consensus. Students should know how to recognise an overclaim without concluding that all evidence is useless.

## Tools and artefacts

Reuse existing checkpoints and the prepared practice board. The board is not a live AI system; its purpose is to provide an inspectable experience. No new simulation is necessary for a first review of Unit 2. An optional journal, forum or argument map can capture evolving reasoning; none is required.

## Transition

Now that students can scrutinise claims, Unit 3 asks when AI assistance adds something valuable beyond established educational tools.
