title: Unit 3 — When should we use AI?

## Purpose

Unit 3 turns the inquiry from evidence into learning decisions: what thinking to retain, which interaction actually serves learning, and how assistance should be designed.

## Three linked moves

1. **Lesson 1 — What thinking should we keep?** Recall the Bastani result, then sort the jobs in a writing task into keep and hand over, change the goal, introduce beginner–expert differences, and test the "is the skill / on the edge" idea. A short game example tests transfer without requiring coding knowledge.
2. **Lesson 2 — Interaction versus learning:** Students answer one negative-number question, then compare four made-up kinds of help (quiz game, number pattern, prepared tutorial, AI-style chat). Distinguish what learners do, what they think about, how the help responds, and evidence of learning.
3. **Lesson 3 — Scaffolding:** Sort tutor messages into model-first, try-first and fading designs. Vary expertise, challenge the idea that difficulty always helps, and consider who controls support.

## Intellectual cautions

The cognitive offloading framework discussed by Nakamura in 2026 (https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-026-10224-6) is a theoretical proposal, not a fully validated experimental rule. The paper's terms are *peripheral* and *constitutive* (the course says central), on a graded continuum rather than two fixed categories. Expertise effects themselves are well established without AI (the expertise reversal effect: Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller, 2003, https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4); what is untested is Nakamura's proposal that they govern generative-AI offloading. Neither more interaction nor more cognitive activity automatically establishes durable learning. Do not replace simplistic faith in AI with simplistic rules such as always using hints or never providing answers.

Background on cognitive offloading for schools: Lodge and Loble (2026), *Artificial intelligence, cognitive offloading and implications for education* (University of Technology Sydney), https://doi.org/10.71741/4pyxmbnjaq.31302475. On tutoring and trying first: VanLehn (2011), https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2011.611369 (step-based computer tutors came close to human tutors in many studies), and Sinha and Kapur (2021), https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105 (problem solving before instruction can help, depending on its design).

## Accessible delivery

Core examples are accessible writing and familiar arithmetic. Student text names no products. If you bring in real platforms students know, treat them as examples, not endorsements or fixed categories: they change, and many now mix prepared feedback with generative AI.

The unit uses authored illustrations and checkpoints. No live AI access, commercial login, full simulation or specialist programming knowledge is required. Optional external exploration may be offered when students already have approved access.

## Facilitation

Progression of **reasoning** rather than continuity of one specialist scenario is the lesson spine. Give students a case to inspect, then change the goal or learner to complicate the initial inference. Distinguish theoretical claims, empirical findings and educational judgements.

Course-level teacher notes describe optional journal, forum and argument-map artefacts. None is a required student submission.

## Transition

Unit 4 examines how formative feedback differs from summative evidence of achievement, and how teachers, students and institutions make decisions about both.
