---
title: Unit 4 — Who decides what learning looks like?
---
## Purpose

Bring the course back to the student's own learning decisions, then widen the inquiry to assessment and educational values. The emphasis is not professional learning for teachers or institutional compliance. Students investigate how their choices are shaped by evidence, incentives, tools and other people's purposes.

## Learning progression

1. **Lesson 1 — How do we know when we need help?** Use the feedback cycle as a self-regulation problem: attempt, interpret feedback, choose a next action, gather new evidence. This is formative assessment viewed from the learner's perspective.
2. **Lesson 2 — How can we tell what we have learned?** Contrast similar products made through different processes. Change the intended assessed capability to examine validity and the difference between learning evidence and summative claims.
3. **Lesson 3 — Who decides what learning is worth?** Examine learner, teacher, qualification and community purposes; explore assessment incentives and agency; return to the driving inquiry using qualified claims and real empirical evidence from Unit 2.

## Sources for teachers

- Hattie and Timperley (2007), *The power of feedback*: https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487 (lesson 1: feedback that says what to do next helps far more than praise or a grade alone).
- Black and Wiliam (1998), *Assessment and classroom learning*: https://doi.org/10.1080/0969595980050102 (lessons 1 and 2: formative assessment can raise achievement).

Assessment incentives are taught in lesson 3 only.

## Distinctions to protect

Formative and summative describe **purposes**, not completely separate classes of activities. A task can inform ongoing learning and later contribute evidence to an achievement judgement.

Unit 2 taught confidence calibration and limits of empirical evidence; Unit 3 examined cognitive offloading and tutor design. Avoid re-teaching these as standalone concepts. In Unit 4, students apply them when deciding what to do next, what a judgement of achievement means and which educational purposes matter.

## Delivery

Use the fictional seasons-feedback scenario, three students' waste-reduction investigations and fictional stakeholder perspectives. These are illustrations, not experiments. No AI account, commercial platform or specialist subject knowledge is required. If needed, provide a simple explanation of the seasons misconception.

The final student inquiry is open. Evidence cannot settle educational values by itself. Avoid demanding a particular policy, a compulsory final essay or a prescribed pro-AI/anti-AI conclusion.

Course-level teacher guidance outlines optional journals, forums, position statements and collaborative argument maps. These remain optional formats; ordinary discussion and built-in checks suffice.

## Course culmination

A strong student position explains **which learning**, **which learner**, **which assistance**, **compared with what**, and **what evidence could change the conclusion**. Students need not converge on consensus.
