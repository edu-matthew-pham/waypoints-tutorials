# Teaching this course

## Driving inquiry

**Does AI help us learn?**

This is a genuinely contested question. The course does not lead students to a prescribed yes/no answer. Students should encounter ideas that complicate earlier claims, differentiate empirical evidence from theory and educational values, and become better at articulating what would change their minds.

The **dialectical dialogue** is the connecting pedagogy, not a ritual in which students must adopt opposing views or change positions at the end. Engage seriously with plausible objections and distinguish a claim's strength from a speaker's confidence.

## What is taught explicitly

Keep concepts and larger conclusions distinct:

- **Check conceptual understanding:** assisted performance, learning, transfer, retention, metacognitive calibration, self-regulation, cognitive offloading, research design and assessment validity.
- **Keep normative conclusions open:** what education should value, when AI adds enough to justify its use, and which human capabilities matter most.
- **Use evidence responsibly:** AI-supported productivity and learning are different outcomes. Unaided tests are informative but imperfect proxies for meaningful learning. Avoid claiming that the theoretical promise of selective offloading has been proven experimentally.

Research, self-regulated learning theory, and sociocultural and political perspectives are **lenses to investigate**, not doctrines students must affirm. Teachers can challenge assumptions about both AI and conventional schooling.

## Flexible classroom artefacts — suggestions, not requirements

The tutorial works without a separate journal, forum or argument-map system. Choose what suits the class, time and school expectations; it is unnecessary to collect the same reasoning in multiple places.

| Optional artefact | Possible use |
|---|---|
| **Individual inquiry journal** | Revisit an initial claim, evidence, counterarguments and questions over the course |
| **Discussion forum** | Continue a reasoned exchange asynchronously, with attention to listening and evidence |
| **Collaborative argument map** | Make contrasting claims, assumptions, evidence and open questions visible |
| **Final position statement** | Present and defend a carefully qualified position, with its evidentiary limits |

These are **teacher-selected formats**, not required submissions, assessment conditions or prerequisites to later lessons. A spoken reflection, ordinary classroom dialogue or built-in checkpoint may suffice. Later prompts to revisit earlier thinking can be answered without an ongoing personal artefact.

## Dialogue facilitation

- Open with an authentic provocation; let students articulate initial ideas before explaining technical vocabulary.
- Introduce substantial evidence, experiences or a counterexample so discussion is more than an exchange of opinions.
- Ask learners to give the strongest reasonable version of a view they question.
- Separate **what happened**, **what it might imply**, and **what we think ought to matter**.
- Do not invent false balance: claims about research and tools still have to survive evidentiary scrutiny.
- Allow uncertainty, justified disagreement and increased confidence where evidence warrants them. Changing one's mind is not an automatic indicator of learning.

## Tools and assessment

Reuse existing interactive boards and conceptual checkpoints where they expose a meaningful distinction. Do not assume live AI is necessary. Prepared simulations should be identified as such and should not be presented as actual experiments or live language models.

Use checkpoints to establish understanding of **concepts and evidence**, rather than grade agreement with a preferred philosophical conclusion. A polished final artefact is not, by itself, a reliable account of the reasoning or capabilities behind it.

## Course progression

1. **What does it mean to learn?** Surface assumptions, contrast performance and learning, and compare educational purposes.
2. **What does the evidence show?** Examine research, metacognitive calibration and the limits of measurement.
3. **When should we use AI?** Examine offloading, established non-AI alternatives and instructional safeguards.
4. **Who decides what learning looks like?** Investigate incentives, agency, educational values and future capabilities.
