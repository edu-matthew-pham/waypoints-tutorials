Levers

A lever trades force for distance: a small push far from the pivot lifts a big load near it, and your hand moves farther than the load. Students find where to put a pivot by testing, work out the push from turning effects, meet the three classes of lever (including the one in their arm), and design a lever to a brief.

A 600 N rock, a 2 m bar, and a block to rest it on. You can push 200 N at most. Where does the block go?

Builds on: How big, which way? (Science › Year 7 Physics) · Does balanced mean no forces? (Science › Year 7 Physics)

Put to work in: Beams and shapes (Engineering › Structures)

Gears trade speed for turning force. A lever makes the same kind of trade with a bar and a pivot (the point it turns on, also called a fulcrum).

The job: a 600 N rock (about 60 kg) sits on one end of a 2 m bar. You can push down on the other end with 200 N at most. Where should the pivot go?

Forces are in newtons (N); 10 N is about the weight of 1 kg.

Explain it to me

A lever is a bar that turns on a pivot. A force's turning effect is the force × its distance from the pivot. It balances when the turning effects on both sides are equal: 600 N × 0.5 m = 200 N × 1.5 m.

Pushing far from the pivot makes your push stronger, by the same factor that your hand moves farther: 3 times the force, 3 times the distance. Where the pivot, the load and the push sit gives three kinds of lever; the one in your arm gives up force to gain speed.

A lever to a brief

Need a hint?

The load is 900 N and you push 200 N: you need an advantage of at least 900 ÷ 200 = 4.5.

Another hint

Your hand may move 0.5 m to lift the load 0.1 m: an advantage of at most 5.

Another hint

Your distance from the pivot ÷ the load's distance must be between 4.5 and 5. With the push at the end, try a pivot between 0.3 m and 0.4 m.

Stretch: a real bar has weight. Which side of the pivot would its weight help, and which would it hinder?

For teachers

Students slide a pivot along a bar until a 200 N push lifts a 600 N rock, learn that turning effects (force × distance from the pivot) balance and that the hand moves farther by the same factor, meet class 2 and class 3 levers in a wheelbarrow and their own arm, and design a lever that meets two competing criteria. About 45 minutes.

Open the teacher guide: answers, solutions, slides and a worksheet.

Students will be able to

  • I can work out the push a lever needs from force × distance on each side of the pivot.
  • I can explain that a lever makes my push bigger by the same factor it makes my hand move farther.
  • I can say whether a lever is class 1, 2 or 3 from where the pivot, the load and the push are.
  • I can design a lever that meets more than one criterion.

Before class

Do Gear up the winch first if you can: this lesson makes the same trade (force for distance, not speed) and the Practise match ties the two together.

A metre ruler on a pencil with coins makes a good start: balance two coins against one and measure from the pencil.

Simplifications: the bar weighs nothing and does not bend, there is no friction at the pivot, and the push and the load act straight down (or up). The board exaggerates the bar's tilt so the movement is easy to see.

Curriculum hooks: Design and Technologies, engineering principles and systems (primary); Science, forces and simple machines (Year 7 forces); Maths, multiplying and dividing decimals.

Running the lesson

  1. Predict: take a vote, then ask someone who chose 'middle is best' to explain. Keep their reason for after the Try.
  2. Try: when the rock lifts, ask how far the hand would have to move.
  3. Worked example: write force × distance on both sides of a drawn pivot. Point at where each distance starts.
  4. Find the mistake: ask Leo's question back: 2 m from where?
  5. Vary, arm: have students hold a bag at the hand and then at the elbow. Which is easier, and why?
  6. Make: compare designs. Who moved the push instead of the pivot?

The hard part (on purpose)

Students measure distances from the end of the bar, not the pivot, and expect a stronger push to come free. Keep asking 'from where?' and 'how far did your hand move?'

When students get stuck

  • A student measures from the end of the bar. Ask: Where does the bar turn? Measure from there. Fix: Every distance starts at the pivot.
  • A student thinks the lever gives extra force for nothing. Ask: How far did your hand move to lift the rock 10 cm? Fix: Less force, more distance, by the same factor.
  • A student cannot tell the classes apart. Ask: Find the pivot first. What is in the middle? Fix: Pivot in the middle: 1. Load in the middle: 2. Push in the middle: 3.
  • A student says class 3 levers are useless. Ask: What happens to the end of a fishing rod when you move the handle a little? Fix: Class 3 gives up force to gain distance and speed.

Stretch and support

Stretch: A 2 m bar that weighs 50 N has its weight at its middle. With the pivot 0.5 m from the 600 N rock, the bar's own weight is 0.5 m on your side. What push do you need now?

Support: Give a drawn bar with the pivot marked and a table: force, distance from pivot, force × distance, for each side.

What each step is for

  • move the pivot: Committing to which way the push changes as the pivot moves tests the new move before the board shows it. Each wrong option is a real slip: the middle is best, nothing changes, or the push depends on speed.
  • Try: find the spot: Testing settles the prediction: with the pivot in the middle the rock does not move; slide it towards the rock until a 200 N push lifts it. The numbers are hidden so students find the spot.
  • Worked example: turning effects balance: The worked example gives the rule: force × distance from the pivot (a turning effect, or moment) is the same on both sides when it balances. Then the trade: the hand moves farther by the same factor. The faded example has a pivot at 0.4 m. Leo measures from the end of the bar, planted after the method.
  • Vary: three kinds of lever: The pivot need not be in the middle. In a wheelbarrow the load sits between the pivot (the wheel) and the push; in an arm the push (the muscle) sits between the pivot (the elbow) and the load. The arm needs more force than the load, and the question asks why a body would do that: it gains distance and speed.
  • levers: Turning effects from the pivot, the distance trade, and the three classes by where the pivot, the load and the push sit.
  • a lever to a brief: Two criteria pull against each other: more advantage means more hand travel. Students reason from both to a narrow band of pivot positions (or move the push in), the same move as the gear briefs.
