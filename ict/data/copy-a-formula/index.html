Copy a formula

When you copy a formula to another cell, its cell names move with it. Copy =C2-B2 from D2 down one row and it becomes =C3-B3, because the formula really means 'the cell one to the left, take away the cell two to the left'. So you write one formula, then copy or fill it down the column, and every row works on its own cells.

The scientist measured thirty bean plants. She wrote the growth formula for the first one. Does she have to type it thirty times?

In the last lesson you wrote a growth formula for one bean. Here are five beans, all measured the same way (every height is in cm). Only D2 has a formula so far.

Explain it to me

When you copy a formula, its cell names move with it.

=C2-B2 in D2 means 'the cell one to the left, take away the cell two to the left'. Pasted into D3 it means the same thing in row 3, so it becomes =C3-B3.

So you write one formula and fill it down the column. Each row uses its own cells.

In your own spreadsheet app

Excel, Google Sheets and Numbers all copy formulas this way. To fill down, select the cell and the cells below it, then press Ctrl+D (Cmd+D on a Mac) or drag the small square at the corner of the cell downwards. To copy and paste, use Ctrl+C and Ctrl+V. Click a pasted cell to check its formula used its own row.

For teachers

Students learn that copying a formula moves its cell names with it, so one formula can be copied or filled down a whole column and each row works on its own cells. They fill a growth column for five Year 7 beans, add a growth per day column, and repeat it in the spreadsheet app the school uses. About 45 minutes.

Open the teacher guide: answers, solutions, slides and a worksheet.

Students will be able to

  • I can copy a formula to another cell and say what happens to its cell names.
  • I can fill a formula down a column so each row uses its own cells.
  • I can tell a copied formula from the same text typed in every row.

Before class

Do the Try board yourself: copy D2, paste it into D3 and click D3 to read its formula.

The one idea: a formula's cell names are places relative to the cell it is in ('one to the left'), so when it is copied they move with it. Spreadsheet people call this a relative reference; students only need the idea that a copy moves its names.

The sheet on the page has Copy, Paste and Fill down buttons, and Ctrl+C and Ctrl+V work on a selected cell. The real tools work the same way. In Excel and Sheets, Ctrl+D fills down a selection, and the small square at the corner of a selected cell can be dragged down (or double-clicked in Excel and Sheets to fill as far as the data goes).

The next lesson, A cell that stays put, shows what to do when a copy must not move one of the names.

Running the lesson

  1. Predict: students who chose =C2-B2 think a copy is exact. Ask what D3 would then show, and whose growth that is.
  2. Try: ask which row D3's formula uses, and whose bean that is.
  3. Worked example: say the rule aloud (the cell one to the left take away the cell two to the left) and ask where it points in row 5.
  4. Practise: ask who finds Fill down quickest and why it is less likely to slip than typing.
  5. Own app: walk round. The common slips are pasting into the wrong cell and dragging the fill handle too far.

The hard part (on purpose)

Believing a copy is the same text. Students expect =C2-B2 to appear in D3, and the Predict shows the other answer. Have them click D3 and read the bar before you explain.

When students get stuck

  • A student types =C2-B2 into every row (Sam's mistake) and every growth is 8. Ask: Click D4 and read the formula. Which bean's row does it use? Fix: Copy D2 and paste it into D4, or select D2 and fill down. The names move for you.
  • A student copies the number 8 across instead of the formula. Ask: Click the pasted cell. Does the bar show a formula or a number? Fix: Copy the cell that holds the formula =C2-B2, not a typed number.
  • A student pastes the formula and the answer is wrong because the formula was pasted into the wrong column. Ask: Which two cells does it use now? Are they the Day 1 and Day 4 cells? Fix: Paste into the Growth column, so the formula has Day 4 one to the left and Day 1 two to the left.
  • A student fills too far down and gets a 0 or an error in an empty row. Ask: Which rows have a bean in them? Fix: Fill only as far as the last plant. The empty rows are not wrong, just not needed.

Stretch and support

Stretch: Write one formula in F2 that shows whether Bean A grew more than Bean B (is D2 minus D3 positive?). What happens to it if you paste it into F3?

Support: A card: A copied formula moves its cell names by as much as the copy moves. D2 to D3 is one row down, so every row number goes up by one.

What each step is for

  • what lands in D3?: Committing to what a pasted formula holds tests the move: its cell names shift by as much as the paste does.
  • Try: copy and paste: Pasting D2 into D3 and reading the bar settles the prediction: the names moved down a row, so D3 uses Bean B's cells.
  • Worked example: one rule, every row: Reading the formula as a rule about places models the move. Sam types the same text in every row, the mistake planted after the method.
  • Vary: change the paste: Each task changes where the formula is pasted: further down, one column across, then a quicker way to fill a column.
  • fill the column: Filling a whole column from one formula, and telling a copied rule from five typed copies.
  • your own sheet: Writing one formula and filling it down twice, once for growth and once for growth per day, has students use the move on their own data.
  • In your own spreadsheet app: Copying a formula down in Excel or Sheets shows the same rule at work in the real tool.
