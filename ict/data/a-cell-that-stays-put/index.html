A cell that stays put

Copying a formula moves every cell name in it, which goes wrong when the formula needs one shared cell, such as g in a weight formula. A dollar sign fixes a name: $B$7 stays on B7 wherever the formula is copied. Putting a shared number in its own cell, then fixing it with $, means you change it once and every answer follows.

Weight is mass times g. A sheet works out four weights on Earth. A teacher says: now do the Moon, where g is a lot smaller. Must she change every formula?

Weight is a force: weight (N) = mass (kg) × g. On Earth, g is about 10 newtons for every kilogram. The sheet below keeps g in its own cell, B7, so it is written once. C2 works out the weight of the backpack from its mass in B2 and g in B7.

Explain it to me

When a formula is copied, every cell name in it moves.

A name with $ in front of its letter and number does not. $B$7 stays on B7 wherever the formula goes.

So put a shared number (like g) in its own cell, fix it with $, and fill the formula down. Change that one cell and every answer follows.

In your own spreadsheet app

Excel, Google Sheets and Numbers all use the dollar sign. You can type the $ yourself, or while the cursor is on a cell name in a formula, press F4 (Fn and F4 on some laptops) to put the $ signs in. Press it again to cycle through the ways of fixing the name. If a weight shows 0, click it and check the cell for g did not move.

For teachers

Students find that copying a formula moves every cell name, including one that should stay put, and fix a shared cell with $. They work out weights from mass and g on a Year 7 science sheet, change g once to move from Earth to the Moon, and repeat it in the spreadsheet app the school uses. About 45 minutes.

Open the teacher guide: answers, solutions, slides and a worksheet.

Students will be able to

  • I can say which cell in a formula should stay put when it is copied.
  • I can fix a cell name with $ and fill the formula down.
  • I can change one shared cell and see every answer follow.

Before class

Do the Try board yourself: paste C2 into C3 and read the formula in the bar.

The one idea: a copy moves every name, so a name that has to stay (a shared value such as g, a rate, a price) gets a $ in front of its column letter and row number. Spreadsheet people call this an absolute reference; students need the idea 'fix it with $'.

Science link: weight (N) = mass (kg) × g, with g about 10 N/kg on Earth (a round value used in school science) and about 1.6 N/kg on the Moon. Use your class's values if they differ.

The page's sheet has no formula that needs $ typed for you: students type the $ themselves. In Excel and Sheets, F4 on a selected name toggles the $ signs; the sheet on the page just uses typing.

Running the lesson

  1. Predict: students who chose 120 expect the paste to move only the mass. Ask which cell the bike's weight would use for g.
  2. Try: ask what B8 holds and why the answer is 0, not an error.
  3. Worked example: ask which part of =B2*$B$7 moves when pasted down and which part stays.
  4. Practise: students who forget the $ get 0 below the first row. Ask them to click C3 and say which cell it used for g.
  5. Own app: walk round. Common slips: $ only on one half of the name, or g typed inside the formula.

The hard part (on purpose)

Seeing why a copy goes wrong. Students think the formula is broken, not that every name moved. Have them click the 0 cell and read which cell it used for g.

When students get stuck

  • A student writes =B2*B7 and fills down (Sam's mistake) and gets zeros. Ask: Click C3. Which cell does it use for g? Fix: Put $ in front of the B and the 7 in the first formula, then fill down again.
  • A student types =B2*10, so g is inside every formula. Ask: How many formulas would you change to try the Moon? Fix: Put g in its own cell and use $B$7 in the formula.
  • A student writes =B2*$B7 or =B2*B$7 (half fixed). Ask: When you fill down, which part of B7 still moves? Fix: Fix both: $B$7 stays on B7 wherever it is pasted.
  • A student changes B7 and nothing changes. Ask: Does the weight cell hold a formula or a typed number? Fix: Replace typed numbers with the formula and fill down.

Stretch and support

Stretch: Add a third column for Mars, where g is about 3.7 N per kg. What do you change, and what stays the same?

Support: A card: Copying moves every name. Put $ in front of the letter and the number of the cell that must stay: $B$7.

What each step is for

  • what lands in C3?: Committing to what a pasted formula shows tests the move: every name moves, including the one that should not.
  • Try: paste and look: Pasting C2 into C3 and reading the bar shows which name moved and which one should have stayed.
  • Worked example: fix the cell: Putting dollar signs on the shared cell models the move. Sam writes the shared cell without them, the mistake planted after the method.
  • Vary: change what is fixed: Each task changes the shared cell, then where the formula is pasted, then which of four written forms is right.
  • weigh the objects: Writing a formula that fixes the shared cell, filling it down, then changing the one cell that holds g.
  • Earth and Moon: Building two weight columns from two shared cells has students fix a different cell in each and use the move on their own data.
  • In your own spreadsheet app: Fixing a cell in Excel or Sheets shows the same idea in the real tool, with its own shortcut.
