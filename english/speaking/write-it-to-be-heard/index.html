Write it to be heard

A listener hears a talk once, in order, so a script is written for the ear: short sentences, one idea at a time, signpost words, a key idea said twice, and special words explained as they are used. Students find signpost words, spot the sentence a listener would lose, choose the version that can be said aloud, rework a long written sentence, and write the start of a script.

A sentence can be perfect on paper and impossible to follow out loud. What changes when no one can read it again?

What you should already know:
  • Plan a talk that has a point · A talk works when the speaker knows who is listening and what they should know, think or do afterwards.

A reader can slow down, look back and read a hard sentence twice. A listener cannot. So a script is written for the ear, not the eye.

Writing for the ear means:

  • Short sentences, each one a breath long, with one idea in it.
  • Signpost words (first, second, so, in short) that tell listeners where they are.
  • Saying the key idea twice, in different words.
  • Explaining a special word when you use it, not before and not after.

A longer sentence is fine if it is easy to say. Two ideas joined by a word like because can sound natural, as long as the speaker can say it in a single breath.

How this lesson works: Predict, Try, Investigate, Modify, Practise, Make
  • Predict: Read the problem and decide what you think before you try anything. A wrong guess is fine: it shows you what to look for.
  • Try: Try it on the board and compare the result with your prediction.
  • Investigate: Work out why it happens, one step at a time.
  • Modify: Change the problem on purpose and see what happens.
  • Practise: A few on your own, to check you have it.
  • Make: Make your own problem using what you have learned. Expect a few tries; that is normal.

From the page to the ear

Here is one long written sentence. Copy it into your own document.

The oval, which has been wet since the storm last week and which the groundskeeper says will not be usable until the drains are cleared, is why all training has moved to the gym, where space is limited.

In your own document, rewrite it as three or more sentences for a speaker to say. Keep every sentence under 15 words. Use at least one joining word such as because, when or until.

Here is one example, for a different sentence. Written: "The pool, which closed on Monday after a leak was found under the deep end and which the council says will stay shut until the repairs finish, is why lessons have moved to the hall." Spoken: "The pool closed on Monday. A leak was found under the deep end. It will stay shut until the repairs finish. So lessons have moved to the hall."

Then say your sentences aloud to a partner. Can they follow it without looking? Where did either of you run out of breath?

A short sentence with a joining word is a complex sentence you can say in one breath. Listen for where the breath runs out.

The start of a script

Write the first four sentences of a script for a talk to Year 5 about drinking more water. Say the main idea once, use at least one signpost word, and use the word 'hydrated' but explain it as you do.

Your working

A strong answer
Hands up if you have ever felt tired by lunchtime. Today I want to show you a simple fix: drink more water. First, water keeps you hydrated, which means your body has all the water it needs. A hydrated brain finds it easier to concentrate.
A different approach that also works
Here is my main idea: your brain works better when you drink water. Let me tell you why. First, being hydrated means your body has enough water to work well. Second, a tired brain often just needs a drink.

Wider: a different listener


For teachers
Students find signpost words in a script, see why a key idea is said twice, spot the sentence a listener would lose, choose the version of a sentence that can be said aloud, rework one long sentence into short ones, and write the start of a script. About 45 minutes.
Open the teacher guide: answers, solutions, slides and a worksheet.
Students will be able to
  • I can explain why a script is written differently from a report.
  • I can use short sentences, signpost words and a repeated main idea.
  • I can explain a special word at the moment I use it.
Before class
Read the Predict sentence aloud to yourself, once, then ask what you remember.


The one idea: a script is written for the ear. This lesson covers AC9E5LY07 node 2 (complex sentences and specialist vocabulary in spoken presentation). The criteria say the complexity must be audible, so the lesson does not ban complex sentences: a short sentence with because in it is good speech. What fails is the long sentence with a clause tucked in the middle.


The Practise step has no board: students rewrite the long sentence in their own document, with three or more sentences under 15 words and at least one joining word, so they keep one short complex sentence (AC9E5LY07 n2). Fifteen words is a rough guide: a 14-word sentence can still be hard to say. The real test is saying it aloud to a partner, who listens for where the breath runs out.


The Make step is a reasoning check, so nothing is marked. The two models differ on purpose: one opens with a question to the room, the other states the main idea first.


The bubbler, oval, tide and trial sentences are made up for the lesson.


Running the lesson
  1. Predict: read the sentence aloud once, then ask 'what do you remember?'.
  2. Try: ask what each signpost tells a listener.
  3. Worked example: ask a student to read Nib's third line aloud. Where does the class lose the thread?
  4. Vary: ask for the version a speaker would not want to say. Why not?
  5. Practise: students rewrite in their own document, then say it aloud to a partner.
  6. Make: partners read each other's four sentences aloud. The writer listens for where the reader trips.
The hard part (on purpose)
Students write a script as an essay and read it out. Ask them to read one sentence aloud: where did they run out of breath?
When students get stuck
  • A student reads the script silently and says it is fine. Ask: Could you say it aloud without stopping for breath? Fix: Say it aloud. Split where you ran out of breath.
  • A student's script is a list of short, disconnected sentences. Ask: How does a listener know how the sentences connect? Fix: Add a signpost word (first, so, this means) to link them.
  • A student explains the special word before using it. Ask: When does the listener need the meaning? Fix: Use the word, then say what it means, straight away.
Stretch and support
Stretch: Write the same four sentences for parents. Say which words you changed, and why.
Support: A card: One idea per sentence. Signpost words. Main idea twice. Say the word, then what it means.
Where it leads
Next: cutting the script down to cue cards, so you say it instead of reading it.


The same habits help in any subject's oral presentation. A science talk can say the key word and what it means in the same breath.


What each step is for
  • what do listeners keep?: Predicting what a listener holds from one hearing of a long written sentence sets up why scripts need a different style.
  • Try: find the signposts: Colouring the signpost words in a short script makes visible the words that carry a listener from one idea to the next.
  • Worked example: what a script does: Asking why a key idea is said twice, catching Nib's sentence that a listener would lose and judging a short sentence with a because in it show what writing for the ear leaves in and takes out.
  • Vary: the version you could say: Choosing the spoken version of each idea shows that the same facts can be set up for the eye or for the ear.
  • from the page to the ear: Reworking a long written sentence into several spoken ones makes students listen for where the breath runs out, not count words.
  • the start of a script: Writing an opening for a listener, explaining a special word and saying the main idea is open work that is compared with models, not marked.
  • In your own class: Reading a real piece of writing aloud and noticing where the voice runs out of breath shows students what to change in their own scripts.
  • Wider: a different listener: Rewriting the same script for a different listener keeps the skill and shows how the listener decides the words.

  • Explain a special word at the moment I use it.
