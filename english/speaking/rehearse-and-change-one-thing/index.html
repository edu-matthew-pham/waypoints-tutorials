Rehearse and change one thing

A rehearsal only helps if the listener says what they heard and the speaker changes something. Students tell specific feedback from vague feedback, turn vague comments into specific ones, choose the one change to make first and say how they will know it worked. The rehearsal with a partner happens in class, with a short checklist.

You say your talk to a partner. They say, 'That was good.' What do you change?

What you should already know:
  • Choose your register · Register is how formal or casual a speaker sounds, and whether they speak as 'I think' or state facts.
  • Plan a talk that has a point · A talk works when the speaker knows who is listening and what they should know, think or do afterwards.

Rehearsing is saying the talk aloud, to someone, before the real one. It finds what you cannot see yourself: a part that is too quiet, a word you trip on, a spot where you read from the cards.

Feedback helps when it is specific: it says what the listener heard or saw, and where.

  • Specific: 'You sped up on the numbers and I lost them.'
  • Vague: 'It was good.' 'Speak up.' 'Do it better.'

Then the speaker changes one thing and says it again. One change is enough to find out whether it worked. Three at once and you cannot tell which one helped.

A checklist for the listener

When you listen to a partner, use these questions. Tick what you saw and heard, then write one thing to keep and one thing to change.

  • I could hear every word, including the last sentence.
  • The speaker looked up from the cards for most of the talk.
  • The speaker slowed down or paused on the most important part.
  • The speaker's tone matched the words.
  • One thing to keep: …
  • One thing to change: …
How this lesson works: Predict, Try, Investigate, Modify, Practise, Make
  • Predict: Read the problem and decide what you think before you try anything. A wrong guess is fine: it shows you what to look for.
  • Try: Try it on the board and compare the result with your prediction.
  • Investigate: Work out why it happens, one step at a time.
  • Modify: Change the problem on purpose and see what happens.
  • Practise: A few on your own, to check you have it.
  • Make: Make your own problem using what you have learned. Expect a few tries; that is normal.

Choose the one change

Nib's partner gave four comments after a rehearsal. 1: 'I couldn't hear the last sentence.' 2: 'You looked at your cards for the whole of point two.' 3: 'The facts were clear.' 4: 'You said um a few times.' Choose the one change Nib should make first. Say what Nib will do differently and how Nib will know it worked.

Your working

A strong answer
Nib should fix comment 1 first, because the last sentence is the call to act, and if listeners can't hear it, the talk fails. Nib will take a breath before it and say it louder and slower. Nib will know it worked if the partner can repeat the sentence after the next rehearsal.
A different choice that also works
Nib should fix comment 2 first, because reading the cards stops Nib talking to the listeners for a whole point. Nib will learn point two so that only the key words are needed, then look up at the end of each sentence. Nib will know it worked if the partner says Nib looked up more in point two.

Wider: feedback in other places


For teachers
Students tell specific feedback from vague feedback, make a vague comment specific, choose the one change to make first and say how they will know it worked. They then rehearse with a partner in class using a short checklist, and tick the step when they have done it. About 45 minutes.
Open the teacher guide: answers, solutions, slides and a worksheet.
Students will be able to
  • I can tell feedback I can act on from feedback I cannot.
  • I can give a partner specific feedback about what I heard and saw.
  • I can change one thing and say whether it worked.
Before class
Do the Try board yourself: eight comments, two labels.


The one idea: feedback is useful when it names what was heard or seen, and one change is enough to learn from. This lesson covers AC9E4LY07 node 2 (a hinge node: rehearse with a peer, name one specific piece of voice feedback received, and make at least one change to delivery based on it) and AC9E8LY07 node 2 (rehearse and deliver for effect, and reflect on the intended and actual effect of voice choices).


The site covers the preparation and the self-check; the rehearsal happens in class. The site cannot listen to a rehearsal or a partner, and no step records one. The step 'Rehearse with a partner' is a tick the student gives once they have done it. For AC9E4LY07 node 2 the evidence that the student rehearsed with a peer, named a specific piece of feedback and made a change is what you see and hear in class, and what the student writes or says in the step. A short checklist for the listener is in the lesson text; print it or put it on the board. Part of AC9E8LY07 node 2 (delivering) is covered in the challenge lesson, in class. This is a case where the site gives partial coverage of a node; a marker for that is proposed in roadmap/english.md.


The Try and Practise boards label comments as specific or vague. Each comment has one defensible label: a specific comment says what was heard or seen and where; a vague one gives a verdict or a general instruction. The Vary board gives the specific rewording of each vague comment. The Make step is a reasoning check, so nothing is marked. Either of the two models is a good answer.


The comments, Nib and the partner are made up for the lesson. Use the checklist in pairs only; do not ask students to give feedback to the whole class or to be rated.


Running the lesson
  1. Predict: ask what Nib would do tomorrow because of 'That was good'.
  2. Try: ask for the question that tells you whether a comment is specific: 'what did they hear or see, and where?'.
  3. Worked example: ask students to turn Nib's entry 2 into a comment Nib could use.
  4. Vary: ask which rewording the speaker could act on in one minute.
  5. Rehearse in class: pairs rehearse a short talk or part of one. The listener uses the checklist and writes one thing to keep and one to change. The speaker changes one thing and says it again. Then the pair swap.
The hard part (on purpose)
Students give kind but vague feedback ('good job'). Ask 'what did you hear, and where?'. Students who receive it say 'okay' and change nothing.
When students get stuck
  • A student's feedback is 'it was good'. Ask: What was good? Where? Fix: Add the what and the where: 'The opening question got my attention.'
  • A student changes three things at once. Ask: If it got better, which change did it? Fix: Change one thing. Say it again. Then decide on the next change.
  • A student takes feedback as criticism and argues. Ask: What did your partner hear or see? Is it true? Fix: Ask the partner to say where it happened. Then try the change.
Stretch and support
Stretch: Rehearse a third time. Change a different thing and say which of the two changes helped more.
Support: The checklist in the lesson text. Sentence starter: 'When you … I could/couldn't …'.
Where it leads

The same feedback habit works in any subject: a science presentation, a drama scene, a sports skill.


What each step is for
  • what can they change?: Predicting what a speaker can do with a vague comment brings out that feedback has to name something to act on.
  • Try: specific or vague?: Labelling feedback comments as specific or vague gives students a test they can use on feedback they give and receive.
  • Worked example: feedback you can use: Asking what makes feedback useful, finding the vague comment in Nib's log and judging why to change one thing show rehearsal as a loop of listening and changing.
  • Vary: make it specific: Choosing the specific rewording of a vague comment shows what the more useful version says.
  • a second set: Labelling a second set of comments with no help from the first builds the habit of testing feedback for what it names.
  • choose the one change: Choosing one change from a list of specific comments and saying how to tell it worked is the open part of the skill, so it is compared with models, not marked.
  • Rehearse with a partner: The rehearsal itself happens in class. The site's step is the student's own report that they rehearsed, got specific feedback and changed one thing.
  • In your own class: Giving specific feedback to a partner, not just receiving it, builds the habit in both directions.
  • Wider: feedback in other places: Noticing specific and vague feedback in other settings keeps the skill and widens the examples.
