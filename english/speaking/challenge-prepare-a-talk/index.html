Challenge: prepare a talk

Students prepare a one-minute talk for a real audience in five parts: plan it, write the script, cut it to cue cards, rehearse with a partner and give it in class, then check the talk against the plan. The site plans, scripts and checks. Rehearsing and presenting happen in class, and a partner or the teacher judges the delivery.

A talk is a few minutes of speaking and a lot of choices behind it. Can you make every one of them on purpose?

What you should already know:
  • Rehearse and change one thing · A rehearsal only helps if the listener says what they heard and the speaker changes something.
  • Use your voice on purpose · A speaker has four voice settings: pace, volume, pitch and tone.

You will prepare a talk of about one minute for your class. Choose one change you would like to see in your school, such as the length of lunch, a new place to sit, or a club. Your listeners are your class, and your aim is for them to agree to try it.

You will use what you have learned in this course: a plan with a point, a script written for the ear, cue cards, a register that suits the listeners, a voice and visuals on purpose, and feedback from a rehearsal. The site helps you plan, script and check. You give the talk in class, to your teacher and your classmates. Nobody here can hear it, so a partner or your teacher judges the delivery.

How this lesson works: Purpose and audience, Plan, Script, Cue cards, Rehearse, Present and check
  • Purpose and audience: Who is listening, and what should they know, think or do afterwards? Decide before you plan.
  • Plan: Opening, two or three points, and a close. Plan the shape before you write a word.
  • Script: Write it to be heard: short sentences, one idea at a time.
  • Cue cards: Cut your script down to the key words you need to say it, not read it.
  • Rehearse: Say it aloud to a partner or a mirror, then change one thing.
  • Present and check: Present in class, then check it against your plan.

Explain it to me

A talk is prepared in steps: a plan with an aim, a script written for the ear, cue cards with the key words, a rehearsal with a partner and one change.

Then you present and check what you meant against what happened.

ScriptWrite it to be heard: short sentences, one idea at a time.

Script: write it to be heard

In your own document, write the opening and your first point as a script. Use four or more sentences, none of them 15 words or longer. Use at least one joining word such as because, when or until. Choose words and a register that suit your class.

Here is one example, from Nib's lunch talk: "Who here has ever wished lunch were a bit longer? Today I want to show you how to get ten more minutes. First, the queue wastes time. Last week the last student was served after nine minutes. That is nine minutes of lunch gone, because the line is slow."

Then say your script aloud to a partner. Could you say each sentence in one breath? Did your partner follow it the first time? Does your register suit your class?

Features: choose a voice and a visual

Choose one voice setting (pace, volume, pitch or tone) and one visual for your talk. For each, say where in your talk you will use it, and why it suits your purpose and your listeners.

Your working

A strong answer
Voice: I will slow down and get a little louder on 'one more window', because it is the request and I want my class to catch it. Visual: a bar chart of how long the queue takes each day, shown in point one. It adds the pattern, which my words would take too long to say, and it helps my class see that the problem is real.

Voice: I will use a warm, steady tone in my opening, because I want my class to feel I am on their side. Visual: a simple drawing of the canteen with the spare window marked, shown in point two. It adds the layout, so listeners can picture where the window is.

Present and checkPresent in class, then check it against your plan.
Present and check: against your plan
After you have given your talk in class, check it against your plan. Choose one voice choice you meant to make. Say what you meant it to do for your listeners, what it did, and what you would change next time.
 
Your working
A strong answer
I meant to slow down and pause before my close, 'Please try a quiet corner for a week.' I wanted my listeners to catch the request. What happened was that I paused, but I also sped up again straight after and some of the class looked away. Next time I would stay slow for the whole last sentence.
A different choice that also works
I meant to look up from my cards for the whole of point two. I wanted my listeners to feel I was talking to them. What happened was that I looked up for the first half and then read the cards again when I forgot a number. Next time I would put the number on the card in large print.

The one idea: the whole course in one piece of work. This lesson is tagged AC9E4LY07 node 3, AC9E6LY07 node 3 and AC9E8LY07 node 2, which are about delivering a talk, and AC9E8LY07 node 1, whose plan-and-justify part is the Features step (the register lesson is the other half). The site covers the preparing and the self-check; you see the delivery. Nothing here listens to a talk, and no step records one. Tick the node's criteria from what you see and hear in class: a clear structure with an opening and a close, at least one complex sentence said aloud, at least one deliberate voice choice (pace, volume, pitch or tone varied for effect), a theme with at least three supporting points (the plan board asks for three), and a precise topic word or two (nothing on the site teaches or checks topic vocabulary beyond the key words on the cards; this part of AC9E6LY07 node 3 is yours to hear). The script is written in the student's own document and nothing checks it, so look for at least one complex sentence when you read it, and listen for whether it is said aloud. The student's reasoning at the end gives their own account of the intended and actual effect of one voice choice, which is what AC9E8LY07 node 2 asks them to reflect on. A partial-coverage marker for steps like these is proposed in roadmap/english.md.


Choose the audience (the class, a Year 5 buddy class, parents at an event) and the time (about a minute). Students choose a change for the school, so the topic is theirs; no one is asked anything personal. Keep topics low-stakes and school-based. Nib's lunch example in the plan and cards boards is made up for the lesson.


What the boards count: the plan board counts filled lines and correct labels (a student could type anything, so read the plans); the cue board counts cards, words per card (limit 8) and key words as a share of the script (a quarter at most). Neither judges the content. The script step has no board: students write it in their own document and a partner or you read it. Whether the plan suits the class, whether the register fits and whether the script sounds natural are for a partner or you to judge by listening. Students paste their script into the cue board to cut it down.


Print cue cards gives index cards two across a page. Print highlighted script is there for a student who needs more on the page.


Running the lesson
  1. Predict: ask which of the earlier lessons the listener's verdict is about.
  2. Plan: partners say the aim back. If they cannot, it needs fixing before the script.
  3. Script: students read it aloud once, quietly, and mark where they run out of breath.
  4. Cue cards: cover the script and say one part from the cards. Add a word where they got stuck.
  5. Rehearse: pairs use the checklist, then change one thing and say it again. In class.
  6. Present: each student gives the talk, from cards. Listeners use the checklist. Allow a second go for anyone who wants it.
  7. Check: the reasoning step is written after presenting. Read a few to see what students noticed about their own voice.
The hard part (on purpose)
Students prepare well and then read the script. Ask them to put the script away before they present; the cards are the only page in their hand.
When students get stuck
  • A student reads the script aloud, word for word, when presenting. Ask: What are the cards for? Fix: Put the script away. Cards only. Pause and look up between parts.
  • A student's talk runs well over the time. Ask: Which point would your listeners miss least? Fix: Cut a point or a detail. A shorter talk said well is better.
  • A student freezes when presenting. Ask: What is the first key word on your first card? Fix: Offer a second go, perhaps to a partner first. Do not press.
Stretch and support
Stretch: Give the talk a second time to a different audience, such as Year 5, and change the register and one visual.
Support: The cue board's sample script, a checklist card, and a partner who listens first. Rehearse to a partner before the class.

The same preparing steps work for any subject's oral task: a science report, a history talk, a drama monologue.


What each step is for
  • Purpose and audience: what will go wrong?: Predicting what a listener says after a talk that ignores most of the course draws the earlier lessons together before students prepare their own.
  • Plan: opening, points, details and close: Writing a plan for a talk on a chosen change in the school puts the aim, the audience and the four parts of a plan together.
  • Script: write it to be heard: Writing the opening and first point of the script in their own document, then saying it aloud, shows whether the script is built for the ear, which only a spoken test can tell.
  • Cue cards: say it, don't read it: Cutting a script to cue cards with the limits from the cue-card lesson turns the written script into something a speaker can say.
  • Features: choose a voice and a visual: Choosing one voice setting and one visual for the talk, and justifying each for the listeners, is the plan-and-justify part of AC9E8LY07 n1 and is compared with models, not marked.
  • Rehearse: with a partner, in class: The rehearsal happens in class. The step is the student's own report that they rehearsed with a partner, used the checklist and changed one thing.
  • Present and check: against your plan: Comparing what the student planned with what happened, after the talk is given in class, builds the habit of reflecting on the effect of voice choices.
  • Wider: another talk: Planning a talk for a different purpose keeps the skill and shows how the plan changes with the purpose.
