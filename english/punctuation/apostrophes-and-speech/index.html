Apostrophes and speech marks

An apostrophe does one of two jobs: it stands in for letters that were left out (don't), or it shows who owns something. Speech marks wrap the words someone said, and the comma or question mark that ends them goes inside. Getting these right lets a reader see at a glance what was said, who said it and who owns what.

Same letters, different meaning: one dog or two? What can an apostrophe tell you?

An apostrophe (') does one of two jobs.

  • Letters left out: can't stands in for cannot; the apostrophe sits where the letters were.
  • Who owns it: the pilot's cap is the cap of one pilot. For more than one pilot, the apostrophe moves after the s: the pilots' cap.

Speech marks (' ') go around the exact words someone said. The comma, question mark or exclamation mark that ends the spoken words sits inside the closing mark: 'Time to go,' said Ravi. When the speaker's name comes in the middle, the speech carries on in lower case: 'We can leave,' said Ravi, 'if the bus comes.'

A scene with talk

In your own document, write a short scene from a story with two characters talking, at a bus stop or a campsite. Write at least four sentences. Put at least one piece of speech in speech marks, and use at least two apostrophes.

Here is one example: "The bus was late again. 'I can't wait any longer,' said Zoe. Her brother's bag sat on the bench beside her. 'We'll walk,' he said, 'if it doesn't rain.'"

Then swap with a partner. Point to each apostrophe and say its job: letters left out, or an owner. Check that every spoken part ends inside its closing speech mark.

For teachers

Students learn that an apostrophe either stands in for missing letters or shows who owns something, and that speech marks wrap spoken words with the closing punctuation inside. They choose words with apostrophes, match contractions, catch a stray apostrophe, choose speech punctuation, sort out its and it's and write a short scene with talk. About 45 minutes.

Open the teacher guide: answers, solutions, slides and a worksheet.

Students will be able to

  • I can say what an apostrophe is doing in a word.
  • I can put the apostrophe in the right place for one owner and for more than one.
  • I can tell a plain plural from an owner.
  • I can punctuate speech, including a said tag.

Before class

Do the Try board yourself: three gaps, three choices each.

The one idea: an apostrophe has two jobs, and a plain plural is not one of them. This lesson covers AC9E3LA11 nodes 1 and 2 (contractions, possession) and AC9E4LA12 nodes 1 and 2 (quotation marks, and the comma and capital letter conventions around a said tag). It stays at Years 3 to 4 level, so it can be skipped with a class that is secure.

Speech marks: the site uses single marks (the Australian style). Whether a class uses single or double is a style choice; keep one. The comma before a said tag sits inside the closing mark, and speech that continues after the speaker is named carries on in lower case.

The Make step has no board: students write the scene in their own document (Google Docs or OneNote) and swap with a partner, or you read it. Nothing they write is stored or checked by the site.

All sentences are made up for the lesson.

Running the lesson

  1. Predict: ask for the reason, 'whose bone is it?', before showing the answer.
  2. Try: after choosing, ask what the apostrophe would mean in the other choices.
  3. Worked example: Nib's report. Ask what 'giraffe's' would mean if you took the apostrophe literally.
  4. Vary: read each sentence aloud and ask where the speaker's voice stops.
  5. Make: partners point to each apostrophe and say its job.

The hard part (on purpose)

Students add an apostrophe to every word ending in s. Ask 'is a letter missing, does someone own something, or does it just mean more than one?' before they write it.

When students get stuck

  • A student writes 'the girl's bags' for three girls. Ask: How many girls own the bags? Fix: More than one owner: the apostrophe goes after the s, girls'.
  • A student writes 'it's tail' for the puppy's tail. Ask: Can you say it is tail? Fix: Its tail has no apostrophe, like his and hers. It's means it is.
  • A student puts the comma outside the closing speech mark. Ask: Is the comma part of what was said? Fix: It ends the spoken words, so it goes inside.

Stretch and support

Stretch: Take a paragraph from a class novel with speech in it and find every apostrophe and every said tag. What job does each do?

Support: A two-column card: letters left out on one side, owner on the other, with three examples each.

What each step is for

  • who owns the bone?: Predicting the possessive for one dog shows students already know the apostrophe has a job before they learn the rule.
  • Try: the right word: Choosing the correct word in each sentence shows the apostrophe either replaces letters or shows an owner.
  • Worked example: what the apostrophe is doing: Matching contractions to their full words, catching an apostrophe used to make a plural and placing speech marks shows what each mark is for.
  • Vary: speech punctuation: Choosing the mark for speech in sentences with the speaker named in different places shows the comma rule changes with where the speaker goes.
  • owners and missing letters: Mixing contractions, owners and a look-alike word in the same sentences makes students check each apostrophe's job.
  • a scene with talk: Writing a short scene with speech and apostrophes puts both marks to work in a real text type.

Opening phrases and shared ownersA long phrase at the start of a sentence, such as After the long walk through the bush, is followed by a comma so the reader knows where the main part begins. The same phrase at the end needs no comma. For owners, the apostrophe goes on the last name when two people share one thing (Mia and Ravi's story) and on each name when they each have their own (Mia's and Ravi's stories).
