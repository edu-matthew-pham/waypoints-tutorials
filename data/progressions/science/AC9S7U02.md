# AC9S7U02 — Food webs and ecosystems

**Year 7**
**Strand:** Understanding

## ACARA Descriptor

use models, including food webs, to represent matter and energy flow in ecosystems and predict the impact of changing abiotic and biotic factors on populations

## Prior Knowledge

Students can identify producers, consumers and decomposers and construct simple food chains. They have not studied energy flow quantitatively or modelled multi-factor ecosystem changes.

## Progression Endpoint

Students use food web models to represent matter and energy flow, and predict the impact of changes to abiotic and biotic factors on populations.

## Waypoint Sequence (5 waypoints, 3 hinges)

#### 1. From food chains to food webs ⚑

Students can construct a food web and identify the roles of producers, consumers and decomposers including microorganisms

**⚑ Hinge:** Students who cannot construct food webs with correct arrow direction and role assignment cannot represent matter and energy flow in Node 2 — the web structure is the scaffolding all later modelling depends on.

**Response modes:**
- ▣ Build a repertoire: Students need to construct food webs across multiple ecosystem types — aquatic, terrestrial, Australian — to build a reliable mental model of web structure. A single ecosystem example may be memorised rather than understood; multiple examples build the structural pattern recognition needed for prediction tasks in later nodes.

**Minimum width:** Construct a food web from a given organism list; identify producers, primary and secondary consumers, and decomposers including microorganisms

**Typical width:** Compare a food chain and food web for the same ecosystem — explain what information is lost in the simpler model

**Wider:**
- Analyse food webs to show feeding relationships including the role of microorganisms in decomposition
- Critique a published food web diagram — what is it missing, what assumptions does it make?
- Investigate First Nations Australians' food webs in a specific region and compare with a scientific representation of the same ecosystem

**Success criteria:**
- Correctly places all organisms in a food web into producer, consumer or decomposer roles
- Arrows show direction of energy flow, not just feeding relationships
- Includes microorganisms in the decomposer role

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7I04
- AC9S7H02

#### 2. Matter and energy flow ⚑

Students can explain the direction and loss of energy through trophic levels and distinguish energy flow from matter cycling

**⚑ Hinge:** Students who conflate matter cycling with energy flow make systematic errors in all population prediction nodes — they predict energy recovery rather than loss, producing confidently wrong cascade explanations in Nodes 3 and 4.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts: Energy flow direction and loss is the conceptual engine behind all population prediction tasks. Students who conflate matter cycling with energy flow make systematic errors in Nodes 3 and 4. Seeing energy flow across multiple ecosystem types — rainforest, desert, aquatic — forces students to apply the principle in new contexts rather than recall a single worked example, resolving the conflation misconception.

**Minimum width:** Explain the direction of energy flow in a food web; state that energy is lost at each trophic level; distinguish energy flow from matter cycling

**Typical width:** Use a given energy pyramid to explain why top predators are rare; connect to food web structure

**Wider:**
- Model how energy flows into and out of an ecosystem via food web pathways; represent quantitatively using the 10% rule
- Compare energy flow in two different ecosystems (e.g. rainforest vs desert); explain structural differences
- Analyse a real dataset of biomass at each trophic level; explain the pattern using energy flow principles

**Success criteria:**
- States that energy is lost at each trophic level
- Correctly distinguishes energy flow from matter cycling in an explanation
- Traces the direction of energy through at least 3 trophic levels

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7I04
- AC9S7I05

#### 3. Abiotic factors and population effects ★

Students can predict and explain how a change to an abiotic factor cascades through populations in a food web

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students commonly predict only the immediate effect of an abiotic change without tracing the cascade through the web. Scaffolding the multi-step reasoning explicitly — what changes first, what does that affect next, and what follows from that — builds the chain-of-reasoning habit needed for genuine prediction, not just first-order effect identification.

**Minimum width:** Define abiotic factor; give three examples; explain how one abiotic change would affect one population in a given food web

**Typical width:** Trace the cascade effect of one abiotic change (e.g. drought, temperature rise) through at least 3 populations with reasoning at each step

**Wider:**
- Examine how seasonal changes or habitat destruction impact abiotic factors and cause population changes
- Compare cascade effects of two different abiotic changes on the same ecosystem — which is more destabilising and why?
- Investigate First Nations Australians' fire management practices and their effect on abiotic factors and flora/fauna distribution

**Success criteria:**
- Correctly defines abiotic factor and gives 3 examples
- Predicts the effect of one abiotic change on one population with reasoning
- Identifies at least one other population affected by the cascade

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7I01
- AC9S7I05
- AC9S7H03

#### 4. Biotic factors and population effects ⚑ ★

Students can predict and explain how a change to a biotic factor cascades through populations in a food web

**⚑ Hinge:** Predicting biotic cascade effects requires holding the whole food web in mind simultaneously — the same multi-step reasoning skill required in AC9S9U03 (Carbon Cycle and Earth's Spheres), where students must trace carbon flow through biotic and abiotic ecosystem components. Students who haven't secured biotic cascade reasoning here will treat carbon flow as a simple transfer rather than a web of interdependent pathways.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts: Students commonly predict only the immediate effect of species removal without tracing the cascade through the web. Seeing more biotic change scenarios across different ecosystem types — predator removal, invasive species, pollinator loss — forces students to practise multi-step reasoning through food web structure rather than applying a single memorised cascade pattern.

**Minimum width:** Define biotic factor; give three examples; predict the effect of removing one species from a given food web with reasoning

**Typical width:** Analyse a trophic cascade case study (e.g. wolf reintroduction in Yellowstone); explain the chain of biotic effects with evidence

**Wider:**
- Predict effects on local ecosystems when pollinators or predators are removed or introduced
- Investigate First Nations Australians' responses to invasive species and their effect on food webs communities depend on
- Evaluate an invasive species scenario — construct food webs before and after, predict population effects, and propose a management response

**Success criteria:**
- Correctly defines biotic factor and gives 3 examples
- Predicts the effect of removing one species with food web reasoning
- Traces at least one cascade effect beyond the immediate population

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7I01
- AC9S7I05
- AC9S7I07
- AC9S7H03
- AC9S7H04

#### 5. Models, limitations and predictions

Students can evaluate the limitations of food web models and explain how these affect the reliability of predictions

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students commonly identify model limitations as abstract statements ('it doesn't include everything') without reasoning through how a specific limitation affects a specific prediction. Scaffolding the connection — what is missing from this model, and what prediction would be wrong as a result — builds genuine model evaluation rather than generic limitation-listing.

**Minimum width:** Identify one limitation of food web models; explain how this affects the reliability of predictions made from them

**Typical width:** Compare a simple and complex model of the same ecosystem — explain what each can and cannot predict

**Wider:**
- Evaluate a real scientific food web model used in a conservation decision — what did it predict, what did it miss?
- Consider how introduction of a species impacts abiotic and biotic factors beyond immediate food web effects
- Research how First Nations ecological knowledge has improved or challenged scientific food web models in a specific case

**Success criteria:**
- Identifies at least one limitation of food web models
- Explains how that limitation affects the reliability of a specific prediction
