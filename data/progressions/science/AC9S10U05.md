# AC9S10U05 — Newton's laws of motion

**Year 10**
**Strand:** Physical Sciences

## ACARA Descriptor

investigate Newton's laws of motion and quantitatively analyse the relationship between force, mass and acceleration of objects

## Prior Knowledge

Students can represent and explain how balanced and unbalanced forces, including gravity, relate to changes in an object's motion in terms of mass, magnitude and direction (AC9S7U04). There is a two-year gap in the physical sciences strand (Years 8–9 address energy, not force/motion), so force and motion knowledge has had no curriculum reinforcement for three years. Students carry the Aristotelian misconception that moving objects require a continuous applied force to maintain motion. They have no prior exposure to Newton's named laws, acceleration as a defined quantity, F=ma as a quantitative relationship, or action-reaction pairs.

## Progression Endpoint

Students apply Newton's three laws of motion to analyse object behaviour and quantitatively solve problems using F=ma, including multi-force scenarios, free body diagrams, and real-world applications such as vehicle safety features and sport.

## Waypoint Sequence (8 waypoints, 3 hinges)

#### 1. Force as a vector quantity

Students can represent forces as vectors with both magnitude and direction, and add or subtract forces acting along a single axis to find net force.

**Response modes:**
- ◆ Practise here: Students covered force magnitude and direction at Year 7, but three years without reinforcement means vector representation and sign conventions are likely degraded. This node re-establishes the representational tools that all quantitative work in N4 and N6 depends on — precision here prevents systematic sign errors throughout.

**Minimum width:** Students represent a named force as an arrow showing magnitude and direction, and calculate net force for two forces acting along the same line.

**Typical width:** Students represent multiple forces on a single object; calculate net force for forces in the same and opposite directions; distinguish between contact and non-contact forces; use consistent sign conventions for direction.

**Wider:**
- Represent forces on objects in two dimensions and estimate resultant direction — what happens when forces are not perfectly aligned?
- Investigate how First Nations Australians' use of spearthrowers applies directional force principles to achieve greater projectile speed and impact

**Success criteria:**
- Represents a named force as a vector with a labelled magnitude and direction
- Calculates net force for two forces acting along the same axis using a consistent sign convention
- Distinguishes between contact forces (friction, normal) and non-contact forces (gravity)

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U04

#### 2. Newton's First Law — inertia and the Aristotelian misconception ⚑

Students can state Newton's First Law and explain why a moving object with no net force continues at constant velocity, identifying and correcting the misconception that motion requires a continuous force.

**⚑ Hinge:** The Aristotelian misconception — that motion requires a continuous force — directly undermines Newton's Second and Third Laws if unresolved. Students who hold this view interpret F=ma as 'force produces motion' rather than 'net force produces change in motion', causing systematic errors in N4 and N6. Students who cannot separate constant velocity from acceleration also cannot correctly define acceleration in N3.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: The Aristotelian misconception is well-documented and persistent — it survives exposure to the correct definition without explicit confrontation. Students need scenarios where the intuitive 'force causes motion' framing produces a wrong prediction, followed by correction using Newton's First Law. Friction as the hidden force explains almost every everyday 'counterexample' students produce.

**Minimum width:** Students state that an object continues at constant velocity (or remains at rest) when the net force is zero, and explain one everyday scenario where this is counterintuitive (e.g. a sliding hockey puck).

**Typical width:** Students apply Newton's First Law to multiple scenarios including objects at rest and in constant motion; explain inertia as resistance to change in motion; describe how friction explains why objects appear to slow down without a visible opposing force; connect to balanced force concept from Year 7.

**Wider:**
- Analyse why inertia makes seatbelts necessary — trace what happens to an unrestrained passenger when a car stops suddenly
- Investigate historical resistance to Galileo's and Newton's ideas — why was the Aristotelian view so persistent, and what evidence overturned it?

**Success criteria:**
- States Newton's First Law correctly
- Explains why a moving object without net force continues at constant velocity (does not slow down)
- Identifies friction as the hidden force responsible for observed deceleration in everyday scenarios
- Corrects the claim that 'a moving object needs a force to keep moving'

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U04

#### 3. Acceleration as a defined vector quantity ⚑ ★

Students can define acceleration as the rate of change of velocity, calculate acceleration from velocity and time data, and represent acceleration as a vector with direction.

**⚑ Hinge:** Acceleration is used in every subsequent quantitative context. Students who conflate acceleration with speed, or who cannot assign direction to acceleration, produce systematic errors in F=ma calculations (N4), multi-force problems (N6), and all real-world applications (N7). The shift from everyday language ('speeding up') to a defined vector quantity is a representation change that does not happen incidentally.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Acceleration must shift from an everyday colloquial term to a defined vector quantity with units. Students who passed Year 7 with a qualitative understanding ('unbalanced forces change motion') need this redefinition made explicit before F=ma is introduced — otherwise they substitute intuitive acceleration for the defined quantity in calculations.
- ↔ Widen contexts: Multiple representational contexts — data tables, velocity-time graphs, motion sensor outputs — build the mathematical literacy the descriptor's 'quantitatively analyse' demand requires. Each context exercises the same defined quantity from a different angle.

**Minimum width:** Students calculate acceleration using a = Δv/t for a single scenario and state the direction of acceleration relative to net force.

**Typical width:** Students calculate acceleration from distance-time and velocity-time graphs; distinguish between speeding up, slowing down and changing direction as forms of acceleration; explain deceleration as acceleration in the direction opposite to motion; apply correct units.

**Wider:**
- Construct and interpret velocity-time graphs from motion sensor data, reading acceleration from the gradient
- Explain why a car travelling at constant speed around a curve is still accelerating — and predict the direction of the net force required

**Success criteria:**
- Defines acceleration as the rate of change of velocity with correct units (m/s²)
- Calculates acceleration from velocity and time data
- States the direction of acceleration and identifies it as the same direction as net force
- Distinguishes acceleration from speed — identifies deceleration and direction change as forms of acceleration

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U04

#### 4. Newton's Second Law — F = ma ⚑ ★

Students can apply F = ma to calculate force, mass or acceleration in single-force scenarios and explain qualitatively how changing mass or force affects acceleration.

**⚑ Hinge:** F = ma is the quantitative engine of all subsequent nodes. Students who cannot reliably apply the relationship — particularly students who confuse net force with applied force, or who drop direction — cannot solve multi-force problems (N6) or analyse real-world applications (N7). Errors introduced here propagate into every calculation context.

**Response modes:**
- ◆ Practise here: Procedural fluency with F = ma — correct formula rearrangement, unit tracking, direction assignment — is a prerequisite for multi-force work. Students need practice and self-correction at the single-force level before net force scenarios are introduced.
- ↔ Widen contexts: The elaborations name multiple distinct application contexts: vehicle stopping distances, athlete performance, driverless vehicle braking. Each applies F = ma to a different object with different known and unknown quantities — genuine width rather than repeated practice.

**Minimum width:** Students correctly apply F = ma to solve for one unknown (F, m, or a) in a single-force scenario with correct units.

**Typical width:** Students solve F = ma for all three variables across multiple contexts; explain proportional relationships (double the force → double the acceleration; double the mass → halve the acceleration); use graphs to represent F–a relationships at constant mass; apply correct units throughout.

**Wider:**
- Investigate the F–a relationship experimentally using trolleys and hanging masses; plot results and assess linearity
- Analyse the force required to bring a vehicle to a stop within a given distance — connect to the elaboration on speed limits near schools

**Success criteria:**
- Correctly applies F = ma to solve for force, mass or acceleration in a single-force scenario
- Uses correct units (N, kg, m/s²) and maintains direction consistency
- Explains qualitatively how doubling force or mass affects acceleration

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U04
- AC9S8U05

#### 5. Newton's Third Law — action-reaction pairs

Students can identify action-reaction force pairs in a scenario and explain why they do not cancel, despite being equal and opposite.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: The persistent Third Law misconception — that equal and opposite forces cancel and therefore nothing should move — must be addressed by explicitly establishing that action-reaction pairs act on different objects. Scenarios where only one object is being analysed (e.g. a person jumping) help isolate the relevant force before students encounter multi-body analysis.

**Minimum width:** Students identify the action and reaction forces in one scenario (e.g. foot pushing on ground, ground pushing back on foot) and explain that they act on different objects.

**Typical width:** Students identify action-reaction pairs across multiple scenarios (rocket thrust, swimming, jumping); explain why the pairs do not cancel using the 'different objects' principle; distinguish Third Law pairs from balanced forces acting on the same object.

**Wider:**
- Analyse propulsion in a rocket, jet engine or swimmer using Newton's Third Law — predict direction and relative magnitude of each force pair
- Investigate a common Third Law misconception: 'the heavier object exerts more force' — design a demonstration that tests this prediction

**Success criteria:**
- Identifies the action and reaction forces in a named scenario and states which object each acts on
- Explains why action-reaction pairs do not cancel (they act on different objects)
- Distinguishes Third Law pairs from balanced forces acting on the same object

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U04

#### 6. Multi-force analysis and free body diagrams ★

Students can draw a free body diagram for an object with multiple forces and calculate net force, then apply F = ma to determine acceleration.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts: Free body diagrams become meaningful across varied force configurations — not just 'a box on a table' but objects on inclines, in vehicles, in sport contexts. Width here builds the representational flexibility students need for unseen problems.

**Minimum width:** Students draw a free body diagram for a scenario with two or three forces and calculate net force and resulting acceleration.

**Typical width:** Students apply free body diagrams to objects at rest, constant velocity, and accelerating; include normal force, friction, gravity, and applied force; calculate net force and acceleration for multi-step scenarios; explain how airbags, seatbelts and crumple zones extend stopping time to reduce force.

**Wider:**
- Analyse a vehicle collision scenario: given stopping distance and mass, calculate impact force with and without crumple zone — evaluate the safety benefit quantitatively
- Design a free body diagram for a multi-phase motion (e.g. a rocket launching then coasting) — identify when each law applies and when net force changes

**Success criteria:**
- Draws a correctly labelled free body diagram for an object with at least three forces
- Calculates net force and resulting acceleration from a free body diagram
- Applies the result to explain a real-world scenario (e.g. vehicle safety, sport)

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U04
- AC9S8U05

#### 7. Quantitative problem solving — integrating all three laws

Students can solve multi-step force and motion problems that require selecting and applying Newton's laws, using mathematical representations including graphs and algebraic formulas.

**Response modes:**
- ◆ Practise here: Multi-step problem solving requires procedural fluency across several interconnected relationships. Students need structured practice selecting the right law for each step, tracking units across calculations, and checking answers for physical reasonableness before assessment-level problems.

**Minimum width:** Students solve a two-step problem that requires applying at least two of Newton's laws and uses both F = ma and a kinematic relationship (e.g. a = Δv/t).

**Typical width:** Students solve multi-step problems across contexts: vehicles, athletes, objects under gravity; select appropriate laws and formulas; represent solutions graphically; construct arguments supported by quantitative data (e.g. speed limit near schools); check reasonableness of answers.

**Wider:**
- Construct a data-supported argument for a lower speed limit in a school zone — include stopping distance calculations and force analysis at different speeds
- Investigate how driverless vehicle braking systems apply Newton's laws — what sensor data and calculations does the system need to brake in time?

**Success criteria:**
- Solves a multi-step problem applying at least two of Newton's laws with correct units throughout
- Uses mathematical representations (graphs or algebraic formulas) to support a quantitative argument
- Checks the reasonableness of a calculated answer in physical terms

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U04

#### 8. Real-world applications — Newton's laws in context ★

Students can apply Newton's laws to analyse motion in real-world contexts including vehicle safety, sport, and First Nations technologies, and explain design decisions in terms of force, mass and acceleration.

**Response modes:**
- ⊕ Integration point: This node is the full descriptor demand: students bringing all three laws together to investigate and explain motion in contexts not previously analysed. The integration is across laws (selecting the right one), representations (quantitative, diagrammatic, verbal), and contexts (cultural, technological, sporting).
- ↔ Widen contexts: The elaborations name contexts that are genuinely distinct — First Nations spearthrowers, airbags, driverless braking, sport. Each offers a different force configuration and a different design question. This is width, not depth — students are applying the same laws to new situations, not learning new physics.

**Minimum width:** Students apply at least one of Newton's laws to explain a design feature or technique in a real-world context (e.g. why airbags reduce injury, why a longer spearthrower increases projectile speed).

**Typical width:** Students analyse multiple application contexts connecting Newton's laws to design intent; explain how spearthrowers extend force application time and distance to achieve greater acceleration; evaluate safety features quantitatively; compare athletic techniques in terms of force, mass and acceleration; connect to elaboration contexts (sport, driverless vehicles, First Nations technologies).

**Wider:**
- Investigate a specific First Nations throwing technology (spearthrower or bow) and analyse the force-extension mechanism using Newton's Second and Third Laws
- Propose and justify a design modification to a vehicle safety feature using Newton's laws — what change would improve occupant protection, and why?

**Success criteria:**
- Applies at least two of Newton's laws to explain a design feature or technique in a real-world context
- Explains a First Nations throwing technology in terms of force, time of application and resulting acceleration
- Evaluates a vehicle safety feature quantitatively using Newton's Second Law

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U04
- AC9S8U05
