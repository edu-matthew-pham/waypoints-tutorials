# AC9S8U06 — Elements, compounds and mixtures

**Year 8**
**Strand:** Chemical Sciences

## ACARA Descriptor

classify matter as elements, compounds or mixtures and compare different representations of these, including 2-dimensional and 3-dimensional models, symbols for elements and formulas for molecules and compounds

## Prior Knowledge

Students can describe observable material properties (AC9SFU03), recognise physical change preserves material composition (AC9S2U03), explain solid/liquid/gas properties using a particle model (AC9S5U04), distinguish pure substances from mixtures and apply separation techniques (AC9S7U06), and describe particle arrangement, motion and attraction forces (AC9S7U05). The particle model treats particles as undifferentiated — particles have no identity (atom type). The concept of atom type, element as one atom type, and compound as chemically combined different atom types are all new. Chemical symbols and molecular formulas are new.

## Progression Endpoint

Students classify matter as elements (one atom type), compounds (two or more atom types chemically combined in fixed ratios) or mixtures (substances not chemically combined), and use chemical symbols, formulas and 2D/3D models to represent each — selecting representations based on what information each conveys.

## Waypoint Sequence (5 waypoints, 2 hinges)

#### 1. Atoms and atom types — giving the particle model identity ⚑

Students can explain that particles in the particle model are atoms, that atoms have type (element identity), and that different substances are made of different atom types.

**⚑ Hinge:** The atom-type extension to the particle model is the conceptual prerequisite for both elements (N2) and compounds (N3). Students who treat all particles as identical — as in the Y5/Y7 particle model — cannot understand why elements are defined by atom type or why chemically combining different atom types produces a new substance with new properties.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: The shift from undifferentiated particles to typed atoms is the key conceptual move in this standard. Students need this extension made explicit — the Y7 particle model is being upgraded, not replaced. Making the upgrade visible ('you know particles exist; now we add: particles have type') prevents confusion about which model applies where.

**Minimum width:** Students explain that atoms are the particles in the particle model and that different elements consist of different types of atoms.

**Typical width:** Students connect to AC9S7U05's particle model — those particles are atoms; explain that atom type determines what element a substance is; introduce the idea that there are ~118 known element types; explain that atoms of the same element are identical; describe atoms as the smallest unit of an element that retains its chemical identity; note that this is a model — atoms have internal structure (introduced in AC9S9U06).

**Wider:**
- Trace the historical development of the element concept from Democritus through Dalton — what changed in the model and what evidence drove each change?
- Use virtual or physical models to distinguish between different atom types — what makes a carbon atom different from an oxygen atom at this level of model?

**Success criteria:**
- Explains that the particles in the particle model are atoms
- States that different elements consist of different types of atoms
- Explains that atom type determines element identity

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U05
- AC9S5U04

#### 2. Elements — one atom type, symbols and the periodic table

Students can define elements as pure substances consisting of only one type of atom and use chemical symbols to represent them.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Elements are the simplest category — one atom type — and periodic table fluency (looking up symbols, reading position) is a tool students will use throughout secondary chemistry. The historical development of the table connects to the evidence-based nature of scientific models.

**Minimum width:** Students define an element and correctly use symbols for at least five elements.

**Typical width:** Students define elements in terms of atomic composition (one type of atom only); use the periodic table as a reference for symbols; explain that symbols are internationally standardised shorthand; examine how Mendeleev arranged elements and compare to the current table; describe element properties varying across the table; connect element symbols to naming conventions (first letter capitalised, second lowercase).

**Wider:**
- Examine Mendeleev's original periodic table and compare it to the current version — what principle drove the reorganisation from atomic mass to atomic number?
- Investigate an unfamiliar element from the periodic table — predict its properties from its position and research whether the prediction holds

**Success criteria:**
- Defines an element as a pure substance consisting of only one type of atom
- Correctly uses symbols for at least five elements
- Uses the periodic table to locate element symbols and names

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U05

#### 3. Compounds — different atom types chemically combined ⚑

Students can define compounds as substances containing two or more atom types chemically combined in fixed ratios, and use formulas to represent them.

**⚑ Hinge:** The compound definition — chemically combined, new properties, fixed ratio — is the conceptual distinction that separates compounds from mixtures. Students who conflate compounds with mixtures (treating both as 'atoms of different types together') cannot understand why chemical reactions produce genuinely new substances in AC9S8U07, and cannot apply conservation of mass in AC9S9U07.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: The NaCl vs Na + Cl₂ property comparison is the most effective way to make chemical combination concrete. Students need to encounter the dramatic property change before the compound/mixture distinction is introduced — properties are the observable evidence that chemical combination has occurred.

**Minimum width:** Students define a compound and interpret a formula (e.g. H₂O) to identify atom types and ratios.

**Typical width:** Students define compounds: two or more different atom types, chemically bonded, fixed ratio, new substance with different properties from constituent elements; write and interpret formulas for H₂O, CO₂, NaCl, C₆H₁₂O₆; explain subscript notation as atom ratio; contrast compound properties with constituent element properties (NaCl is edible; Na is dangerously reactive, Cl₂ is toxic); explain that chemical combination is permanent under normal conditions.

**Wider:**
- Compare the properties of NaCl to its constituent elements (Na metal and Cl₂ gas) — what does the dramatic property change reveal about the nature of chemical combination?
- Use virtual molecular models to build compound structures and compare the information in the formula to the 3D model

**Success criteria:**
- Defines a compound as two or more atom types chemically combined in fixed ratios
- Interprets a chemical formula to identify atom types and their ratios
- Explains that compounds have different properties from their constituent elements

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U05
- AC9S6U04

#### 4. Mixtures — substances physically combined, properties retained

Students can define mixtures, explain that components retain their properties and can be separated by physical means, and distinguish mixtures from compounds.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Mixture connects directly to AC9S7U06's separation knowledge and extends it with the types classification. The key contrast with compounds — retained properties, variable composition, physical separability — must be explicitly stated. Particle diagram comparison (bonded vs unbonded atoms) makes the compound/mixture distinction visible at the model level.

**Minimum width:** Students define a mixture and explain two differences from a compound.

**Typical width:** Students describe solutions, suspensions and colloids; explain that mixture components retain individual properties; explain variable composition (unlike fixed ratio in compounds); explain physical separability (connects to AC9S7U06 separation techniques); represent mixtures using particle diagrams — different atom types present but not bonded; contrast with compound diagram — atoms bonded in fixed arrangement.

**Wider:**
- Represent the same substance (e.g. salt water) as a particle diagram and as a formula-based representation — explain what each shows and what each conceals
- Investigate how indicators of mixture type are used in practical testing contexts — soil pH, water quality, medical diagnostics

**Success criteria:**
- Defines a mixture and gives examples of solutions, suspensions and colloids
- Explains that mixture components retain their individual properties
- Identifies at least two differences between a mixture and a compound

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U05
- AC9S7U06

#### 5. Representations — comparing symbols, formulas and 2D/3D models

Students can compare representations of elements, compounds and mixtures and explain what information each representation conveys and where each is most useful.

**Response modes:**
- ⊕ Integration point: This node integrates element/compound/mixture classification (N1–N4) with the representational literacy the descriptor explicitly requires. Students evaluate representations as purpose-specific tools — the 'compare different representations' demand of the descriptor.

**Minimum width:** Students identify what information is shown by a symbol, a formula and a 3D model for a named substance, and explain one advantage of each.

**Typical width:** Students compare: symbols (identity, periodic table position — no structural info); molecular formulas (atom types and ratios, compact — no spatial info); structural formulas (bonding connections, 2D arrangement); 3D ball-and-stick/space-filling models (spatial arrangement, bond angles, relative size); particle diagrams for mixtures (relative proportions, unbonded); evaluate which representation is most useful for a given purpose; explain that no single representation captures everything.

**Wider:**
- Examine multiple representations of glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) — symbol, molecular formula, structural formula, 3D model — and identify what each reveals and conceals about the molecule
- Compare physical and digital 3D molecular models of the same compound and evaluate each for accuracy, utility and accessibility

**Success criteria:**
- Identifies what information a symbol, formula and 3D model each convey for a named substance
- Explains one advantage and one limitation of each representation type
- Selects the most appropriate representation for a given purpose and justifies the choice

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U05
- AC9S7U06
