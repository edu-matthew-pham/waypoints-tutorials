# AC9S10U07 — Reaction types and rates

**Year 10**
**Strand:** Chemical Sciences

## ACARA Descriptor

identify patterns in synthesis, decomposition and displacement reactions and investigate the factors that affect reaction rates

## Prior Knowledge

Students can model atom rearrangement in chemical reactions using word and balanced equations and demonstrate conservation of mass (AC9S9U07). They can compare physical and chemical changes and identify energy change indicators (AC9S8U07). They understand elements, compounds and mixtures and can use chemical symbols and formulas (AC9S8U06). They have not yet categorised reactions by structural type (synthesis, decomposition, displacement), identified patterns within those categories, or examined what variables control how fast reactions proceed.

## Progression Endpoint

Students identify structural patterns in synthesis, decomposition and single and double displacement reactions, predict reaction products from type, and explain how temperature, concentration, surface area and catalysts affect reaction rates in terms of particle collision theory.

## Waypoint Sequence (6 waypoints, 2 hinges)

#### 1. Synthesis reactions — combining reactants to form a single product

Students can identify synthesis reactions by their structural pattern (A + B → AB) and predict the product from given reactants.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students need to recognise reaction type from structural pattern — not just from naming or memorisation. Synthesis is the most structurally transparent type (multiple reactants → one product) and provides the reference point for contrasting decomposition and displacement in subsequent nodes.

**Minimum width:** Students identify the synthesis pattern from a given equation and write a word equation for one synthesis reaction.

**Typical width:** Students identify synthesis reactions across multiple examples (metal + oxygen, hydrogen + oxygen, sodium + chlorine); write word and symbolic equations; predict the product of a named synthesis reaction; explain why synthesis reactions are used in manufacturing useful compounds; connect to balanced equation skills from AC9S9U07.

**Wider:**
- Investigate industrial synthesis reactions (e.g. Haber process for ammonia) and explain why controlling reaction conditions is economically important
- Investigate chemical reactions employed by First Nations Australians in production of substances — identify which reaction type(s) are involved

**Success criteria:**
- Identifies a synthesis reaction by its structural pattern (two or more reactants → one product)
- Writes a correct word equation for a named synthesis reaction
- Predicts the product of a synthesis reaction from given reactants

**Related standards:**
- AC9S9U07
- AC9S8U06

#### 2. Decomposition reactions — breaking a compound into simpler products

Students can identify decomposition reactions by their structural pattern (AB → A + B) and describe conditions under which decomposition reactions occur.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Decomposition is the structural inverse of synthesis and is most clearly understood in contrast to it. Students who can identify both patterns have a binary classification tool they can extend to displacement in N3.

**Minimum width:** Students identify the decomposition pattern from a given equation and describe one condition (heat or electricity) that drives a named decomposition reaction.

**Typical width:** Students identify decomposition reactions across multiple examples (thermal decomposition of carbonates, electrolysis of water, decomposition used in metal extraction); write word and symbolic equations; explain why energy input is typically required; predict products of simple decomposition reactions; contrast with synthesis structurally.

**Wider:**
- Investigate electrolysis as a decomposition method and explain how it is used to extract reactive metals (e.g. aluminium from bauxite)
- Examine First Nations Australians' methods for converting toxic plants into edible food — identify any decomposition-type reactions involved in these processes

**Success criteria:**
- Identifies a decomposition reaction by its structural pattern (one reactant → two or more products)
- Describes at least one condition (heat or electrical energy) that drives a decomposition reaction
- Writes a correct word equation for a named decomposition reaction

**Related standards:**
- AC9S9U07
- AC9S8U06

#### 3. Displacement reactions — patterns of reactivity and product prediction ⚑ ★

Students can identify single and double displacement reactions and use reactivity patterns to predict whether a displacement reaction will occur and what the products will be.

**⚑ Hinge:** Displacement reactions are structurally more complex than synthesis or decomposition — both single and double displacement require pattern recognition and the activity series adds a predictive layer. Students who conflate displacement with synthesis/decomposition, or who cannot use reactivity to predict whether a reaction proceeds, cannot integrate across all three reaction types in N4 or apply the classification tool to unseen reactions.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Single displacement requires an additional decision — will this reaction occur? — that synthesis and decomposition do not. Students need the activity series as a tool and must understand what it encodes (relative reactivity based on electron-giving tendency) before they can use it predictively. This is a new reasoning move, not just pattern recognition.
- ↔ Widen contexts: Metal + acid, metal + salt, neutralisation and precipitation are structurally distinct displacement contexts. Each exercises the type-identification and prediction skills from a different angle, and each maps to real-world applications named in the elaborations.

**Minimum width:** Students identify a single displacement reaction from a given equation and explain why a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one.

**Typical width:** Students identify single displacement reactions (metal + acid, metal + salt solution) and double displacement reactions (precipitation, neutralisation); use an activity series to predict whether single displacement will occur; write word and symbolic equations; explain neutralisation as a double displacement producing water and a salt; identify the precipitate in a double displacement reaction.

**Wider:**
- Investigate a metal reactivity series experimentally — place metals in acid or salt solutions and rank them by reactivity; compare to the published activity series
- Examine First Nations Australians' production of acids or ethanol and identify which reaction type(s) are involved, predicting products from the reaction pattern

**Success criteria:**
- Identifies single and double displacement reactions from given equations
- Uses an activity series to predict whether a single displacement reaction will occur
- Predicts the products of a neutralisation or precipitation reaction

**Related standards:**
- AC9S9U07
- AC9S8U07

#### 4. Reaction type patterns — classifying and predicting across all three types

Students can classify an unfamiliar chemical reaction as synthesis, decomposition or displacement and predict products, using structural patterns rather than memorisation.

**Response modes:**
- ⊕ Integration point: This node consolidates the three reaction type nodes into a classification tool that students apply to unfamiliar reactions. The move from 'identify this named reaction' to 'classify this unfamiliar equation' is the integration demand that completes the 'identify patterns' half of the descriptor.

**Minimum width:** Students correctly classify three unfamiliar reactions (one of each type) and predict the products for each.

**Typical width:** Students apply classification to a range of equations in varied formats (word, symbolic, molecular model); identify reaction type from reactant and product patterns alone; explain the classification logic rather than pattern-matching from memory; connect reaction type to useful products (elaboration context).

**Wider:**
- Analyse a multi-step reaction sequence and classify each step — what types are involved in a commercial process such as soap-making or metal extraction?
- Evaluate the claim that 'all chemical reactions can be classified into these three types' — are there reactions that do not fit cleanly?

**Success criteria:**
- Classifies an unfamiliar reaction as synthesis, decomposition or displacement using structural pattern
- Predicts products for each reaction type from the pattern alone
- Explains the classification reasoning rather than recalling named examples

**Related standards:**
- AC9S9U07

#### 5. Collision theory — the particle basis of reaction rates ⚑

Students can explain reaction rate in terms of particle collision frequency and energy, and use collision theory to predict how changing conditions will affect rate.

**⚑ Hinge:** Collision theory is the explanatory model for all four rate factors. Students who lack the collision model learn rate factors as isolated memorised rules (heat = faster, more acid = faster) without a unifying explanation. They cannot transfer to an unseen factor, cannot explain WHY each variable works, and cannot use the model to design investigations.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Collision theory requires students to think about reactions at the particle level — a level below what is directly observable. Students need the mental model established explicitly before the four rate factors are introduced, or the factors remain disconnected facts.

**Minimum width:** Students state that chemical reactions require particles to collide with sufficient energy, and explain why more frequent or more energetic collisions increase reaction rate.

**Typical width:** Students describe that only collisions with energy above the activation energy threshold result in reaction; explain why concentration, temperature, surface area and catalysts each increase collision frequency or energy; represent the relationship between collision theory and observable reaction rate.

**Wider:**
- Design an investigation to test the effect of one variable (temperature, concentration or surface area) on reaction rate — predict using collision theory and evaluate against results
- Examine how catalysts work at the particle level — why do they lower activation energy without being consumed?

**Success criteria:**
- Explains that reactions require particle collisions with energy above the activation energy threshold
- Predicts that increasing collision frequency or energy increases reaction rate
- Identifies at least two ways to increase collision frequency or energy

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U05
- AC9S9U07

#### 6. Rate factors — temperature, concentration, surface area and catalysts ★

Students can explain how each of the four rate factors affects reaction rate using collision theory, and investigate one factor experimentally.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts: Each rate factor is a distinct investigative context and the elaborations explicitly expect investigation. Applying collision theory to each factor in turn — rather than listing them — builds the explanatory fluency the descriptor's 'investigate' demand requires.

**Minimum width:** Students explain how two of the four rate factors affect rate using collision theory language (collision frequency or energy).

**Typical width:** Students explain all four factors using collision theory: temperature (increases particle kinetic energy → more collisions exceed activation energy); concentration (more particles per volume → more frequent collisions); surface area (more exposed particles → more collision opportunities); catalysts (lower activation energy → greater proportion of collisions succeed); distinguish catalyst from reactant.

**Wider:**
- Investigate the effect of temperature or concentration on a visible reaction (e.g. sodium thiosulfate + HCl, or hydrogen peroxide + yeast) and quantitatively analyse the rate change
- Examine reactions employed by First Nations Australians — identify which rate factors are being exploited in a specific food preparation or substance production technique

**Success criteria:**
- Explains how each of the four rate factors affects reaction rate using collision theory
- Distinguishes catalysts from reactants and explains why catalysts are not consumed
- Designs or describes an investigation to test the effect of one rate factor

**Related standards:**
- AC9S9U07
- AC9S7U05
