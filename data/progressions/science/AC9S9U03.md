# AC9S9U03 — Carbon cycle and earth's spheres

**Year 9**
**Strand:** Earth and Space Sciences

## ACARA Descriptor

represent the carbon cycle and examine how key processes including combustion, photosynthesis and respiration rely on interactions between Earth's spheres (the geosphere, biosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere)

## Prior Knowledge

Students can describe Earth's surface changes (AC9S5U02), model how Earth's tilt and revolution produce seasonal change (AC9S6U02), and compare reversible and irreversible changes including combustion (AC9S6U04). They can use food web models to represent matter and energy flow through ecosystems (AC9S7U02) and explain plate tectonics and rock cycle processes (AC9S8U03, AC9S8U04). They have not yet encountered Earth as a system of interacting spheres, or carbon as a substance that cycles through those spheres via identifiable chemical and biological processes.

## Progression Endpoint

Students represent the carbon cycle using diagrams or models and explain how combustion, photosynthesis and respiration each move carbon between Earth's spheres — including how human activity through combustion perturbs the natural cycle.

## Waypoint Sequence (5 waypoints, 2 hinges)

#### 1. Earth's spheres — geosphere, biosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere ⚑

Students can name and describe Earth's four spheres and give examples of matter or processes found in each.

**⚑ Hinge:** The four-sphere framework is the structural scaffold the carbon cycle diagram depends on. Students who cannot locate processes (photosynthesis, combustion, respiration) in specific spheres cannot represent carbon movement between spheres in N3–N5, and cannot explain what 'sphere interaction' means when examining each process.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students have prior knowledge of individual Earth systems (rock cycle, water cycle, ecosystems) but not the integrating four-sphere framework. Establishing the framework explicitly — with clear examples in each sphere — is the orientation node the rest of the standard builds on.

**Minimum width:** Students name all four spheres and give one example of matter or a process in each.

**Typical width:** Students describe the composition and location of each sphere; give multiple examples of matter and processes in each; describe the boundaries between spheres as zones of interaction; explain that Earth functions as a system where spheres influence each other.

**Wider:**
- Map a familiar phenomenon — a rain event, a bushfire — across all four spheres and identify which sphere interactions are involved
- Investigate First Nations Australians' firestick farming as a practice that deliberately manages interactions between the biosphere and atmosphere

**Success criteria:**
- Names all four of Earth's spheres and describes what each contains
- Gives at least one example of matter and one process located in each sphere
- Explains that Earth functions as a system where spheres interact

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U03
- AC9S8U04
- AC9S7U02

#### 2. Carbon as a substance cycling through Earth's spheres

Students can identify where carbon is stored in each of Earth's spheres and explain that carbon moves between these stores.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students need to understand carbon as a substance with identifiable locations before they can trace its movement in the cycle. The 'stores and flows' framing — familiar from food web energy flow in AC9S7U02 — is the conceptual bridge to carbon cycle representation.

**Minimum width:** Students identify carbon stores in at least three spheres (e.g. CO₂ in atmosphere, organic matter in biosphere, carbonate rocks in geosphere) and state that carbon moves between stores.

**Typical width:** Students describe carbon stores in all four spheres (atmospheric CO₂, dissolved carbon in oceans, organic carbon in living and dead matter, fossil fuels and carbonate rocks in geosphere); explain that carbon is neither created nor destroyed — it moves between stores; introduce carbon cycle as the map of those movements.

**Wider:**
- Investigate carbon sequestration in a local ecosystem — what stores exist and how large are they relative to atmospheric CO₂?
- Compare timescales of carbon movement: carbon in a leaf vs carbon in a coal seam — why does geological storage matter for climate?

**Success criteria:**
- Identifies carbon stores in at least three of Earth's four spheres with examples
- Explains that carbon moves between stores rather than being created or destroyed
- Distinguishes fast-cycling carbon stores (atmosphere, living organisms) from slow stores (fossil fuels, carbonate rock)

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U02
- AC9S6U04

#### 3. Photosynthesis and respiration — carbon movement through the biosphere ⚑

Students can explain how photosynthesis and respiration move carbon between the atmosphere and biosphere, and represent these as flows in the carbon cycle.

**⚑ Hinge:** Photosynthesis and respiration are the biological processes that dominate carbon cycling between biosphere and atmosphere. Students who cannot represent these as directional flows cannot construct a correct carbon cycle diagram in N5, and cannot explain why deforestation or ecosystem loss accelerates CO₂ accumulation in the atmosphere.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students often know photosynthesis and respiration as chemical equations without locating them as sphere-exchange processes. Reframing both processes as carbon-flow arrows between specific spheres — the representation the descriptor demands — requires an explicit conceptual shift from 'what happens in a cell' to 'how carbon moves between stores'.

**Minimum width:** Students state that photosynthesis removes CO₂ from the atmosphere and stores carbon in organic matter, and that respiration releases CO₂ back to the atmosphere.

**Typical width:** Students describe photosynthesis and respiration as complementary processes; explain that photosynthesis moves carbon from atmosphere to biosphere and respiration does the reverse; connect to food web energy flow from AC9S7U02; represent both processes as arrows in a carbon cycle diagram; explain that decomposition is also a form of respiration returning carbon to the atmosphere.

**Wider:**
- Investigate how carbon moves through a food web — trace a carbon atom from CO₂ through photosynthesis, herbivore, carnivore and decomposer back to atmosphere
- Investigate First Nations Australians' fire management regimes and explain how controlled burning affects the photosynthesis-respiration balance in an ecosystem

**Success criteria:**
- Explains that photosynthesis moves carbon from the atmosphere to the biosphere
- Explains that respiration and decomposition move carbon from the biosphere to the atmosphere
- Represents both processes as directional arrows between spheres in a diagram

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U02
- AC9S6U04

#### 4. Combustion — carbon release from geosphere to atmosphere ★

Students can explain how combustion of fossil fuels moves carbon from the geosphere to the atmosphere and describe the impact of human activity on this flow.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts: The elaborations name multiple combustion contexts — industrial, transport, firestick farming, carbon capture — each of which illuminates a different aspect of the combustion-cycle interaction. Varied contexts build the system-level understanding required for carbon cycle representation.

**Minimum width:** Students explain that combustion of fossil fuels releases carbon stored in the geosphere as CO₂ into the atmosphere.

**Typical width:** Students connect fossil fuel formation to ancient carbon sequestration (geological timescale); explain that combustion rapidly releases carbon that took millions of years to sequester; describe human industrial and transport combustion as the dominant driver; connect to the greenhouse effect elaboration — increased atmospheric CO₂ affects heat retention; calculate or estimate an individual's carbon footprint.

**Wider:**
- Investigate First Nations Australians' firestick farming as a controlled combustion practice — how does this differ from fossil fuel combustion in terms of carbon cycle impact?
- Model the carbon cycle perturbation: if combustion adds X tonnes of CO₂ to the atmosphere annually, and photosynthesis + ocean absorption removes Y, what happens to the atmospheric store over time?

**Success criteria:**
- Explains that combustion of fossil fuels moves carbon from geosphere to atmosphere
- Describes why this represents a net addition to the atmospheric carbon store (geological timescale of sequestration vs rapid release)
- Connects increased atmospheric CO₂ to the greenhouse effect and its role in heat retention

**Related standards:**
- AC9S6U04
- AC9S8U03

#### 5. Representing the carbon cycle — integrating processes across all spheres

Students can construct or annotate a carbon cycle diagram that represents all key processes (photosynthesis, respiration, combustion, decomposition) as flows between Earth's spheres.

**Response modes:**
- ⊕ Integration point: This node integrates the four-sphere framework (N1), carbon stores (N2), photosynthesis and respiration flows (N3), and combustion (N4) into a single representative model. Constructing the diagram is the descriptor's 'represent the carbon cycle' demand — the integration point for all prior nodes.

**Minimum width:** Students correctly label a carbon cycle diagram showing at least four carbon flows between named spheres.

**Typical width:** Students construct a complete carbon cycle diagram from memory or annotate a template with all major flows; explain the direction and mechanism of each flow; identify where human activity has altered natural flow rates; explain how carbon sequestration (natural or technological) works within the cycle; describe the cycle as a system in approximate balance before industrialisation.

**Wider:**
- Evaluate a carbon sequestration strategy (reforestation, biochar, ocean fertilisation) using the carbon cycle diagram — which flows does it target and what are the risks?
- Investigate how First Nations Australians' traditional fire management reduces net greenhouse gas emissions — map the mechanism onto the carbon cycle diagram

**Success criteria:**
- Constructs or correctly annotates a carbon cycle diagram with at least four labelled flows between named spheres
- Identifies which flows are biological, which are geological, and which are human-driven
- Explains how human combustion has altered the balance of the natural carbon cycle

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U02
- AC9S6U04
