# AC9S7U05 — Particle theory and properties

**Year 7**
**Strand:** Understanding

## ACARA Descriptor

use particle theory to describe the arrangement of particles in a substance, including the motion of and attraction between particles, and relate this to the properties of the substance

## Prior Knowledge

Students know that matter exists as solids, liquids and gases and can describe observable properties. They have not used particle theory to explain properties or modelled particle behaviour at a submicroscopic level.

## Progression Endpoint

Students use particle theory to describe the arrangement, motion and attraction of particles in substances and relate this to observable physical properties, including changes of state.

## Waypoint Sequence (5 waypoints, 3 hinges)

#### 1. Introducing the particle model ⚑

Students can match particle diagrams to states of matter and state the key ideas of particle theory

**⚑ Hinge:** Students who cannot reliably reproduce the four-component particle model (particles, spacing, motion, attraction) cannot use it as an explanatory framework in Node 2 onwards — they treat diagrams decoratively rather than as reasoning tools.

**Response modes:**
- ◆ Practise here: Matching particle diagrams to states and stating the four key ideas is a precision task that students commonly approximate — they confuse spacing and motion, or omit attraction. Practice with feedback on the four-component structure (particles, spacing, motion, attraction) until the model is reliably reproduced is what helps here.

**Minimum width:** State the key ideas of particle theory (particles, spacing, motion, attraction); correctly match particle diagrams to solid, liquid and gas

**Typical width:** Explain three observable properties of a substance using particle theory language; draw and annotate particle diagrams

**Wider:**
- Use and construct models, diagrams or virtual simulations to represent particle arrangement in different states
- Evaluate the particle model as a model — what does it explain well, what does it not explain, and what evidence supports it?
- Compare the particle model with how materials properties are described in Design and Technologies contexts

**Success criteria:**
- Matches particle diagrams to correct states of matter
- States the 4 key ideas of particle theory
- Identifies differences in arrangement, motion and attraction across states

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7I04
- AC9S7H01

#### 2. Particle arrangement, motion and attraction in each state ⚑

Students can describe particle arrangement, motion and attraction for each state and explain observable properties using this model

**⚑ Hinge:** Students who treat particle diagrams as decorative rather than explanatory cannot apply the model to property explanation in Node 3. The pattern-recognition built across multiple substances in this node is what enables the explanatory move — without it, Node 3 collapses to labelling.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts: Particle arrangement is the foundation of all property explanations. Students who treat particle diagrams as decorative rather than explanatory cannot use the model to reason. More substances and state comparisons force students to use diagrams as reasoning tools rather than illustrations — the misconception resolves through breadth of application, not repetition on the same substance.

**Minimum width:** Describe particle arrangement, motion and attraction for each state; explain why solids hold shape and gases fill containers

**Typical width:** Compare particle diagrams for 6 substances — rank by particle spacing and predict relative density; check against data

**Wider:**
- Relate motion and energy of particles to distances between particles in different states of the same substance
- Compare attractive forces in solid, liquid and gaseous states and relate to relative position and movement of particles
- Investigate an anomalous case (e.g. ice floating on water) — explain using particle theory and evaluate whether the basic model needs refinement

**Success criteria:**
- Describes particle arrangement, motion and attraction for each state
- Explains why solids hold shape and gases fill containers using particle theory
- Does not treat particle diagrams as decorative — uses them to support reasoning

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7I04
- AC9S7I05

#### 3. Explaining physical properties using particle theory ⚑ ★

Students can use particle theory to explain physical properties of substances including compressibility, viscosity and density

**⚑ Hinge:** Using particle theory explanatorily — not just descriptively — is directly prerequisite for AC9S7U06 (Pure Substances and Mixtures), the immediate downstream standard. Selecting and justifying separation techniques requires students to explain property differences at the particle level; students who can only label diagrams cannot access the property-technique reasoning that underpins every node in U06.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts: This is the key application node — using the model to explain rather than just describe. Each new property context (compressibility, viscosity, density, diffusion) forces a fresh application of the particle model, resolving the misconception that particle theory is a recall task rather than an explanatory framework. The prediction direction — properties from diagram — is particularly effective at exposing whether students can actually use the model.

**Minimum width:** Use particle theory to explain at least two physical properties (e.g. compressibility, viscosity, density) of a given substance

**Typical width:** Compare the physical properties of three substances using particle theory; explain why properties differ in terms of arrangement, motion and attraction

**Wider:**
- Investigate properties of materials such as density, melting point and compressibility and explain in terms of particle arrangement
- Explain the process of diffusion in a liquid and a gas in terms of particle motion
- Predict the properties of an unfamiliar substance given only its particle diagram; justify predictions and test against published data

**Success criteria:**
- Uses particle theory to explain at least 2 physical properties of a given substance
- Explanation references arrangement, motion or attraction — not just state labels
- Correctly links a named property to the relevant particle behaviour

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7I01
- AC9S7I05
- AC9S7I07

#### 4. Changes of state and energy

Students can explain changes of state using particle theory and correctly identify energy input or output for each change

**Response modes:**
- ⊕ Integration point: Changes of state integrate particle arrangement (Node 2), property explanation (Node 3), and energy input/output into a coordinated account of what happens during state change. Students who understand each element separately but cannot explain why temperature plateaus during melting — why energy input doesn't raise temperature but does change state — have not integrated the model components.

**Minimum width:** Explain melting, freezing, evaporation and condensation using particle theory; correctly identify energy input/output for each change

**Typical width:** Analyse a heating/cooling curve — identify each state change, explain plateau regions using particle theory, connect to energy changes

**Wider:**
- Examine how changing motion and energy of particles is affected by heat energy absorbed or released
- Model changes in particle arrangement as substances change state using diagrams or virtual simulations
- Investigate sublimation or a non-standard state change — explain using particle theory and evaluate whether the basic model is sufficient

**Success criteria:**
- Explains melting, freezing, evaporation and condensation using particle theory
- Correctly identifies energy input or output for each change
- Does not confuse temperature change with state change during plateau regions

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7I03
- AC9S7I04
- AC9S7I05

#### 5. Particle theory as a model: scope and limits

Students can identify the scope and limitations of the particle model and explain what evidence supports it

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students commonly describe model limitations abstractly without reasoning through why the model fails for a specific phenomenon. Scaffolding the reasoning — what does the model assume, and what does this phenomenon require that the model doesn't provide — builds genuine model evaluation rather than generic limitation-listing.

**Minimum width:** Identify one phenomenon particle theory explains well and one it does not explain; give a reason for each

**Typical width:** Compare particle theory with an alternative model — explain what each is designed to explain and why scientists use multiple models

**Wider:**
- Research a historical development of particle theory — how did evidence change the model over time?
- Analyse how characteristics and properties of materials are used in Design and Technologies to create solutions — connect to particle-level explanations
- Evaluate published claims about a material's properties using particle theory as a critical lens

**Success criteria:**
- Identifies one phenomenon particle theory explains well and one it does not
- Gives a reason for each based on the model's assumptions
