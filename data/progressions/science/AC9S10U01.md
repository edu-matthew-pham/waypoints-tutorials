# AC9S10U01 — Genetics and inheritance

**Year 10**
**Strand:** Biological Sciences

## ACARA Descriptor

explain the role of meiosis and mitosis and the function of chromosomes, DNA and genes in heredity and predict patterns of Mendelian inheritance

## Prior Knowledge

Students can describe reproductive structures and processes in animals and plants and analyse how sexual and asexual reproduction enable species survival (AC9S9U02). They can recognise cells as the basic units of living things and describe specialised cell structures (AC9S8U01). They have not yet examined the molecular basis of heredity — chromosomes, DNA and genes as functional units — or the mechanics of meiosis and mitosis as cell division processes, or Mendelian inheritance as a predictive model.

## Progression Endpoint

Students explain the roles of meiosis and mitosis in heredity, describe the structural relationship between chromosomes, DNA and genes, and predict genotype and phenotype ratios in monohybrid crosses including dominant/recessive and sex-linked patterns.

## Waypoint Sequence (6 waypoints, 3 hinges)

#### 1. Chromosomes, DNA and genes — the structural hierarchy ⚑

Students can describe the structural relationship between chromosomes, DNA and genes, and explain how genetic information is organised and stored.

**⚑ Hinge:** The structural hierarchy is the conceptual foundation all subsequent nodes require. Students who cannot locate genes on chromosomes, or who conflate DNA with chromosomes, cannot follow how meiosis separates chromosome pairs (N2), how alleles are transmitted (N4), or how sex-linked inheritance depends on chromosome identity (N6).

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students commonly conflate DNA, genes and chromosomes as interchangeable terms. Making the containment hierarchy explicit — chromosome contains DNA; gene is a segment of DNA — is the scaffold the transmission model in N4 requires. Diagrams that show the same information at multiple scales are effective here.

**Minimum width:** Students describe that chromosomes are made of DNA and that genes are sections of DNA that carry instructions for heritable characteristics.

**Typical width:** Students represent the chromosome → DNA → gene hierarchy using diagrams or models; explain that humans have 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs; describe that genes code for proteins and that proteins determine characteristics; distinguish genotype from phenotype.

**Wider:**
- Investigate how mutations — changes in DNA sequence — can alter gene function and connect to real genetic conditions (cystic fibrosis, sickle cell anaemia)
- Explore First Nations Australians' knowledges of heredity as evidenced by kinship and marriage laws — what observations about inheritance do these systems encode?

**Success criteria:**
- Describes chromosomes as structures made of DNA
- Explains that genes are segments of DNA that code for heritable characteristics
- Distinguishes genotype (genetic information) from phenotype (expressed characteristic)

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U01
- AC9S9U02

#### 2. Mitosis — cell division for growth and repair

Students can explain the outcome of mitosis and describe its role in growth, repair and asexual reproduction.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Mitosis is primarily a contrast node — its value is in establishing what meiosis is NOT. Students need a clear outcome model (two identical cells, chromosome number preserved) before the reduction and recombination logic of meiosis in N3 is meaningful.

**Minimum width:** Students state that mitosis produces two genetically identical daughter cells from one parent cell and explain its role in growth or repair.

**Typical width:** Students describe that mitosis copies all chromosomes before division to maintain chromosome number; explain why genetic identity is preserved; connect to asexual reproduction from AC9S9U02; distinguish mitosis from meiosis in terms of purpose and outcome.

**Wider:**
- Investigate how uncontrolled mitosis is the basis of cancer — what goes wrong, and how do treatments target dividing cells?
- Compare mitosis-based asexual reproduction (identical offspring) to sexual reproduction — what are the evolutionary trade-offs?

**Success criteria:**
- States that mitosis produces two genetically identical daughter cells
- Explains that chromosome number is maintained through mitosis
- Identifies growth and repair as the biological purposes of mitosis

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U01
- AC9S9U02

#### 3. Meiosis — halving chromosome number and generating variation ⚑

Students can explain how meiosis halves the chromosome number to produce gametes and describe how meiosis generates genetic variation.

**⚑ Hinge:** Meiosis is the mechanistic foundation of the inheritance model. Students who cannot explain why gametes carry one allele of each gene pair cannot construct Punnett squares correctly in N4, cannot explain why offspring ratios are probabilistic, and cannot account for sex-linked inheritance in N6 (where the sex chromosomes are the relevant pair being separated).

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: The logic of meiosis — homologous pairs separating so each gamete gets one from each pair — is the mechanism that makes Punnett squares work. Students who learn Punnett squares without this foundation produce correct ratios by rote without understanding why the probabilities hold. This must be established before N4.

**Minimum width:** Students explain that meiosis produces four genetically unique cells with half the chromosome number, and that this enables fertilisation to restore the full number.

**Typical width:** Students explain chromosome separation during meiosis; describe that homologous pairs are separated so each gamete receives one chromosome from each pair; explain how random assortment and crossing over during meiosis generate variation; connect gamete formation to the inheritance model in N4.

**Wider:**
- Investigate errors in meiosis (non-disjunction) and connect to chromosomal conditions such as Down syndrome or Klinefelter syndrome
- Compare the variation generated by meiosis + fertilisation to asexual reproduction — why does sexual reproduction produce more variation, and what are the costs?

**Success criteria:**
- Explains that meiosis halves the chromosome number to produce gametes
- Describes that each gamete receives one chromosome from each homologous pair
- Explains how meiosis contributes to genetic variation in offspring

**Related standards:**
- AC9S9U02

#### 4. Alleles and Mendelian inheritance — dominant and recessive ⚑ ★

Students can explain dominant and recessive allele relationships and use Punnett squares to predict genotype and phenotype ratios in monohybrid crosses.

**⚑ Hinge:** Mendelian prediction is the core quantitative demand of the descriptor. Students who cannot correctly assign alleles to gametes via Punnett squares — the most common failure point — produce incorrect ratios throughout and cannot extend to sex-linked patterns in N6 or pedigree interpretation. Punnett square errors typically stem from unresolved meiosis logic from N3.

**Response modes:**
- ◆ Practise here: Punnett square construction requires procedural precision — correct allele assignment, correct gamete formation from each parent, correct offspring reading. Errors are systematic and students need structured practice with self-correction before moving to sex-linked patterns.
- ↔ Widen contexts: Pedigree diagrams, real genetic conditions, and kinship structure examples each apply the Mendelian model to a different representational and cultural context. This is genuine width — the model is the same, the context and representation vary.

**Minimum width:** Students correctly construct a Punnett square for a monohybrid cross involving dominant and recessive alleles and state the expected phenotype ratio.

**Typical width:** Students apply Punnett squares to multiple cross types (PP × pp, Pp × Pp, Pp × pp); predict and explain genotype and phenotype ratios; use correct notation (capital/lowercase); connect allele pairs to the chromosome pairs separated by meiosis; interpret pedigree diagrams for dominant and recessive traits.

**Wider:**
- Investigate a real genetic condition (e.g. cystic fibrosis or haemochromatosis) using pedigree analysis — identify carriers and predict risk for offspring
- Explore First Nations Australians' kinship systems as evidence of observed heredity patterns — what inheritance regularities do marriage laws reflect?

**Success criteria:**
- Constructs a Punnett square for a monohybrid cross and states correct genotype and phenotype ratios
- Uses correct allele notation and correctly forms gametes from each parental genotype
- Interprets a pedigree diagram to identify phenotypes and infer genotypes for dominant/recessive traits

**Related standards:**
- AC9S9U02

#### 5. Fertilisation and heredity — connecting meiosis to offspring variation

Students can explain how meiosis and fertilisation together determine heredity and account for variation among siblings from the same parents.

**Response modes:**
- ⊕ Integration point: This node integrates N1 (DNA/chromosomes), N3 (meiosis) and N4 (Mendelian ratios) into a coherent account of heredity. Students who can construct a Punnett square but cannot explain why the probabilities hold need this connective node before sex-linked inheritance is introduced.

**Minimum width:** Students explain that fertilisation combines one gamete from each parent to restore the full chromosome number, and that random gamete combination produces variation among offspring.

**Typical width:** Students trace the full pathway from parent genotypes → meiosis → gametes → fertilisation → offspring genotypes; explain why siblings from the same parents differ genetically; connect Punnett square probabilities to the randomness of fertilisation; explain that genetic information from both parents is represented in all offspring.

**Wider:**
- Investigate how genetic information from both parents is passed to offspring and analyse how this increases species variation compared to asexual reproduction
- Explore environmental and other factors that cause mutations and explain how mutations introduce new variation not explained by Mendelian inheritance alone

**Success criteria:**
- Traces the pathway from parent genotypes through meiosis, gametes and fertilisation to offspring genotype
- Explains why siblings from the same parents can have different genotypes and phenotypes
- Connects Punnett square probability ratios to the random combination of gametes at fertilisation

**Related standards:**
- AC9S9U02

#### 6. Sex-linked inheritance ★

Students can explain sex determination by sex chromosomes and predict genotype and phenotype ratios for sex-linked traits using Punnett squares.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Sex-linked inheritance requires students to adapt the Punnett square notation they established in N4 — alleles are now carried on chromosomes of different sizes, and males have only one copy of X-linked genes. The logic extension from N4 must be made explicit, not assumed.
- ↔ Widen contexts: Multi-generational pedigrees for X-linked conditions (haemophilia, colour blindness) provide genuine width — students apply the sex-linked model to varied family structures and predict carrier and affected status. This is the elaboration's explicit expectation.

**Minimum width:** Students explain that sex is determined by X and Y chromosomes and correctly apply a Punnett square to predict phenotype ratios for one sex-linked trait.

**Typical width:** Students explain why sex-linked conditions (e.g. colour blindness, haemophilia) affect males and females differently; apply X-linked notation correctly; predict carrier status in females; interpret pedigrees for X-linked traits; explain why Y-linked traits pass only father to son.

**Wider:**
- Analyse a pedigree for an X-linked condition across three generations — identify carriers, predict risk for specific family members
- Compare rates of sex-linked condition expression in males vs females using probability analysis — why are males more frequently affected by X-linked recessive conditions?

**Success criteria:**
- Explains that sex is determined by the X and Y chromosome pair
- Uses correct X-linked allele notation in a Punnett square and predicts phenotype ratios for males and females
- Identifies why X-linked recessive conditions are more frequently expressed in males than females

**Related standards:**
- AC9S9U02
