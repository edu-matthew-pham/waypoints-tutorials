# AC9S8U04 — Rock cycle

**Year 8**
**Strand:** Earth and Space Sciences

## ACARA Descriptor

describe the key processes of the rock cycle, including the timescales over which they occur, and examine how the properties of sedimentary, igneous and metamorphic rocks reflect their formation and influence their use

## Prior Knowledge

Students can compare observable properties of rocks and minerals (AC9S3U02) and describe weathering, erosion, transportation and deposition as surface processes (AC9S5U02). Plate tectonics provides heat, pressure and uplift mechanisms (AC9S8U03). Prior learning covers surface rock identification and surface processes only — rock formation processes (igneous crystallisation, metamorphism, lithification) and the rock cycle as a system are entirely new.

## Progression Endpoint

Students describe the rock cycle as an interconnected system linking sedimentary, igneous and metamorphic formation, explain the geological timescales involved, and examine how each rock type's properties reflect its formation history and determine appropriate use.

## Waypoint Sequence (5 waypoints, 1 hinge)

#### 1. Sedimentary rocks — weathering, transport, deposition and lithification

Students can describe the formation of sedimentary rocks through the sequence of weathering, erosion, transportation, deposition and lithification, and connect observable properties to formation.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Sedimentary formation is the most connected to prior knowledge — weathering and erosion from AC9S5U02 are the first steps. Building the full formation chain from prior surface processes to completed rock is the appropriate entry point. Formation-property reasoning (rounded grains = transported; layers = sequential deposition) establishes the method used in N2 and N3.

**Minimum width:** Students describe the four-step formation sequence and identify one observable property of sedimentary rock that reflects each.

**Typical width:** Students describe: physical and chemical weathering breaks rock into sediment; erosion and transportation carry sediment; deposition layers sediment; lithification (compaction and cementation) produces rock; explain layering as a record of deposition events; explain rounded grains as evidence of transport; explain that fossils occur in sedimentary rocks (organisms buried in sediment); identify examples (sandstone, limestone, shale, coal).

**Wider:**
- Examine fossil evidence in sedimentary rock samples to predict the environment and approximate timescale of formation
- Investigate First Nations Australians' selection of sedimentary rocks for specific purposes — what observable properties reflect formation and guided selection?

**Success criteria:**
- Describes the formation sequence: weathering → erosion → transport → deposition → lithification
- Connects at least two observable properties of sedimentary rock to formation steps
- Identifies fossils as occurring in sedimentary rocks and explains why

**Related standards:**
- AC9S5U02
- AC9S3U02

#### 2. Igneous rocks — magma cooling and crystallisation ⚑

Students can explain the formation of igneous rocks from cooling magma or lava and connect crystal size to cooling rate.

**⚑ Hinge:** Crystal size as a direct record of cooling rate is the most diagnostic formation-property link in the rock cycle. Students who grasp this reasoning can apply it independently to novel igneous samples. It also models the formation-property reasoning method that N3 and N5 extend to metamorphic rocks and uses.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Crystal size = cooling rate is a mechanistic link students can derive rather than memorise — slower cooling allows more time for crystal growth. Making this causal chain explicit (not just 'granite has large crystals') gives students a formation-reading tool that transfers to novel samples.

**Minimum width:** Students distinguish intrusive from extrusive igneous formation and explain the crystal size difference using cooling rate.

**Typical width:** Students explain: magma cools slowly underground (intrusive) → large crystals (granite); lava cools rapidly at surface (extrusive) → small crystals or glassy texture (basalt, obsidian); explain that crystal size is a direct record of cooling rate — a key formation-property link; connect to plate tectonics — magma is produced at divergent boundaries and subduction zones; identify examples and describe properties.

**Wider:**
- Investigate the relationship between cooling rate and crystal size using a salt or alum crystallisation experiment — predict and test how different cooling rates affect crystal size
- Analyse igneous rock samples and infer their formation environment from crystal size alone

**Success criteria:**
- Distinguishes intrusive (underground, slow cooling) from extrusive (surface, rapid cooling) igneous formation
- Explains that slower cooling produces larger crystals
- Applies cooling rate reasoning to infer the formation environment of a named igneous rock

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U03

#### 3. Metamorphic rocks — heat and pressure without melting

Students can explain metamorphic rock formation through heat and pressure applied to existing rock, and describe observable properties that reflect these conditions.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Metamorphic formation is the least intuitive of the three types — heat and pressure without melting is a process with no everyday equivalent. Students need the formation conditions explained explicitly (subduction, burial, mountain building) with the observable consequences (banding, density, hardness) derived from those conditions.

**Minimum width:** Students state that metamorphic rocks form when existing rocks are subjected to heat and pressure without melting, and identify one observable property reflecting this.

**Typical width:** Students explain that existing rocks (any type) subjected to intense heat and pressure underground undergo recrystallisation without melting; explain banding/foliation as a result of minerals being compressed and reoriented; explain that metamorphic rocks are denser and harder than their precursors; identify parent rock and metamorphic product pairs (shale → slate → phyllite → schist; limestone → marble); connect to plate tectonics — subduction zones and mountain-building provide the heat and pressure.

**Wider:**
- Examine the metamorphic series from shale → slate → phyllite → schist → gneiss and explain what increasing grade of metamorphism reveals about the conditions of formation
- Compare a metamorphic rock to its parent rock and identify the structural changes produced by heat and pressure

**Success criteria:**
- Explains that metamorphic rocks form when existing rocks are subjected to heat and pressure without melting
- Connects banding or foliation to the directional compression of metamorphism
- Identifies at least one parent rock and its metamorphic product

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U03

#### 4. The rock cycle — processes connecting all three types over geological time ★

Students can describe the rock cycle as a set of interconnected processes by which rocks are continuously transformed between types, and identify the geological timescales involved.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: The rock cycle requires students to reconceptualise rock as temporary — any rock can become any other rock type given sufficient time and appropriate conditions. This is a significant conceptual shift from stable, fixed rock types.
- ↔ Widen contexts: Mineral journey tracing, timescale comparison, and mining impact analysis each illuminate different aspects of the cycle as a dynamic Earth system.

**Minimum width:** Students trace at least two complete pathways in the rock cycle, naming the process at each transformation step.

**Typical width:** Students describe all major pathways: weathering/erosion → sediment → sedimentary; burial/heat/pressure → metamorphic; melting → magma → igneous; uplift (any deep rock) → surface → weathering again; compare timescales: surface weathering (thousands to millions of years), burial and metamorphism (tens of millions), subduction and melting (similar); explain that plate tectonics drives the cycle by providing uplift, subduction, and heat; emphasise that no rock is permanent.

**Wider:**
- Trace the journey of a specific mineral (e.g. quartz) through multiple rock cycle stages and estimate the total time elapsed
- Investigate how mining of ores and minerals intersects with the rock cycle — what processes concentrate economically useful minerals, and what does extraction disrupt?

**Success criteria:**
- Traces at least two complete rock cycle pathways, naming the process at each step
- Compares the timescales of surface and deep rock cycle processes
- Explains how plate tectonics drives the rock cycle

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U03
- AC9S5U02

#### 5. Rock properties, formation history and use

Students can explain how the properties of each rock type reflect its formation history and evaluate the suitability of specific rocks for named uses.

**Response modes:**
- ⊕ Integration point: This node integrates the formation-property links established in N1–N3 and the cycle understanding from N4 into practical application — properties as a consequence of formation history determining use. It is the descriptor's 'examine how properties reflect formation and influence use' demand.

**Minimum width:** Students explain the use of two rocks by connecting their properties to formation and to the demands of the application.

**Typical width:** Students connect: granite (interlocked large crystals, hard, weather-resistant → building, benchtops); marble (recrystallised calcite, polishable → sculpture, tiles); sandstone (layered, porous, relatively soft → building, grinding); slate (fine-grained metamorphic, splits in sheets → roofing); coal (compressed organic material → fuel); connect First Nations rock selection to observable formation-linked properties; use dichotomous keys to identify rock samples.

**Wider:**
- Investigate First Nations Australians' quarrying practices — how were grindstones, hammerstones and cutting tools selected based on observable properties, and what does this reveal about practical knowledge of formation-linked properties?
- Evaluate the environmental impact of quarrying a specific rock type and examine rehabilitation initiatives

**Success criteria:**
- Explains the properties of at least two rock types in terms of their formation processes
- Connects named rock properties to suitability for a specific use
- Identifies how traditional rock selection practices reflect knowledge of formation-linked properties

**Related standards:**
- AC9S3U02
- AC9S5U02
