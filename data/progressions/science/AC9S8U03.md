# AC9S8U03 — Plate tectonics

**Year 8**
**Strand:** Earth and Space Sciences

## ACARA Descriptor

investigate tectonic activity including the formation of geological features at divergent, convergent and transform plate boundaries and describe the scientific evidence for the theory of plate tectonics

## Prior Knowledge

Students can describe observable properties of rocks and minerals (AC9S3U02) and explain how weathering, erosion, transportation and deposition change Earth's surface (AC9S5U02). Prior learning is entirely surface-level — there is no curriculum treatment of Earth's interior at any prior year level. Earth-Sun-Moon cycles (AC9S7U03) address celestial geometry, not geological interior. Students have no prior exposure to Earth's internal structure, plates, or tectonic processes.

## Progression Endpoint

Students describe the formation of geological features at divergent, convergent and transform plate boundaries and explain the scientific evidence — including seafloor spreading, continental fit, fossil distribution and earthquake/volcanic patterns — that supports the theory of plate tectonics.

## Waypoint Sequence (6 waypoints, 2 hinges)

#### 1. Earth's internal structure — layers, states and the lithosphere ⚑

Students can describe Earth's layered internal structure, identify the state and composition of each layer, and explain that the lithosphere is the rigid outer layer.

**⚑ Hinge:** The lithosphere concept is the prerequisite for the plate model in N2. Students who do not understand that there is a rigid outer layer sitting on a semi-fluid asthenosphere cannot grasp why plates can move independently, or why they behave as they do at boundaries in N3–N5.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students have no prior mental model of Earth's interior. The layered structure with varying states — particularly the mantle as solid yet able to flow over geological time — is counterintuitive. This node must build the interior model explicitly before any tectonic mechanism is introduced.

**Minimum width:** Students name Earth's main layers, state whether each is solid or liquid, and identify the lithosphere as the rigid outer layer.

**Typical width:** Students describe crust (thin, solid, oceanic vs continental), mantle (mostly solid but flows slowly over geological time, convection occurs in upper mantle), outer core (liquid iron/nickel), inner core (solid); explain that temperature and pressure increase with depth; describe the lithosphere as crust plus rigid upper mantle; explain that Australia's ancient craton reflects a long history of tectonic stability.

**Wider:**
- Investigate how we know about Earth's interior without direct observation — what does seismic wave behaviour reveal about layer boundaries and states?
- Compare the relative thickness of the crust to Earth's radius using a scale model — why is the lithosphere described as like the skin of an apple?

**Success criteria:**
- Names Earth's main internal layers and describes the state (solid/liquid) of each
- Explains that the lithosphere is the rigid outer layer (crust + upper mantle)
- States that temperature and pressure increase with depth

**Related standards:**
- AC9S5U02

#### 2. Tectonic plates and the driving mechanism ⚑

Students can explain that the lithosphere is divided into tectonic plates and describe mantle convection as the mechanism driving plate movement.

**⚑ Hinge:** The plate-as-moving-unit concept is the structural prerequisite for all three boundary types. Students who do not understand that plates are distinct rigid units that move relative to each other cannot explain why different boundary types produce different features — the feature depends entirely on the direction of relative plate motion.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: The plate model requires students to reconceptualise Earth's surface from a fixed landscape to a dynamic system of moving pieces. The convection mechanism must be established before boundary types — without a driving mechanism, plate movement appears arbitrary and boundary feature formation cannot be explained causally.

**Minimum width:** Students state that the lithosphere is divided into moving plates and identify convection in the mantle as the driving mechanism.

**Typical width:** Students describe plates as large rigid sections of lithosphere; explain that heat from Earth's core drives convection currents in the mantle; describe how convection creates slab pull and ridge push forces; explain that plates move at rates of centimetres per year (fingernail growth rate); describe the major plates; connect the extreme age and stability of the Australian craton to its position away from active plate boundaries.

**Wider:**
- Investigate the relative significance of slab pull, ridge push and mantle convection — which force dominates and what evidence supports this?
- Research First Nations Australians' cultural accounts of earthquakes and volcanic events — what geological record do these accounts preserve?

**Success criteria:**
- Explains that the lithosphere is divided into tectonic plates that move
- Describes mantle convection as the mechanism driving plate movement
- States that plates move at rates of centimetres per year

**Related standards:**
- AC9S5U02

#### 3. Divergent boundaries — plates moving apart

Students can describe the geological features formed where plates diverge and explain the process producing each.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Each boundary type is most clearly understood through the plate movement direction → stress type → feature chain. Divergent = tension → crust thins → magma rises → new crust. Making this causal chain explicit for each type builds a transferable reasoning framework rather than a list of boundary features.

**Minimum width:** Students describe seafloor spreading and mid-ocean ridges as the result of diverging plates.

**Typical width:** Students explain that diverging plates create tension in the crust; magma rises to fill the gap — forming new oceanic crust (seafloor spreading) and mid-ocean ridges; on continents, divergence produces rift valleys; explain that rock at mid-ocean ridges is youngest and age increases with distance from the ridge; describe volcanic activity along ridges.

**Wider:**
- Investigate Marie Tharp's topographic mapping of the Atlantic Ocean floor — how did her maps reveal the mid-ocean ridge system and what resistance did she face in getting her findings accepted?
- Model divergent boundary interactions using physical materials — evaluate the model's accuracy and identify what it cannot show

**Success criteria:**
- Explains that diverging plates produce tension and allow magma to rise
- Describes mid-ocean ridges and seafloor spreading as products of divergent boundaries
- States that oceanic crust is youngest at ridges and ages with distance

**Related standards:**
- AC9S5U02

#### 4. Convergent boundaries — plates colliding

Students can describe the geological features formed at convergent boundaries and distinguish ocean-continent from continent-continent convergence.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Convergent boundaries are more complex than divergent — the outcome depends on the density of the colliding plates (oceanic vs continental). Students need the density distinction made explicit to explain why subduction occurs in ocean-continent but not continent-continent collisions.

**Minimum width:** Students describe subduction and explain one feature (trench or volcanic arc) that results from ocean-continent convergence.

**Typical width:** Students explain ocean-continent convergence: denser oceanic plate subducts under continental → trench, volcanic arc, earthquakes; continent-continent convergence: neither subducts → folding and uplift → mountain ranges (Himalayas); explain why continental crust doesn't subduct (less dense); describe the Ring of Fire as a product of convergent boundaries around the Pacific; evaluate engineering solutions to tectonic hazards.

**Wider:**
- Investigate engineering solutions designed to reduce tectonic event impacts — what assumptions about plate forces do these solutions embed?
- Analyse earthquake and volcanic activity distribution maps and identify which patterns correspond to convergent vs divergent boundaries

**Success criteria:**
- Describes subduction as the process at ocean-continent convergent boundaries
- Explains the formation of ocean trenches and volcanic arcs at ocean-continent boundaries
- Explains why continent-continent convergence produces mountain ranges rather than subduction

**Related standards:**
- AC9S5U02

#### 5. Transform boundaries — plates sliding past each other

Students can describe transform boundaries, explain the geological features they produce, and explain why earthquakes are concentrated there.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Transform boundaries are structurally simpler than convergent but the friction-stress-release mechanism for earthquakes needs explicit explanation. Students commonly assume volcanoes occur at all plate boundaries — the contrast with convergent and divergent in terms of volcanic activity is important.

**Minimum width:** Students describe transform boundaries as plates sliding horizontally past each other and state that they produce earthquakes.

**Typical width:** Students explain that plates grind horizontally past each other at transform boundaries; friction prevents smooth movement — stress builds and releases suddenly as earthquakes; describe strike-slip faults (e.g. San Andreas); explain that transform boundaries produce earthquakes but not volcanic activity; contrast with convergent and divergent in terms of feature type.

**Wider:**
- Compare the seismic hazard at transform, convergent and divergent boundaries — which type produces the most damaging earthquakes and why?
- Investigate a specific transform fault (San Andreas, Alpine Fault NZ) and explain the geological history of the boundary

**Success criteria:**
- Describes transform boundaries as plates sliding horizontally past each other
- Explains why earthquakes occur at transform boundaries (stress build-up and release)
- States that transform boundaries produce earthquakes but not volcanic activity

**Related standards:**
- AC9S5U02

#### 6. Evidence for plate tectonics — multiple converging lines ★

Students can describe at least three independent lines of evidence supporting the theory of plate tectonics and explain what each demonstrates.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts: Each evidence type — continental fit, fossils, geology, seafloor age, seismic patterns — is a distinct investigative context contributing independent support for the same conclusion. Working across all types builds the 'multiple converging lines' understanding the descriptor's 'describe the scientific evidence' demand requires.

**Minimum width:** Students describe two lines of evidence and explain what each demonstrates about plate movement or history.

**Typical width:** Students explain: continental fit (jigsaw shapes, especially South America/Africa); matching fossil distributions across now-separated continents; matching geological formations across continental margins; seafloor age pattern — youngest at ridges, oldest at subduction zones; earthquake and volcanic activity concentrated at boundaries; construct a timeline of theory development including Marie Tharp's contribution; examine how geologist resistance to Wegener illustrates theory validation processes.

**Wider:**
- Construct a timeline showing how the evidence base accumulated and why the theory was initially resisted — what changed when seafloor data became available?
- Research First Nations Australians' cultural accounts that record historical earthquake or volcanic events — what geological evidence do these accounts preserve?

**Success criteria:**
- Describes at least three distinct lines of evidence supporting plate tectonics
- Explains what each evidence type demonstrates about plate movement or history
- Identifies seafloor age patterns as particularly strong evidence for seafloor spreading
