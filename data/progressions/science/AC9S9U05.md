# AC9S9U05 — Conservation of energy and efficiency

**Year 9**
**Strand:** Physical Sciences

## ACARA Descriptor

apply the law of conservation of energy to analyse system efficiency in terms of energy inputs, outputs, transfers and transformations

## Prior Knowledge

Students can classify energy as kinetic or potential and explain energy transfers and transformations in simple systems (AC9S8U05). They can explain energy transfer and transformation in electrical circuits (AC9S6U03). They have qualitative understanding of energy transformation but have not yet applied conservation of energy as a quantitative law, or analysed system efficiency — the proportion of useful output energy — as a calculated quantity.

## Progression Endpoint

Students apply the law of conservation of energy to account for all energy inputs and outputs in a system, calculate efficiency as useful output divided by total input, and use Sankey diagrams to represent energy flow — applying this analysis to real systems including electricity generation and appliances.

## Waypoint Sequence (4 waypoints, 2 hinges)

#### 1. Conservation of energy — total energy is maintained in any system ⚑

Students can state the law of conservation of energy and apply it to account for all energy in a system — including energy that is transferred as heat or sound rather than as useful output.

**⚑ Hinge:** Conservation of energy is the law the entire standard applies. Students who believe energy is 'used up' or 'disappears' in a system cannot correctly identify all outputs when calculating efficiency in N2, and will misinterpret Sankey diagrams as showing energy loss rather than energy redistribution. This misconception is common and must be resolved before efficiency analysis is introduced.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: The misconception that energy is 'used up' or 'lost' in a transformation is persistent and directly blocks efficiency analysis. Students must explicitly encounter and resolve this before N2 — efficiency only makes sense as a ratio if total energy is conserved.

**Minimum width:** Students state that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred or transformed, and apply this to explain why the total energy in a closed system remains constant.

**Typical width:** Students apply conservation of energy to trace all energy outputs in a system (useful + wasted); explain that 'wasted' energy is not lost — it is transferred to the environment, typically as heat or sound; connect to prior kinetic/potential energy knowledge from AC9S8U05; explain that conservation of energy applies even when energy appears to disappear.

**Wider:**
- Investigate the energy transformations in a bouncing ball — where does the kinetic energy go at each bounce, and can you account for all of it?
- Apply conservation of energy to a pendulum system — trace energy between kinetic and potential forms over multiple swings and explain why the pendulum eventually stops

**Success criteria:**
- States the law of conservation of energy correctly
- Explains that 'wasted' energy is transferred to the environment, not destroyed
- Accounts for all energy outputs (useful + wasted) in a simple system

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U05
- AC9S6U03

#### 2. Efficiency — useful output as a proportion of total input ⚑ ★

Students can define efficiency, calculate it as a ratio of useful output to total input energy, and explain why no real system achieves 100% efficiency.

**⚑ Hinge:** Efficiency as a calculated ratio is the quantitative core of the descriptor's 'analyse system efficiency' demand. Students who cannot apply the formula correctly — particularly students who confuse efficiency with output quantity rather than proportion — cannot use Sankey diagrams quantitatively in N3 or compare real systems in N4.

**Response modes:**
- ◆ Practise here: Efficiency calculation requires procedural precision — identifying useful output correctly, applying the ratio, interpreting the result as a proportion. Students need structured practice before the Sankey diagram representation in N3.
- ↔ Widen contexts: Multiple efficiency contexts — appliances, generation sources, traditional technologies — build the comparative analytical capability the descriptor's 'analyse' demand requires. Each context has a different energy input/output profile.

**Minimum width:** Students calculate efficiency using efficiency = useful output ÷ total input and explain why real systems are less than 100% efficient.

**Typical width:** Students apply the efficiency formula across multiple contexts (light bulb, engine, solar panel); explain that inefficiency results from energy being transferred in non-useful forms (heat, sound, light); explain why 100% efficiency is unachievable in practice; connect to energy star ratings on appliances; compare efficiency values to evaluate system performance.

**Wider:**
- Investigate the efficiency of ground ovens used by First Nations Australians — estimate energy input and useful thermal output and compare to a modern equivalent
- Compare the efficiency of electricity generation from coal, nuclear, hydroelectricity, gas, solar and wind using published data — what does the variation reveal about energy conversion processes?

**Success criteria:**
- Calculates efficiency as useful output ÷ total input with correct units
- Explains why no real system achieves 100% efficiency
- Compares efficiency values across two systems and interprets the difference

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U05
- AC9S6U03

#### 3. Sankey diagrams — representing energy inputs, outputs and losses

Students can construct and interpret Sankey diagrams to represent energy flow through a system, reading off efficiency and identifying where energy losses occur.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Sankey diagrams encode both qualitative (direction, type) and quantitative (width = energy quantity) information simultaneously. Students need explicit instruction on reading the representation — particularly that arrow width is proportional to energy, not just indicating flow direction — before they can use diagrams to analyse real systems.

**Minimum width:** Students correctly read efficiency and identify the largest energy loss from a given Sankey diagram.

**Typical width:** Students construct Sankey diagrams for simple systems from energy data; interpret the width of arrows as proportional to energy quantity; identify which transformation stage is least efficient; evaluate the diagram as a representation — what it shows clearly and what it obscures; critique diagrams for accuracy.

**Wider:**
- Construct a Sankey diagram for a multi-stage energy system (e.g. coal → electricity → light) and calculate the overall efficiency as a product of stage efficiencies
- Evaluate the claim that Sankey diagrams are more informative than efficiency percentages alone — when is the diagram more useful, and when is a number sufficient?

**Success criteria:**
- Reads efficiency and identifies major energy losses from a given Sankey diagram
- Constructs a Sankey diagram from energy data with correctly proportioned arrows
- Critiques a Sankey diagram — identifies what it represents well and any limitations

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U05

#### 4. Applying conservation of energy and efficiency to real systems

Students can apply the law of conservation of energy and efficiency analysis to evaluate real energy systems and identify where improvements could be made.

**Response modes:**
- ⊕ Integration point: This node integrates conservation of energy (N1), efficiency calculation (N2) and Sankey representation (N3) into analysis of real systems — the descriptor's full 'apply the law of conservation of energy to analyse system efficiency' demand. Students bring all three tools to bear on novel systems.

**Minimum width:** Students apply conservation of energy and calculate efficiency for one real system and suggest one improvement based on the analysis.

**Typical width:** Students analyse electricity generation systems (coal, solar, hydro) using efficiency data; evaluate appliance energy star ratings; apply efficiency analysis to sporting contexts (pole vault, archery) to explain how technique improvements reduce energy loss; connect efficiency improvement to reduced resource use and environmental impact.

**Wider:**
- Compare the efficiency of traditional First Nations ground ovens to modern cooking technologies — account for all energy inputs and outputs in both systems
- Evaluate a proposed efficiency improvement in a named energy system — use conservation of energy to predict the impact on total energy use and waste heat

**Success criteria:**
- Applies conservation of energy to account for all energy inputs and outputs in a real system
- Calculates efficiency for a real system and interprets the result
- Identifies where energy losses occur and suggests a practical improvement

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U05
- AC9S6U03
