# AC9S8U07 — Physical and chemical changes

**Year 8**
**Strand:** Chemical Sciences

## ACARA Descriptor

compare physical and chemical changes and identify indicators of energy change in chemical reactions

## Prior Knowledge

Students can recognise that physical changes preserve material composition (AC9S2U03), compare reversible and irreversible changes at an observable level (AC9S6U04), use particle theory for substance properties (AC9S7U05), and classify matter as elements, compounds or mixtures (AC9S8U06). AC9S6U04 provides a reversibility heuristic that mostly works but fails for dissolving. The new substance criterion — grounded in compound formation from AC9S8U06 — is new. Exothermic and endothermic as named categories of energy change in reactions are new.

## Progression Endpoint

Students compare physical and chemical changes using the new substance criterion, identify observable indicators of chemical change, and explain exothermic and endothermic reactions as categories of energy change — applying all three criteria to classify and analyse unfamiliar changes.

## Waypoint Sequence (6 waypoints, 1 hinge)

#### 1. Physical change at the particle level — rearrangement without new substance

Students can explain physical change as rearrangement of particles without formation of new substances, and represent this using the particle model.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: The particle-level representation of physical change establishes the baseline against which chemical change will be contrasted in N2. Dissolving as a physical change is important to address here — it is the case most likely to be misclassified as chemical change, and the particle diagram makes the 'no new substance' criterion visible.

**Minimum width:** Students explain physical change using the particle model — particles rearrange but atom types and bonding remain the same.

**Typical width:** Students describe physical changes (melting, dissolving, tearing, bending) using particle diagrams — particles change position or arrangement but no new compound is formed; connect to AC9S2U03 (physical change preserves material composition) and AC9S3U04 (state changes); explain that physical changes are typically reversible because no new substance has formed; note that reversibility is a consequence, not the definition.

**Wider:**
- Represent dissolving at the particle level — show NaCl ions separating and dispersing in water — and explain why this is a physical change despite appearing to 'disappear'
- Investigate which physical changes are reversible and which are difficult to reverse in practice (e.g. tearing paper) — explain using particle rearrangement

**Success criteria:**
- Explains physical change as particle rearrangement without formation of new substances
- Represents a physical change using a particle diagram showing no new substance
- Explains why reversibility is a consequence of physical change, not the definition

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U05
- AC9S8U06
- AC9S2U03

#### 2. Chemical change — new substance formation at the particle level ⚑

Students can explain chemical change as the formation of new substances through bond breaking and forming, and apply the new substance criterion to classify changes.

**⚑ Hinge:** The new substance criterion replaces the unreliable reversibility heuristic from AC9S6U04. Students who retain reversibility as the primary test will misclassify dissolving (reversible but not chemical) and some reactions. The new substance criterion must be explicitly established as the correct test before indicators and energy change are introduced.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Explicitly confronting the reversibility heuristic failure — dissolving is reversible but not chemical; some chemical reactions can be reversed — is necessary. Students need the new substance criterion presented as a replacement, not an addition, to their prior classification tool. Particle-level diagrams showing bond-breaking and bond-forming make the criterion visible.

**Minimum width:** Students state the new substance criterion and correctly classify three unfamiliar changes as physical or chemical.

**Typical width:** Students explain that chemical change involves bonds breaking between atoms and new bonds forming — producing compounds with different atom arrangements and different properties; connect to AC9S8U06's compound concept — a new compound is a new substance; explain that chemical changes are typically not easily reversed because new substances have different properties from reactants; apply the new substance criterion to classify: burning (new compounds formed), rusting (iron oxide ≠ iron), cooking (proteins/starches structurally altered).

**Wider:**
- Investigate an ambiguous case — is dissolving chemical or physical? Predict using the new substance criterion, test by evaporation, and evaluate the result
- Analyse before-and-after property data for three unfamiliar changes and classify each using the new substance criterion — explain your reasoning

**Success criteria:**
- States that new substance formation is the criterion defining chemical change
- Correctly classifies at least three changes using the new substance criterion
- Explains why reversibility alone is not a reliable criterion using a named counterexample

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U06
- AC9S6U04
- AC9S7U05

#### 3. Observable indicators of chemical change

Students can identify and explain four observable indicators of chemical change and explain why each suggests a new substance has formed.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Indicators are evidence of chemical change, not its definition. Students commonly invert this — classifying a change as chemical because they see a colour change. Making the evidence-criterion relationship explicit (indicator suggests new substance may have formed → confirm by checking properties) prevents systematic misclassification.

**Minimum width:** Students name four indicators and describe one observation demonstrating each.

**Typical width:** Students describe: gas production (bubbles, odour — a gaseous compound has formed); precipitate formation (solid appears in solution — an insoluble compound has formed); colour change (new compound has different light absorption); temperature change (energy released or absorbed as bonds break and form); perform or analyse simple reactions to identify indicators; explain that indicators are evidence of the criterion, not the definition — the indicator suggests a new substance may have formed.

**Wider:**
- Design a reaction investigation that uses multiple indicators to confirm chemical change — which combinations give the strongest evidence?
- Investigate how chemical change indicators are used in practical testing contexts — soil pH kits, water quality tests, pregnancy tests — and identify which indicator each exploits

**Success criteria:**
- Names four observable indicators of chemical change
- Explains why each indicator suggests a new substance has formed
- Distinguishes indicators (observable evidence) from the definition (new substance)

**Related standards:**
- AC9S6U04

#### 4. Exothermic reactions — energy released to surroundings

Students can identify exothermic reactions by temperature rise, explain that energy is released as bonds form in the products, and give examples.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Exothermic and endothermic are most clearly understood in parallel contrast. Treating exothermic first — where the temperature rise is observable and intuitive — provides the reference point for the less intuitive endothermic in N5.

**Minimum width:** Students identify exothermic reactions by observable temperature rise and give two examples.

**Typical width:** Students describe exothermic reactions (combustion, neutralisation, respiration, hand warmers); explain that when products form, the energy stored in new bonds is less than was stored in reactant bonds — the difference is released as heat; explain why temperature rises in the surroundings; connect to AC9S8U05's energy classification — chemical potential energy → thermal energy; describe applications: hand warmers, self-heating cans, fire-starting.

**Wider:**
- Investigate the temperature change in a neutralisation reaction — measure the heat released and compare different acid/base combinations
- Examine how exothermic reactions are exploited in hand warmers and self-heating food cans — what chemical system is used and how is the release triggered?

**Success criteria:**
- Identifies exothermic reactions by a temperature rise in the surroundings
- Explains that exothermic reactions release energy stored in chemical bonds
- Gives at least two examples of exothermic reactions

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U05
- AC9S3U03

#### 5. Endothermic reactions — energy absorbed from surroundings ★

Students can identify endothermic reactions by temperature drop, explain that energy is absorbed to break reactant bonds, and give examples.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts: Multiple endothermic reaction contexts — dissolving, photosynthesis, thermal decomposition, cold packs — each apply the same energy-absorption concept in a different domain. Breadth here is more productive than depth on a single example.

**Minimum width:** Students identify endothermic reactions by observable temperature fall and give two examples.

**Typical width:** Students describe endothermic reactions (photosynthesis, thermal decomposition, dissolving ammonium nitrate); explain that the energy required to break reactant bonds exceeds the energy released in product bond formation — the difference is absorbed from surroundings as heat; explain why temperature falls in the surroundings; describe applications: cold packs; contrast with exothermic in terms of energy direction.

**Wider:**
- Investigate the temperature change when ammonium nitrate dissolves in water — explain the observation using bond energy reasoning
- Compare an exothermic and endothermic reaction using the same experimental setup — identify all variables that need controlling to make the comparison fair

**Success criteria:**
- Identifies endothermic reactions by a temperature fall in the surroundings
- Explains that endothermic reactions absorb energy from surroundings
- Gives at least two examples of endothermic reactions and distinguishes them from exothermic

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U05
- AC9S3U03

#### 6. Comparing physical and chemical changes — integrating all criteria

Students can apply the new substance criterion, observable indicators and energy change together to classify and analyse unfamiliar changes.

**Response modes:**
- ⊕ Integration point: This node integrates particle-level physical change (N1), new substance criterion (N2), indicators (N3) and energy change (N4–N5) into a complete analytical framework. Applying all criteria to novel cases — particularly cases where a single indicator could mislead — is the descriptor's full 'compare physical and chemical changes' demand.

**Minimum width:** Students apply all three criteria to classify two unfamiliar changes and justify each classification.

**Typical width:** Students analyse before-and-after data (appearance, temperature, mass, properties) to classify changes; explain that multiple converging indicators increase confidence; examine cases where a single indicator is misleading — temperature change also occurs in physical processes (state change) without chemical change; connect to AC9S8U06 — chemical change produces a new compound; evaluate the claim that 'if it glows, it must be chemical' against counterexamples.

**Wider:**
- Analyse a multi-step process (cooking a meal, or a First Nations food preparation technique) — identify all physical and chemical changes involved and classify each using the full criterion set
- Investigate how chemical change indicators are used in environmental or medical testing — what does each indicator detect, and how is false-positive risk minimised?

**Success criteria:**
- Applies the new substance criterion, observable indicators and energy change to classify an unfamiliar change
- Explains why multiple converging criteria increase classification confidence
- Identifies a case where a single indicator is misleading and explains using the new substance criterion

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U06
- AC9S6U04
- AC9S8U05
