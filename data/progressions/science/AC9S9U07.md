# AC9S9U07 — Chemical Equations and Conservation of Mass

**Year 9**
**Strand:** Chemical Sciences
**Planning mode:** A — Concept-certifying

## ACARA Descriptor

model the rearrangement of atoms in chemical reactions using a range of representations, including word and simple balanced chemical equations, and use these to demonstrate the law of conservation of mass

## ACARA Elaborations

- identifying reactants and products in chemical reactions
- using models and representations to show the rearrangement of atoms in chemical reactions
- investigating chemical reactions in closed and open systems and relating data obtained to the law of conservation of mass
- writing symbolic equations that are easy to balance and explaining, using the law of conservation of mass, and atoms, the rationale for balancing chemical equations
- investigating why most elements are not found in their elemental state
- predicting how ideas of green chemistry will affect the environment

## Prior Knowledge

Students can compare physical and chemical changes and identify indicators of energy change in chemical reactions (AC9S8U07). They can classify matter as elements, compounds and mixtures and use chemical symbols and formulas (AC9S8U06). They can compare reversible and irreversible changes (AC9S6U04). They have not yet modelled atom rearrangement as the mechanism of chemical change, written balanced symbolic equations, or applied conservation of mass as a quantitative principle to chemical reactions.

## Progression Endpoint

Students model chemical reactions as atom rearrangement using word equations, molecular models and balanced symbolic equations, and use conservation of mass — demonstrated through atom counting — to explain why equations must be balanced.

## Width Tiers

**Width varies by:** phenomena and contexts

**Minimum width:** Identify reactants and products in at least two reactions; write at least two balanced symbolic equations; explain why equations must be balanced using conservation of mass; explain mass change in open vs closed systems.

**Typical width:** Identify reactants and products; explain atomic rearrangement using models; write and balance simple symbolic equations; investigate reactions in closed and open systems; relate mass data to conservation of mass; compare information conveyed by molecular models vs balanced equations.

**Wider:**
- Investigate chemical reactions First Nations Australians use to convert toxic plants into edible food products — examining traditional food processing chemistry
- Examine green chemistry principles — minimising waste products, energy use and environmental impact — and predict how they will change industrial chemistry practice
- Investigate why most elements are not found in their elemental state and examine processes used to obtain them — connecting equilibrium concepts to real extraction chemistry

## Waypoint Sequence (5 waypoints, 2 hinges)

#### 1. Reactants and products — identifying what changes in a chemical reaction

Students can identify reactants and products in a chemical reaction and describe the change in terms of new substances being formed.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning — unpack the logic explicitly: Students have prior knowledge of chemical change indicators but not the reactant-product framing as a formal input-output model. Establishing this framing — reactants consumed, products formed — is the conceptual orientation for the atom rearrangement model in N2.

**Minimum width:** Students correctly identify reactants and products in a named reaction and state that new substances with different properties are formed.

**Typical width:** Students identify reactants and products in multiple reaction types; connect to prior physical vs chemical change knowledge from AC9S8U07 — new substances with different properties are the defining feature; explain that chemical change involves bond breaking and forming; describe observable evidence (colour change, gas production, precipitate, energy change) as indicators of product formation.

**Wider:**
- Investigate why most elements are not found in their elemental state — what does their chemical reactivity tell us about the products formed in natural reactions?
- Examine a combustion reaction in terms of reactants and products — identify all products, including water vapour, not just CO₂

**Success criteria:**
- Identifies reactants and products in a named chemical reaction
- Explains that chemical reactions produce new substances with different properties
- Connects reactant-product change to prior indicators of chemical change (AC9S8U07)

**Terms introduced:**
- **Chemical reaction**: A process in which reactants are converted into products — new substances with different properties are formed. At Year 9, the key conceptual shift is understanding that chemical reactions involve the rearrangement of atoms, not the creation or destruction of matter. Observable indicators (colour change, gas production, precipitate, energy change) provide evidence that a reaction has occurred. (Reactant, Product)

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U07 — Physical and Chemical Changes
- AC9S8U06 — Elements, Compounds and Mixtures

#### 2. Atom rearrangement — chemical reactions as atomic-level redistribution ⚑

Students can model a chemical reaction as the rearrangement of atoms using molecular models or diagrams, showing that atoms are neither created nor destroyed.

**⚑ Hinge — ◎ Scaffold reasoning — unpack the logic explicitly:** Atom rearrangement is the mechanistic foundation of the conservation of mass principle and the conceptual basis for why equations must be balanced. Students who understand chemical change only at the observable level — new substance formed — cannot understand why balancing an equation is a meaningful scientific constraint rather than an arbitrary rule.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning — unpack the logic explicitly: Students commonly treat balancing equations as a procedural task without understanding why it is required. Making atom rearrangement explicit — same atoms, new arrangement — is the conceptual move that makes conservation of mass a necessary consequence rather than an imposed rule.

**Minimum width:** Students use a molecular model or diagram to represent a simple reaction (e.g. hydrogen + oxygen → water) showing the same atoms before and after.

**Typical width:** Students model multiple reactions using molecular models, structural diagrams, or ball-and-stick representations; count atoms on each side to verify they match; explain that bonds between atoms break and reform to produce new arrangements; explain that the type and number of atoms is unchanged — only their arrangement changes; connect this to why mass is conserved.

**Wider:**
- Investigate a reaction in a closed system (sealed container) and demonstrate conservation of mass by weighing before and after — relate the result to the atom rearrangement model
- Use molecular modelling software to build reactant and product structures for a named reaction and confirm atom count matches

**Success criteria:**
- Uses a molecular model or diagram to represent a simple reaction showing atoms before and after
- Counts atoms on each side of a representation and confirms they match
- Explains that atoms are rearranged — not created or destroyed — in chemical reactions

**Terms introduced:**
- **Atom rearrangement**: The mechanistic model of chemical reactions: atoms present in the reactants break their existing bonds, rearrange, and form new bonds to produce products. The type and number of atoms is unchanged — only their arrangement changes. This model explains why mass is conserved (same atoms before and after) and why equations must be balanced (same atom count on both sides). (Bond breaking, Bond forming)

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U06 — Elements, Compounds and Mixtures
- AC9S8U07 — Physical and Chemical Changes

#### 3. Word equations — naming reactants and products

Students can write correct word equations for named chemical reactions, correctly identifying all reactants and products.

**Response modes:**
- ◆ Practise here — fluency matters more than breadth: Word equations require correct identification of all reactants and all products — a common error is omitting water or CO₂ in combustion. Structured practice with self-correction at the word equation level is the prerequisite for symbolic equation writing in N4.

**Minimum width:** Students write a correct word equation for two named reactions.

**Typical width:** Students write word equations for combustion, neutralisation, and displacement reactions; include all products (including water in combustion); use correct arrow notation; explain the limitation of word equations — they name substances but do not show quantities or atom counts.

**Wider:**
- Write word equations for First Nations Australians' chemical processes — e.g. reactions used to convert toxic plants into edible food — and identify reactants and products
- Investigate green chemistry principles — examine a reaction used in industry and write the word equation, identifying any wasteful products and suggesting alternatives

**Success criteria:**
- Writes a correct word equation for at least two named reactions, including all products
- Uses correct arrow notation (reactants → products)
- Explains what word equations show and what they do not (no quantities, no atom counts)

**Terms introduced:**
- **Word equation**: A representation of a chemical reaction using the names of reactants and products connected by an arrow: reactants → products. Word equations identify what reacts and what is formed but do not show quantities, atom counts, or chemical formulas. They are the first formal representation of reactions and the foundation for symbolic equation writing.

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U06 — Elements, Compounds and Mixtures

#### 4. Balanced symbolic equations — demonstrating conservation of mass ⚑

Students can write and balance simple symbolic chemical equations and explain why balancing demonstrates the law of conservation of mass.

**⚑ Hinge — ◆ Practise here — fluency matters more than breadth:** Balancing symbolic equations is the procedural and conceptual endpoint of the descriptor — it is the representation through which conservation of mass is demonstrated. Students who cannot balance equations cannot demonstrate conservation of mass, and persistent errors (changing subscripts, wrong atom counting) are systematic and require explicit correction before the law can be applied.

**Response modes:**
- ◆ Practise here — fluency matters more than breadth: Balancing equations requires procedural precision across two constraints simultaneously: identifying which atoms need balancing, and adjusting only coefficients. Systematic errors — changing subscripts, miscounting polyatomic groups — are common and propagate into all subsequent chemistry work (AC9S10U07).

**Minimum width:** Students correctly balance a simple symbolic equation by adjusting coefficients (not subscripts) and verify by atom counting.

**Typical width:** Students balance symbolic equations for multiple reaction types; explain the rule that subscripts cannot be changed (they define the substance) while coefficients can; verify balance by atom counting on both sides; explain that a balanced equation is a model demonstrating conservation of mass; connect to the atom rearrangement model from N2 — balanced equations show the same atoms before and after.

**Wider:**
- Write and balance symbolic equations for reactions used to obtain elements from their compounds (e.g. metal extraction) and explain what the balanced equation reveals about mass relationships
- Apply conservation of mass quantitatively — given the mass of one reactant, predict the mass of product formed using the molar ratios in a balanced equation (introductory level)

**Success criteria:**
- Balances a simple symbolic equation by adjusting coefficients (not subscripts)
- Verifies a balanced equation by counting atoms of each element on both sides
- Explains that a balanced equation demonstrates conservation of mass — same atoms, same total mass, on both sides

**Terms introduced:**
- **Balanced chemical equation**: A symbolic representation of a chemical reaction using chemical formulas, with coefficients adjusted so that the number of atoms of each element is equal on both sides. Balancing demonstrates conservation of mass: same atoms in, same atoms out. Only coefficients (numbers in front of formulas) may be changed — subscripts (numbers within formulas) define the substance and cannot be altered. (Coefficient, Subscript)

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U06 — Elements, Compounds and Mixtures
- AC9S8U07 — Physical and Chemical Changes

#### 5. Conservation of mass — connecting representation to empirical evidence

Students can use balanced equations to explain the law of conservation of mass, and connect this to experimental evidence from reactions in closed and open systems.

**Response modes:**
- ⊕ Integration point — components come together here: This node integrates atom rearrangement (N2), word equations (N3) and balanced symbolic equations (N4) into an empirically grounded account of conservation of mass. Connecting the symbolic model to experimental data — particularly explaining open vs closed system discrepancies — is the descriptor's 'demonstrate the law of conservation of mass' demand.

**Minimum width:** Students explain why a reaction in a closed system shows no change in total mass, and why an open system may appear to lose or gain mass.

**Typical width:** Students investigate or analyse data from closed system reactions to confirm mass conservation; explain apparent mass changes in open systems (gas escaping or being absorbed from air); use balanced equations to predict mass of products from known reactant masses; evaluate the claim that 'mass is conserved in chemical reactions' against experimental data.

**Wider:**
- Investigate green chemistry principles — if mass is always conserved, why does green chemistry focus on waste? Reconcile atom economy with conservation of mass
- Examine a reaction where mass appears to change in an open system and explain the discrepancy using conservation of mass — what left or entered the system?

**Success criteria:**
- Explains why total mass is conserved in a closed system chemical reaction
- Explains apparent mass changes in open system reactions using conservation of mass
- Uses a balanced equation to connect the symbolic representation to the empirical mass conservation result

**Terms introduced:**
- **Conservation of mass**: The principle that the total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products in a chemical reaction — because atoms are rearranged, not created or destroyed. In a closed system, mass before and after a reaction is identical. In an open system, mass may appear to change because gases escape to or enter from the atmosphere — but total mass (including gases) is still conserved. (Closed system, Open system)
