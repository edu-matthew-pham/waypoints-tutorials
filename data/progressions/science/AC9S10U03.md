# AC9S10U03 — Big bang theory

**Year 10**
**Strand:** Earth and Space Sciences

## ACARA Descriptor

describe how the big bang theory models the origin and evolution of the universe and analyse the supporting evidence for the theory

## Prior Knowledge

Students can model and explain how Earth-sun-moon positions cause eclipses, seasons and tides (AC9S7U03) and describe daily and seasonal environmental changes (AC9S1U02, AC9S2U01). They have scale and cyclic-phenomenon understanding at the solar system level. They have not yet encountered cosmological scale (galaxies, light-years, astronomical units), the origin and timeline of the universe, or the evidence base for the big bang — cosmic microwave background radiation, redshift of galaxies, or Hubble's observations.

## Progression Endpoint

Students describe how the big bang theory models the origin and evolution of the universe — including its major stages and the formation of stars and galaxies — and analyse the supporting evidence including redshift, cosmic microwave background radiation and the observed proportions of matter.

## Waypoint Sequence (5 waypoints, 1 hinge)

#### 1. Scale and structure of the universe

Students can describe the major structural components of the universe using appropriate scale and units, and place Earth within the cosmic hierarchy.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students enter with solar system scale understanding but without cosmological scale. Establishing the size and structure of the universe is prerequisite orientation — students cannot interpret redshift data or the CMB without understanding that observations involve enormous distances and timescales. Scientific notation and unit fluency are required for all subsequent elaboration contexts.

**Minimum width:** Students name the major components of the universe in order of scale (planet, star, solar system, galaxy, universe) and use at least one appropriate unit (light-year or astronomical unit) correctly.

**Typical width:** Students describe the scale of the universe using scientific notation and appropriate units; compare distances within the solar system (AU) to intergalactic distances (light-years); describe galaxies as collections of billions of stars; explain that the observable universe contains billions of galaxies; compare the age of the universe to the age of Earth.

**Wider:**
- Construct a scaled timeline or distance model of the universe and reflect on the limits of human intuition at cosmological scales
- Explore First Nations Australians' knowledges of celestial bodies and origin stories — what observational knowledge of the night sky underlies these accounts?

**Success criteria:**
- Names the major structural components of the universe in order of scale
- Uses light-years or astronomical units correctly in a distance context
- Uses scientific notation to express cosmological distances or ages

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U03

#### 2. The big bang model — origin and timeline of the universe ⚑

Students can describe the big bang theory as a model of the origin and evolution of the universe and outline the major stages from the initial singularity to the formation of stars and galaxies.

**⚑ Hinge:** The model itself is the target all evidence nodes support. Students who do not have a clear mental model of the big bang — what it claims, what the timeline asserts — cannot evaluate whether a piece of evidence (redshift, CMB, matter proportions) supports or challenges the model in N3, N4 and N5. Evidence evaluation requires a prior claim to evaluate against.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: The big bang model makes counterintuitive claims — the universe began in a single event, space itself expanded, all matter and energy originated at a point. Students need to reconstruct the model explicitly before evidence analysis, as students who misunderstand the model (e.g. believing the big bang was an explosion in space) misinterpret every piece of evidence.

**Minimum width:** Students describe the big bang as the origin of space, time and matter approximately 13.8 billion years ago, and identify at least two major stages in the universe's evolution.

**Typical width:** Students construct or interpret a timeline of major events: initial expansion, formation of subatomic particles, formation of atoms, first stars, galaxy formation; explain that the universe has been expanding and cooling since the big bang; describe stars as forming from gravitational collapse of hydrogen clouds; explain the formation of heavier elements in stars.

**Wider:**
- Research recent advances — gravitational waves, dark matter, dark energy — and identify what new knowledge they add to or challenge in the standard big bang model
- Investigate the Australian Square Kilometre Array Pathfinder and explain what types of data it collects and what cosmological questions it addresses

**Success criteria:**
- Describes the big bang as the origin of space, time and matter approximately 13.8 billion years ago
- Outlines at least three major stages in the universe's evolution from the initial event to galaxy formation
- Explains that the universe has been expanding and cooling since the big bang

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U03

#### 3. Redshift and the expanding universe ★

Students can explain how the redshift of light from distant galaxies provides evidence that the universe is expanding, and connect this to the big bang model.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students commonly confuse redshift with the object emitting red light, rather than understanding it as a wavelength shift indicating motion. The Doppler analogy (sound pitch from a moving source) is the standard bridge, but students need explicit connection to the big bang model — redshift is evidence of expansion, not just motion.
- ↔ Widen contexts: Hubble's observations, modern redshift surveys, and the distinction between local and cosmological recession are distinct investigative contexts that build the 'analyse evidence' capacity the descriptor demands.

**Minimum width:** Students describe that light from distant galaxies is redshifted (shifted toward longer wavelengths) and explain that this indicates galaxies are moving away from us.

**Typical width:** Students explain the Doppler effect as the mechanism behind redshift; describe Hubble's observations that more distant galaxies have greater redshift; explain that an expanding universe is consistent with — and predicted by — the big bang model; explain that looking at distant objects is looking back in time.

**Wider:**
- Examine real redshift data from galaxy spectra and calculate recession velocity — what does the relationship between distance and velocity (Hubble's Law) show?
- Investigate blue shift as a contrasting case — which galaxies are blue-shifted and what does this indicate about local vs cosmological motion?

**Success criteria:**
- Explains that redshift of light from distant galaxies indicates they are moving away from us
- Connects the expanding universe (evidenced by redshift) to the big bang model
- Describes Hubble's observations as a key evidential basis for the expanding universe

**Related standards:**
- AC9S9U04

#### 4. Cosmic microwave background radiation and matter proportions

Students can describe how cosmic microwave background radiation and the observed proportions of matter in the universe provide independent lines of evidence supporting the big bang theory.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts: CMB and matter proportions are qualitatively different evidence types from redshift — one is electromagnetic, one is compositional. Students need both to understand what 'multiple lines of evidence' means in a scientific context. Each evidence type exercises the 'analyse' demand from a different angle.

**Minimum width:** Students describe that the CMB is a faint radiation field detected everywhere in the universe and explain what it indicates about the early universe.

**Typical width:** Students explain that the CMB is the thermal afterglow of the early hot dense universe, predicted by the big bang model and detected in 1965; describe that the proportions of hydrogen, helium and other matter in the universe match big bang nucleosynthesis predictions; explain these as two independent lines of evidence, distinct from redshift.

**Wider:**
- Compare the CMB discovery to the process of theory validation described in AC9S10H01 — how did an unexpected observation confirm a theoretical prediction?
- Investigate dark matter and dark energy as current anomalies in the big bang model — what evidence points to their existence, and how do they challenge or extend the model?

**Success criteria:**
- Describes cosmic microwave background radiation and explains what it indicates about the early universe
- Explains that the proportions of matter (hydrogen, helium) in the universe are consistent with big bang predictions
- Identifies CMB and matter proportions as independent lines of evidence, separate from redshift

**Related standards:**
- AC9S10H01

#### 5. Analysing the evidence base — how multiple lines support the theory ★

Students can analyse how redshift, CMB and matter proportions together constitute a mutually supporting evidence base for the big bang theory, and explain what each line independently demonstrates.

**Response modes:**
- ⊕ Integration point: This is the descriptor's full 'analyse the supporting evidence' demand. Students synthesise the three evidence strands — redshift (N3), CMB and matter proportions (N4) — into an account of why the big bang theory is the accepted model, and evaluate the evidence base as a whole.
- ↔ Widen contexts: Gravitational waves, dark matter, First Nations astronomical knowledge, and the technology underpinning data collection are all genuine width contexts for evidence analysis at this level.

**Minimum width:** Students explain what each of three evidence types independently demonstrates and describe how they converge on the same theoretical model.

**Typical width:** Students evaluate the strength of the evidence base as a whole — what would need to be true for all three lines to be consistent without the big bang model being correct?; describe the role of technology in generating new astronomical data; connect to how scientific consensus forms around converging evidence; acknowledge remaining open questions (dark matter, dark energy).

**Wider:**
- Research First Nations Australians' knowledge of celestial bodies and compare the observational foundations of different knowledge systems — what counts as evidence in different epistemological frameworks?
- Investigate the discovery of gravitational waves and evaluate how it adds to or extends the evidence base for cosmological models

**Success criteria:**
- Analyses how at least three lines of evidence independently support the big bang theory
- Explains what each evidence type demonstrates and why convergence strengthens the theory
- Acknowledges at least one open question or area of ongoing investigation in cosmology

**Related standards:**
- AC9S10H01
