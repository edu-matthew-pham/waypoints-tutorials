# AC9S9U04 — Wave and particle models

**Year 9**
**Strand:** Physical Sciences

## ACARA Descriptor

use wave and particle models to describe energy transfer through different mediums and examine the usefulness of each model for explaining phenomena

## Prior Knowledge

Students can explain how light travels in straight paths and describe reflection and refraction (AC9S5U03). They can explain energy transfer and transformation in electrical circuits (AC9S6U03) and classify energy as kinetic or potential (AC9S8U05). They can use particle theory to explain physical properties of substances (AC9S7U05). They have encountered both particle and wave ideas separately but have not yet applied them as competing or complementary models for explaining energy transfer, or evaluated which model is more useful for a given phenomenon.

## Progression Endpoint

Students use wave and particle models to describe how energy is transferred through different mediums — including sound, heat and light — and examine the usefulness and limitations of each model for explaining phenomena including electromagnetic radiation.

## Waypoint Sequence (5 waypoints, 1 hinge)

#### 1. The particle model of energy transfer — conduction and convection ★

Students can explain conduction and convection using the particle model, describing how particle motion and collisions transfer energy through matter.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students have prior particle theory (AC9S7U05) applied to physical properties but not to energy transfer. Explicitly connecting particle motion to energy transfer — collision as the mechanism, not just 'heat moves through the material' — establishes the particle model as an explanatory tool before the wave model is introduced.
- ↔ Widen contexts: Conduction and convection in varied material and cultural contexts build the model's explanatory range before it is compared with the wave model.

**Minimum width:** Students explain conduction as energy transfer through particle collisions in a solid, and convection as bulk movement of particles carrying energy in a fluid.

**Typical width:** Students apply the particle model to explain why metals conduct heat well (closely packed particles, efficient collision transfer) while insulators do not; explain convection currents as density-driven particle movement; connect to AC9S7U05's particle theory; describe how First Nations Australians' traditional clothing and bedding exploit or minimise conduction and convection.

**Wider:**
- Investigate insulation designs from First Nations Australians' clothing and bedding across different climate regions — what particle-level mechanisms do the materials exploit?
- Design a fair test comparing the insulating properties of two materials and explain the results using the particle model

**Success criteria:**
- Explains conduction using particle collisions as the transfer mechanism
- Explains convection using bulk particle movement driven by density differences
- Applies the particle model to explain why a named material is a good conductor or insulator

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U05
- AC9S8U05

#### 2. The wave model of energy transfer — sound ⚑

Students can describe sound as a wave and explain how sound energy is transferred through a medium using the wave model, including the relationship between wave properties and sound characteristics.

**⚑ Hinge:** The wave model of sound is the first full application of wave properties (frequency, amplitude, wavelength, medium-dependence) as a coherent framework. Students who do not secure the wave model for sound cannot extend it to electromagnetic radiation in N4, and cannot evaluate which model is more useful for which phenomenon — the descriptor's core analytical demand.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students have encountered sound in everyday contexts but not as a formal wave model with defined properties. Establishing wave properties (frequency, amplitude, wavelength, medium-dependence) through sound — the most concrete wave context — is the scaffold for the more abstract electromagnetic wave model in N4.

**Minimum width:** Students describe sound as a longitudinal wave and explain that it requires a medium, using compression and rarefaction to describe how energy is transferred.

**Typical width:** Students relate frequency to pitch and amplitude to loudness; model wave transfer using a slinky spring; explain why sound cannot travel through a vacuum; describe how the medium's properties affect sound transmission speed; investigate sound transmission in First Nations traditional instruments.

**Wider:**
- Investigate how material selection affects sound energy transfer in a First Nations traditional instrument — explain the design choices using the wave model
- Compare sound transmission speed in air, water and solids and explain the differences using particle spacing and bonding

**Success criteria:**
- Describes sound as a longitudinal wave requiring a medium
- Uses compression and rarefaction to explain how sound energy is transferred
- Relates frequency to pitch and amplitude to loudness with examples

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U05

#### 3. The particle model of electricity — static charge and current

Students can explain static electricity and electrical current using the particle model of charge, and connect particle behaviour to concepts of voltage, conductors and insulators.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students have prior knowledge of circuits (AC9S6U03) but at the observable level — components, connections, current as a functional concept. The particle model reframes current as electron flow and extends the model to static electricity, which has no circuit equivalent. This is a model extension, not revision.

**Minimum width:** Students explain static electricity as the accumulation of charge due to electron transfer, and electrical current as the flow of electrons through a conductor.

**Typical width:** Students explain charging by friction, induction and contact using electron transfer; describe current as directed electron flow driven by voltage; explain conductors as materials with free electrons and insulators as materials without; connect to electrical circuit concepts from AC9S6U03; explain electrostatic discharge as rapid electron movement to neutralise charge imbalance.

**Wider:**
- Investigate an electrostatics phenomenon (lightning, Van de Graaff generator) and explain it fully using the particle model of charge
- Examine how semiconductor materials — with intermediate conductivity — challenge the simple conductor/insulator binary, and explore applications in electronics

**Success criteria:**
- Explains static electricity as electron transfer between materials producing charge imbalance
- Describes electrical current as directed electron flow through a conductor
- Distinguishes conductors from insulators using the particle model (free vs bound electrons)

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U05
- AC9S6U03

#### 4. The wave model of light and electromagnetic radiation ★

Students can describe light and electromagnetic radiation as waves and explain how the wave model accounts for phenomena including reflection, refraction and the electromagnetic spectrum.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts: Applying the wave model across the full EM spectrum — from radio to gamma — and to real communication and medical technologies builds the model's explanatory range needed for the 'usefulness' evaluation in N5.

**Minimum width:** Students describe light as a transverse wave and use the wave model to explain reflection and refraction.

**Typical width:** Students describe the electromagnetic spectrum as a family of transverse waves varying in frequency and wavelength; explain that all EM waves travel at the same speed in a vacuum; connect wave frequency to energy (radio → microwave → infrared → visible → UV → X-ray → gamma); explain how EM wave properties determine their use in communication technologies; apply the wave model to explain reflection and refraction from AC9S5U03.

**Wider:**
- Investigate the forms of electromagnetic radiation used in different modern communication technologies — what wave properties make each suitable for its application?
- Examine how the wave model explains UV damage to skin and X-ray imaging — what wave property is the key variable?

**Success criteria:**
- Describes light as a transverse wave and the EM spectrum as a family of waves differing in frequency and wavelength
- Uses the wave model to explain reflection and refraction
- Explains how wave frequency relates to energy across the electromagnetic spectrum

**Related standards:**
- AC9S5U03
- AC9S8U05

#### 5. Evaluating model usefulness — wave vs particle for different phenomena

Students can evaluate the usefulness of wave and particle models for explaining different energy transfer phenomena and identify where each model succeeds or fails.

**Response modes:**
- ⊕ Integration point: This node is the descriptor's 'examine the usefulness of each model' demand. Students bring both models — particle (N1, N3) and wave (N2, N4) — into comparative evaluation across phenomena. The key conceptual move is recognising that models are domain-specific tools, not universal truths.

**Minimum width:** Students identify one phenomenon better explained by the wave model and one better explained by the particle model, and explain why in each case.

**Typical width:** Students systematically evaluate wave and particle models against phenomena: sound (wave model — medium required, wave properties), conduction (particle model — collision mechanism), light (wave model accounts for diffraction, reflection, refraction; particle model explains photoelectric effect); introduce the concept of photons as a case where neither model alone is sufficient; explain that models are tools with domains of usefulness, not literal descriptions.

**Wider:**
- Research the wave-particle duality of light — what experimental evidence supports each model, and how does the concept of photons attempt to reconcile them?
- Evaluate the particle model of light (photons) for explaining the photoelectric effect and discuss what this reveals about the limits of classical models

**Success criteria:**
- Evaluates the usefulness of each model for at least two distinct phenomena
- Identifies at least one phenomenon where the wave model is more useful and one where the particle model is more useful
- Explains that scientific models are tools with domains of usefulness rather than literal descriptions of reality

**Related standards:**
- AC9S5U03
- AC9S7U05
- AC9S8U05
