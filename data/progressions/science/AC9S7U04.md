# AC9S7U04 — Forces and Motion

**Year 7**
**Strand:** Physical Sciences (Understanding)
**Planning mode:** A — Concept-certifying

## ACARA Descriptor

investigate and represent balanced and unbalanced forces, including gravitational force, acting on objects, and relate changes in an object's motion to its mass and the magnitude and direction of forces acting on it

## ACARA Elaborations

- investigating the effects of applying different forces to familiar objects of the same and different mass
- analysing the effect of balanced and unbalanced forces on an object's motion, such as starting, stopping and changing direction
- measuring the magnitude of a force using a force meter and representing the magnitude and direction of forces acting on an object using force arrow diagrams
- investigating how Earth's gravitational force is the attractive force which pulls objects to the centre of Earth and its magnitude is related to the mass of an object
- investigating how simple machines such as levers and pulleys are used to change the magnitude of force needed to perform a task
- examining how gravity affects objects in space, including moons, planets, stars, galaxies and black holes
- analysing the forces acting on boomerangs and how early First Peoples of Australia designed an air foil profile which allowed for multiple variations and applications
- investigating the effect of forces through the application of simple machines, such as the bow and arrows used by Torres Strait Islander Peoples or the spearthrowers used by First Peoples of Australia

## Prior Knowledge

Students can identify pushes and pulls as forces and know that gravity pulls objects toward Earth. They have not represented forces as vectors, analysed balanced/unbalanced states, or applied F=ma reasoning.

## Progression Endpoint

Students represent and explain how balanced and unbalanced forces, including gravity, relate to changes in an object's motion in terms of mass, magnitude and direction.

## Width Tiers

**Width varies by:** phenomena and contexts

**Minimum width:** Represent balanced and unbalanced forces acting on a familiar object using force arrow diagrams; explain how an unbalanced force changes an object's motion.

**Typical width:** Investigate effects of different forces on objects of same and different mass; measure force magnitude; represent forces using diagrams; explain how gravity relates to mass; analyse balanced and unbalanced forces causing starting, stopping and direction change.

**Wider:**
- Investigate how simple machines (levers, pulleys) change the magnitude of force needed to perform a task
- Examine how gravity affects objects in space including moons, planets, stars and black holes
- Analyse the forces acting on boomerangs and how First Peoples of Australia designed an aerofoil profile enabling multiple variations and applications
- Investigate how spearthrowers used by First Peoples of Australia or bow and arrows used by Torres Strait Islander Peoples apply force principles through simple machines

## Waypoint Sequence (5 waypoints, 3 hinges, 3 width opportunities)

#### 1. Forces as vectors: magnitude and direction ⚑

Students can represent forces on an object as vectors showing correct magnitude and direction and name the forces

**⚑ Hinge — ◆ Practise here — fluency matters more than breadth:** Force diagram errors — wrong arrow direction, omitted forces, incorrect relative sizes — propagate into every subsequent node. Nodes 2-5 all require accurate force diagrams as reasoning tools; students with imprecise diagrams cannot correctly identify balanced/unbalanced states.

**Response modes:**
- ◆ Practise here — fluency matters more than breadth: Representing forces as vectors — correct arrow direction, relative size, and force naming — is a precision skill students commonly approximate. They confuse spacing and direction, or omit forces (e.g. normal force). Repeated practice with feedback on diagram completeness and accuracy is what helps, not broader contexts.

**Minimum width:** Represent at least two forces on an object using arrows with correct relative size and direction; name the forces

**Typical width:** Draw force diagrams for 4 different scenarios (stationary, accelerating, decelerating, falling); justify arrow choices

**Wider:**
- Measure the magnitude of forces using a force meter; represent magnitude and direction using force arrow diagrams
- Analyse a real photograph of an object in motion — identify all forces acting and represent them in a diagram
- Investigate forces acting on boomerangs and how First Peoples of Australia designed an aerofoil profile — represent forces involved
- Limited width — representing forces as vectors is a precision skill that does not vary meaningfully across contexts. More scenarios build fluency but the conceptual content is fixed. The genuine width comes at later nodes where force diagrams are used as reasoning tools.

**Success criteria:**
- Represents at least 2 forces on an object with correct arrow direction and relative size
- Names each force represented
- Diagram is consistent with the described scenario

**Terms introduced:**
- **Force**: A push or pull acting on an object, measured in newtons (N). Forces are vector quantities — they have both magnitude (size) and direction. Multiple forces can act on an object simultaneously, and their combined effect determines whether the object's motion changes. (Vector, Magnitude, Direction, Normal force, Aerofoil, Simple machines)
- **Force diagram**: A diagram showing all forces acting on an object as vector arrows, with correct relative magnitude and direction. Force diagrams are reasoning tools — they are how students identify whether forces are balanced or unbalanced and predict the resulting motion. Also called free body diagrams.

#### 2. Balanced forces and equilibrium ⚑ ★

Students can identify whether forces on an object are balanced or unbalanced and predict the resulting motion state

**⚑ Hinge — ↔ Widen contexts — more examples resolve misconceptions here:** The misconception that balanced forces mean no forces acting propagates directly into Node 3 — students who hold it cannot correctly predict motion states or explain why objects at constant velocity have balanced forces.

**★ Width opportunity:** The engineering structure direction offers advancing students genuinely rich application — analysing how real structures maintain equilibrium connects force balance to design principles and requires students to identify all forces in a complex system, not just a simplified two-force scenario.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts — more examples resolve misconceptions here: The misconception that balanced forces mean no forces — rather than equal and opposite forces — is extremely common and propagates into every subsequent node. Students who hold this misconception cannot correctly predict motion states or apply Newton's First Law. Working across multiple scenario types (stationary, moving at constant velocity, engineering structures) exposes and resolves the misconception — more contexts, not more repetition of the same scenario.

**Minimum width:** Define balanced forces; correctly identify whether forces are balanced in a given diagram; state that balanced forces mean no change in motion

**Typical width:** Analyse 6 scenarios — classify each as balanced or unbalanced; predict the motion state for each with reasoning

**Wider:**
- Analyse the effect of balanced and unbalanced forces on an object's motion including starting, stopping and changing direction
- Investigate a real engineering structure (e.g. bridge, building) — identify how balanced forces are maintained and what happens if equilibrium is disturbed
- Investigate how simple machines such as levers and pulleys change the magnitude of force needed — represent forces involved and identify whether forces are balanced

**Success criteria:**
- Correctly identifies balanced vs unbalanced forces in a given diagram
- States that balanced forces mean no change in motion — not that no forces are acting
- Predicts the motion state from a force diagram

**Terms introduced:**
- **Balanced forces**: Forces acting on an object that are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, resulting in no change to the object's motion. An object with balanced forces either stays stationary or continues moving at constant velocity — balanced does not mean 'no forces acting.' (Equilibrium, Newton's First Law, Constant velocity)

#### 3. Unbalanced forces and changes in motion ★

Students can explain that unbalanced forces cause a change in motion and represent this with force diagrams

**★ Width opportunity:** The fair test design direction gives advancing students genuine investigative richness — designing a test to isolate the effect of force magnitude on acceleration requires planning controls, identifying variables, and interpreting data as evidence for a relationship. The First Peoples spearthrower direction connects force analysis to cultural design knowledge.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts — more examples resolve misconceptions here: Students frequently apply unbalanced force reasoning only to obvious acceleration scenarios (car speeding up) and miss direction changes (turning, projectile) or deceleration as force-caused changes. Multiple motion-change contexts — starting, stopping, turning, falling — build the generalised understanding that any change in motion requires an unbalanced force.

**Minimum width:** Explain that unbalanced forces cause a change in motion (speed or direction); give two examples with force diagrams

**Typical width:** Analyse motion scenarios — identify the unbalanced force, predict the change in motion, draw the force diagram

**Wider:**
- Investigate the effects of applying different forces to familiar objects of the same and different mass — identify the pattern between force magnitude and rate of motion change
- Design a fair test to investigate how the magnitude of an unbalanced force affects the rate of motion change
- Investigate forces through the application of traditional tools such as spearthrowers used by First Peoples of Australia — analyse the forces involved and how tool design optimises the unbalanced force

**Success criteria:**
- States that unbalanced forces cause a change in motion
- Gives 2 examples with correct force diagrams
- Identifies both speed and direction as possible changes

**Terms introduced:**
- **Unbalanced forces**: Forces acting on an object that do not cancel out — the net force is not zero. Unbalanced forces cause a change in motion: the object speeds up, slows down, or changes direction. Any change in motion requires an unbalanced force. (Spearthrower)

- AC9S7I02 — Plan reproducible investigations

#### 4. Gravitational force and mass ⚑ ★

Students can distinguish mass from weight, explain gravitational force as dependent on mass, and calculate weight from mass

**⚑ Hinge — ↔ Widen contexts — more examples resolve misconceptions here:** Mass/weight confusion propagates directly into AC9S10U05 (Newton's Laws of Motion) — F=ma is meaningless if students conflate mass and weight, and gravitational force scenarios in Newton's Laws require the distinction to be functional. Students who hold this misconception produce systematically wrong force calculations three years later.

**★ Width opportunity:** The space direction — gravity affecting moons, planets, stars and black holes — gives advancing students genuinely rich territory for applying the mass-gravity relationship at astronomical scale. The misconception refutation direction is also rich: constructing an evidence-based argument against a common belief requires integrating force diagram reasoning with experimental data.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts — more examples resolve misconceptions here: Mass/weight confusion is the most persistent misconception in this standard — students who conflate the two make systematic errors in force calculations and cannot correctly explain gravitational scenarios. Encountering gravitational force across multiple contexts (Earth, Moon, Mars, space) where mass stays constant but weight changes forces the distinction to become functional rather than definitional.

**Minimum width:** Distinguish mass from weight; explain gravitational force as dependent on mass; calculate weight given mass and gravitational field strength

**Typical width:** Compare gravitational force on the same object on Earth, the Moon, and Mars — explain differences using gravitational field strength; represent using force diagrams

**Wider:**
- Investigate how Earth's gravitational force pulls objects to the centre of Earth and how its magnitude relates to mass
- Examine how gravity affects objects in space including moons, planets, stars, galaxies and black holes — explain patterns in terms of mass and distance
- Evaluate a common misconception (e.g. 'objects fall at different rates based on mass') using experimental evidence; construct a refutation using force diagrams and particle reasoning

**Success criteria:**
- Correctly distinguishes mass from weight with definitions
- Calculates weight correctly given mass and g
- Explains gravitational force as dependent on mass — not just Earth's pull

**Terms introduced:**
- **Gravitational force**: The attractive force between any two objects that have mass. On Earth, gravitational force pulls objects toward the centre of the Earth. The strength of gravitational force depends on mass — more massive objects experience a greater gravitational force. Extends gravitational pull from AC9S7U03 with the mass-dependence relationship. (Gravitational field strength)
- **Mass**: The amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms (kg). Mass is a property of the object itself — it does not change with location. Mass determines how much gravitational force an object experiences and how much it resists acceleration.
- **Weight**: The gravitational force acting on an object, measured in newtons (N). Weight = mass × gravitational field strength (W = mg). Unlike mass, weight changes with location because gravitational field strength varies between planets, moons and other bodies.

#### 5. Relating force, mass and motion change

Students can describe the qualitative relationship between force, mass and acceleration and interpret data from force investigations

**Response modes:**
- ⊕ Integration point — components come together here: This node integrates force vector representation (Node 1), balanced/unbalanced force reasoning (Nodes 2-3), and gravitational force (Node 4) into a qualitative account of how force, mass and motion change relate. Students who can handle each component separately but cannot reason about which of two objects accelerates more under the same force have not integrated the model.

**Minimum width:** State the relationship between force, mass and acceleration qualitatively; predict whether a heavier or lighter object will accelerate more under the same force

**Typical width:** Analyse data from a force investigation — identify the pattern between force, mass and acceleration; represent graphically

**Wider:**
- Evaluate a real-world application (e.g. vehicle safety, rocket launch) — explain how engineers manipulate force and mass to achieve desired acceleration outcomes
- Investigate how simple machines change force magnitude for a task; analyse the trade-offs between force and distance involved
- Analyse the forces acting on a boomerang in flight — explain how design variables (aerofoil shape, mass, throwing force) affect the motion outcome

**Success criteria:**
- States the qualitative relationship between force, mass and acceleration
- Correctly predicts which object accelerates more under the same force
- Identifies the pattern in data from a force investigation

**Terms introduced:**
- **Acceleration**: A change in an object's velocity — its speed, direction, or both. At Year 7, acceleration is understood qualitatively: a greater unbalanced force produces a greater acceleration, and a greater mass produces a smaller acceleration for the same force. (Deceleration)
