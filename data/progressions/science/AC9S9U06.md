# AC9S9U06 — Atomic Model and Radioactive Decay

**Year 9**
**Strand:** Chemical Sciences
**Planning mode:** A — Concept-certifying

## ACARA Descriptor

explain how the model of the atom changed following the discovery of electrons, protons and neutrons and describe how natural radioactive decay results in stable atoms

## ACARA Elaborations

- comparing the mass and charge of protons, neutrons and electrons
- examining how the discovery of electrons, protons and neutrons resulted from experimental evidence
- explaining that differences in the number of neutrons in atoms of the same element results in isotopes
- describing how different unstable isotopes decay such as radon-222, iodine-131 and cobalt-60
- defining half-life and simulating radioactive decay
- investigating how radiocarbon dating has been used to establish First Peoples' presence for more than 60,000 years
- identifying applications of radioactivity in medicine and industry
- discussing how mass and energy are connected at all scales

## Prior Knowledge

Students can use particle theory to explain physical properties of substances (AC9S7U05) and classify matter as elements, compounds and mixtures using symbols and formulas (AC9S8U06). They have a functional particle model but it is the simple undifferentiated particle model — particles have no internal structure. They have not yet encountered subatomic particles, the historical development of atomic models, or the concept of nuclear instability and radioactive decay.

## Progression Endpoint

Students explain how experimental evidence drove the development of the nuclear model of the atom through the discovery of electrons, protons and neutrons, and describe how unstable isotopes undergo radioactive decay (alpha, beta, gamma) to produce stable atoms — including half-life as a measure of decay rate.

## Width Tiers

**Width varies by:** phenomena and contexts

**Minimum width:** Describe at least two atomic model revisions with the evidence that prompted each; explain what an isotope is; describe at least two types of radioactive decay with examples; define half-life and use it to calculate remaining mass.

**Typical width:** Trace atomic model development from Dalton through Thomson, Rutherford to Bohr with evidence for each revision; explain isotopes as same-element different-neutron atoms; describe alpha, beta and gamma decay; apply half-life calculations; identify applications of radioactivity in medicine and industry.

**Wider:**
- Examine how Thomson, Millikan and Rutherford experiments provide consistent independent evidence for the nuclear model (AC9S9H01)
- Investigate how radiocarbon and other dating methods established that First Peoples of Australia have been present for more than 60,000 years
- Examine medical and industrial applications of radioactivity — cancer diagnosis and treatment, flaw detection in aircraft components (AC9S9H02)
- Explore how understanding of mass-energy equivalence connects conservation of energy to nuclear processes

## Waypoint Sequence (5 waypoints, 2 hinges, 1 width opportunity)

#### 1. The nuclear model of the atom — protons, neutrons and electrons ⚑

Students can describe the structure of the nuclear atom in terms of protons, neutrons and electrons, and use atomic number and mass number to describe specific atoms.

**⚑ Hinge — ◎ Scaffold reasoning — unpack the logic explicitly:** The nuclear model is the structural foundation all subsequent nodes require. Students who cannot correctly assign protons, neutrons and electrons to an atom from its atomic and mass numbers cannot define isotopes in N2, understand why some isotopes are unstable in N3, or follow decay sequences in N4.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning — unpack the logic explicitly: Students enter with a functional but structure-free particle model. Introducing internal atomic structure requires the prior model to be explicitly extended — particles now have constituent parts. The historical transition from Thomson to Rutherford's model is the elaboration's evidence-based framing for this conceptual shift.

**Minimum width:** Students describe the nucleus as containing protons and neutrons, with electrons surrounding it, and use atomic number to identify an element.

**Typical width:** Students describe the relative mass and charge of protons, neutrons and electrons; explain that atomic number equals proton number and determines the element's identity; explain that mass number equals protons plus neutrons; describe electrons as occupying shells around the nucleus; connect to the prior undifferentiated particle model as a simplification that is now being replaced.

**Wider:**
- Compare the mass and charge of subatomic particles quantitatively — what does the near-zero mass of the electron reveal about where most atomic mass is located?
- Examine how the discovery of the nucleus changed scientific understanding — what did Rutherford's gold foil experiment show that Thomson's plum pudding model could not explain?

**Success criteria:**
- Describes the nuclear atom: protons and neutrons in the nucleus, electrons in shells
- Uses atomic number and mass number to determine proton, neutron and electron counts
- Describes the relative mass and charge of protons, neutrons and electrons

**Terms introduced:**
- **Atom**: The smallest unit of an element that retains the element's identity. At Year 9, the simple undifferentiated particle model is replaced by the nuclear model: a dense central nucleus containing protons (positive charge, relative mass 1) and neutrons (no charge, relative mass 1), surrounded by electrons (negative charge, negligible mass) in shells. The number of protons (atomic number) determines which element the atom is. (Proton, Neutron, Electron, Nucleus)
- **Atomic number**: The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom — the defining characteristic of an element. All atoms of the same element have the same atomic number; changing the atomic number changes the element. Atomic number also determines the element's position in the periodic table. (Mass number)

#### 2. Isotopes — same element, different neutron numbers

Students can define isotopes as atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons, and explain why some isotopes are unstable.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning — unpack the logic explicitly: Isotopes require students to understand that atomic identity is determined by proton number alone — same protons, different neutrons, same element. This is the prerequisite for understanding why only some atoms decay (unstable neutron-to-proton ratios) in N3.

**Minimum width:** Students define isotopes correctly and identify that unstable isotopes undergo radioactive decay.

**Typical width:** Students describe naturally occurring isotopes of common elements (carbon-12/13/14, uranium-235/238); explain that isotopes have the same chemical properties (same proton number) but different masses; explain that neutron-to-proton ratio affects nuclear stability; describe unstable isotopes as those with configurations that tend toward decay; connect carbon-14 specifically to radiocarbon dating.

**Wider:**
- Investigate how radiocarbon dating has been used to establish that First Peoples of Australia have been present for more than 60,000 years — what does the carbon-14 decay rate reveal about sample age?
- Compare stable and unstable isotope distributions for a named element and identify what neutron-to-proton ratios characterise stability

**Success criteria:**
- Defines isotopes as atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons
- Identifies that some isotopes are unstable and undergo radioactive decay
- Uses isotope notation (e.g. carbon-14) correctly

**Terms introduced:**
- **Isotope**: Atoms of the same element (same proton number) with different numbers of neutrons (different mass number). Isotopes have identical chemical properties (determined by protons/electrons) but different nuclear properties — some isotopes are stable while others are unstable and undergo radioactive decay. Carbon-12 (stable) and carbon-14 (radioactive) are isotopes of carbon. (Nuclear stability, Carbon-14)

- AC9S8U06 — Elements, Compounds and Mixtures

#### 3. Types of radioactive decay — alpha, beta and gamma ⚑ ★

Students can describe alpha, beta and gamma decay in terms of what is emitted and how each changes the nucleus, and compare their penetrating power.

**⚑ Hinge — ◎ Scaffold reasoning — unpack the logic explicitly:** The three decay types are the mechanistic content of the descriptor's 'describe how natural radioactive decay results in stable atoms' demand. Students who cannot distinguish decay types — particularly students who conflate alpha/beta emission with gamma, or who cannot track changes to atomic and mass numbers — cannot follow decay sequences toward stability in N4 or understand why different isotopes have different applications.

**★ Width opportunity:** Medical and industrial applications of radioactive isotopes are explicitly named in the elaborations and provide genuine investigative width. Each application requires matching decay type and half-life to context — a richer application of the decay model than identification alone.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning — unpack the logic explicitly: Alpha, beta and gamma are qualitatively different in emission type, effect on nucleus, and penetrating power. Students need a clear comparison framework — what is emitted, what changes in the nucleus, how far it penetrates — before applying decay to stability sequences.
- ↔ Widen contexts — more examples resolve misconceptions here: Medical imaging, cancer treatment, materials testing, and radiocarbon dating are each a distinct application context that requires matching decay type to purpose. These contexts build genuine understanding of why decay type matters beyond identification.

**Minimum width:** Students describe what is emitted in each decay type (alpha particle, beta particle, gamma ray) and state one difference in their penetrating power.

**Typical width:** Students describe alpha decay as emission of a helium nucleus (reducing atomic number by 2, mass number by 4); describe beta decay as emission of an electron from the nucleus (increasing atomic number by 1); describe gamma emission as release of high-energy electromagnetic radiation with no change to atomic number or mass; compare penetrating power (alpha — stopped by paper, beta — by aluminium, gamma — reduced by lead/concrete); describe applications in medicine and industry.

**Wider:**
- Investigate the use of radioactive isotopes in medicine — how is the type of decay and half-life matched to the diagnostic or treatment application?
- Examine how gamma radiation is used to check for faults in aircraft and spacecraft materials — what property of gamma makes it suitable for this application?

**Success criteria:**
- Describes what is emitted in alpha, beta and gamma decay
- Explains how alpha and beta decay change the atomic number and mass number of the nucleus
- Compares the penetrating power of alpha, beta and gamma radiation

**Terms introduced:**
- **Radioactive decay**: The spontaneous emission of particles or energy from an unstable nucleus as it transitions toward a more stable configuration. Three types of decay are recognised at this level: alpha (emission of a helium nucleus), beta (emission of an electron from the nucleus), and gamma (emission of high-energy electromagnetic radiation). Decay is random at the individual atom level but statistically predictable for large samples.
- **Alpha decay**: Radioactive decay in which the nucleus emits an alpha particle — a helium nucleus consisting of 2 protons and 2 neutrons. Alpha decay reduces the atomic number by 2 and the mass number by 4, changing the element. Alpha particles are the largest and slowest decay emission — they are stopped by a sheet of paper or a few centimetres of air but are highly ionising at close range.
- **Beta decay**: Radioactive decay in which a neutron in the nucleus converts to a proton and an electron — the electron is emitted as a beta particle. Beta decay increases the atomic number by 1 (new proton) but does not change the mass number, changing the element. Beta particles are smaller and faster than alpha particles — stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium.
- **Gamma radiation**: High-energy electromagnetic radiation emitted from a nucleus, typically following alpha or beta decay when the daughter nucleus is in an excited state. Gamma emission releases energy but does not change the atomic number or mass number — no particles are emitted, only energy. Gamma rays are the most penetrating form of nuclear radiation — reduced but not fully stopped by thick lead or concrete. (Penetrating power)

#### 4. Half-life — rate of radioactive decay

Students can define half-life, use half-life data to calculate remaining activity after a given number of half-lives, and explain how half-life determines the suitability of isotopes for different applications.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning — unpack the logic explicitly: Half-life is a probabilistic concept — it describes the behaviour of large populations of nuclei, not individual atoms. Students commonly expect individual atoms to decay at half-life intervals. Making the probabilistic nature explicit, and connecting the constant half-life to practical application matching, completes the decay model.

**Minimum width:** Students define half-life as the time for half the nuclei in a sample to decay and calculate remaining quantity after two half-lives.

**Typical width:** Students apply half-life calculations across multiple timescales (carbon-14 at 5,730 years, uranium-238 at 4.5 billion years); interpret decay curves graphically; explain why short-lived isotopes are suitable for medical imaging and long-lived ones for geological dating; explain that half-life is constant and unaffected by physical or chemical conditions.

**Wider:**
- Simulate radioactive decay using digital tools or physical models (dice) and compare the simulated decay curve to the mathematical model
- Investigate how uranium-238 half-life data is used in geological dating — what assumptions must be true for radiometric dating to give accurate ages?

**Success criteria:**
- Defines half-life as the time for half of the nuclei in a sample to decay
- Calculates the remaining quantity of a radioisotope after a given number of half-lives
- Explains why half-life determines the suitability of an isotope for a named application

**Terms introduced:**
- **Half-life**: The time taken for half the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay. Half-life is constant for a given isotope and is unaffected by temperature, pressure or chemical environment. After one half-life, 50% remains; after two, 25%; after three, 12.5%. Half-life determines an isotope's suitability for applications: short half-lives for medical imaging (rapid decay = brief exposure), long half-lives for geological dating (slow decay = measurable over millions of years). (Decay curve)

#### 5. Decay sequences — how unstable isotopes reach stability

Students can describe how a series of decay events transforms an unstable isotope into a stable one, and explain the relationship between the descriptor's descriptor claim that 'natural radioactive decay results in stable atoms'.

**Response modes:**
- ⊕ Integration point — components come together here: This node brings together the nuclear model (N1), isotope instability (N2), decay types (N3) and half-life (N4) into a complete account of how decay sequences produce stable atoms — the descriptor's explicit endpoint. Tracing a decay chain exercises all prior nodes in sequence.

**Minimum width:** Students trace a simple one or two-step decay sequence (e.g. radon-222 → polonium-218 via alpha decay) and identify the stable end product.

**Typical width:** Students trace multi-step decay chains (e.g. uranium-238 → lead-206); track changes to atomic number and mass number at each step; explain that each decay step moves the nucleus toward a more stable neutron-to-proton ratio; connect to the historical development of atomic theory — decay provided early evidence for nuclear structure.

**Wider:**
- Investigate how mass and energy are connected in nuclear reactions — what happens to the 'missing mass' in a decay event? (binding energy / E=mc² at a qualitative level)
- Examine how the discovery of radioactive decay changed the understanding of the atom's internal structure — what did natural transmutation reveal that the earlier model could not explain?

**Success criteria:**
- Traces a named decay sequence and identifies the stable end product
- Tracks changes to atomic number and mass number at each decay step
- Explains how the sequence of decay events moves the nucleus toward stability

**Terms introduced:**
- **Decay sequence**: A series of radioactive decay events in which an unstable isotope undergoes successive alpha and/or beta decays, each producing a new element, until a stable isotope is reached. The decay sequence demonstrates the descriptor's claim that 'natural radioactive decay results in stable atoms' — the sequence ends when the neutron-to-proton ratio reaches a stable configuration.
