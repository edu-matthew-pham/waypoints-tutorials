# AC9S8U01 — Cells as basic units

**Year 8**
**Strand:** Biological Sciences

## ACARA Descriptor

recognise cells as the basic units of living things, compare plant and animal cells, and describe the functions of specialised cell structures and organelles

## Prior Knowledge

Students can group living things by external features (AC9SFU01), compare living and non-living things and life cycles (AC9S3U01), explain how structural features enable survival (AC9S5U01), and classify organisms using dichotomous keys (AC9S7U01). All prior learning is at the organism level — external structures, observable features, habitat relationships. There is no prior curriculum treatment of internal cell structure, cellular scale, or sub-organism organisation.

## Progression Endpoint

Students recognise that all living things are made of cells, compare plant and animal cells identifying structures present in each, and describe the functions of key organelles including nucleus, cell membrane, cell wall, cytoplasm, chloroplasts and vacuoles.

## Waypoint Sequence (5 waypoints, 2 hinges)

#### 1. Cellular scale — why cells are invisible and require magnification

Students can describe the scale of cells relative to organisms and explain why microscopes are required to observe them.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students enter with entirely organism-level biology. The shift to cellular scale is the entry point for all subsequent nodes — students who do not grasp why cells are invisible cannot make sense of microscopy as a tool or understand why cell theory required new technology to discover.

**Minimum width:** Students state that cells are microscopic and explain that the microscope was necessary to discover them.

**Typical width:** Students compare cell size to familiar objects using scale diagrams; explain that a human body contains trillions of cells; describe how light microscopes magnify; explain that single-celled organisms are complete living things at microscopic scale; connect the invention of the microscope to cell discovery.

**Wider:**
- Trace the historical development of cell discovery — Hooke, Leeuwenhoek, Schleiden, Schwann — and explain what each contribution added to the emerging cell theory
- Use a digital microscope or photomicrographs to observe prepared slides and describe what is visible at different magnifications

**Success criteria:**
- States that cells are microscopic and not visible to the naked eye
- Explains that microscopes were necessary to discover and observe cells
- Gives an approximate size comparison between a cell and a familiar object

**Related standards:**
- AC9S7U01

#### 2. Cell theory — all living things are made of cells ⚑

Students can state that all living things — including plants, fungi and single-celled organisms — are made of cells, and distinguish single-celled from multicellular organisms.

**⚑ Hinge:** Cell theory universality is the prerequisite for the plant-animal comparison in N4. Students who believe only animals (or only complex organisms) have cells cannot make sense of the plant cell structures in N3 or explain why plant cells have additional organelles. This misconception is documented and persistent.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: The misconception that plants do not have cells, or that only animals have 'real' cells, must be explicitly addressed. Students need to encounter plant cells, fungal cells and bacterial cells as cells before organelle differences are introduced — otherwise the plant-animal comparison in N4 has no foundation.

**Minimum width:** Students state cell theory and give one example each of a single-celled and multicellular organism.

**Typical width:** Students apply cell theory universally — explicitly including plants, bacteria and single-celled organisms that students may not intuitively expect to have cells; explain that cells carry out all life processes; describe that multicellular organisms are organised collections of cells; connect to AC9S3U01's living/non-living distinction — cells are what makes something living.

**Wider:**
- Examine photomicrographs of cells from bacteria, yeast, leaf tissue and human cheek — compare visible structures and discuss what 'cell' means across such different organisms
- Investigate which organisms are single-celled vs multicellular and discuss what this implies about the minimum requirements for life

**Success criteria:**
- States that all living things are made of cells
- Includes plants and single-celled organisms explicitly in this claim
- Distinguishes single-celled from multicellular organisms with examples from each

**Related standards:**
- AC9S3U01
- AC9S7U01

#### 3. Cell structures shared by plant and animal cells — nucleus, membrane, cytoplasm ⚑

Students can identify and describe the function of structures present in both plant and animal cells: nucleus, cell membrane and cytoplasm.

**⚑ Hinge:** The three shared structures are the foundation for the plant-animal comparison in N4. Students who cannot correctly assign functions to nucleus, membrane and cytoplasm cannot identify what is additional in plant cells or explain why those additions exist.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Structure-function pairing for organelles is most securely learned by connecting physical properties to function. The nucleus contains DNA and directs cell activity; the membrane is a boundary with selectivity; cytoplasm is the medium for chemistry. Learning functions as a list without this reasoning produces rapid forgetting.

**Minimum width:** Students state the function of nucleus, cell membrane and cytoplasm.

**Typical width:** Students describe nucleus as the genetic control centre containing DNA; cell membrane as a selectively permeable barrier controlling what enters and exits; cytoplasm as the aqueous medium in which chemical reactions occur; explain that these three structures are universal — present in essentially all cells; connect nucleus to heredity (DNA) in anticipation of AC9S10U01.

**Wider:**
- Investigate what happens when the nucleus is removed from a cell — what does this reveal about its role as control centre?
- Compare the cell membrane to a security checkpoint — what analogy works and where does it break down?

**Success criteria:**
- States the function of nucleus, cell membrane and cytoplasm
- Explains at least one structural feature of each that relates to its function
- Identifies these three as structures present in both plant and animal cells

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U01

#### 4. Plant-specific structures — cell wall, chloroplasts, large central vacuole ★

Students can identify cell wall, chloroplasts and large central vacuole as plant-specific structures and explain the functional significance of each.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Plant-specific structures are most meaningfully understood as solutions to organism-level problems: no skeleton → need rigid wall; autotrophic → need photosynthesis machinery; osmotic needs → need vacuole. This organism-level reasoning connects to prior survival/adaptation knowledge and prepares the structure-function analysis in AC9S8U02.
- ↔ Widen contexts: Cell modelling, tissue comparison within plants, and microscopy each apply the plant-animal comparison in a different representational context. Each reveals different aspects of the structural differences.

**Minimum width:** Students identify the three plant-specific structures and state the function of each.

**Typical width:** Students explain cell wall as rigid structural support (plants lack a skeleton); chloroplasts as sites of photosynthesis (plants are autotrophs); large central vacuole for turgor pressure and storage; explain why animal cells lack these — animals have skeletons, obtain food rather than making it, lack turgor requirements; examine prepared slides or photomicrographs of plant and animal cells; design a physical or digital model justified by function.

**Wider:**
- Compare cells from different plant tissues — why do root cells lack chloroplasts while leaf mesophyll cells have many? What does this reveal about cell specialisation within a plant?
- Design and justify a cell model — for each modelling choice, explain what it represents accurately and what it simplifies

**Success criteria:**
- Identifies cell wall, chloroplasts and large central vacuole as plant-specific structures
- Explains the function of each plant-specific structure
- Connects each plant-specific structure to an organism-level need that animal cells do not have

**Related standards:**
- AC9S5U01

#### 5. Additional organelles — mitochondria, ribosomes and cell specialisation

Students can describe the roles of mitochondria and ribosomes as organelles present in both cell types, and recognise that cells are specialised — different cells have different organelle complements.

**Response modes:**
- ⊕ Integration point: This node integrates the structure-function principle established in N3 and N4 into the broader concept of cell specialisation — different organelle complements for different functions. This is the bridge to AC9S8U02's tissue and organ system analysis, where specialisation is the mechanism enabling tissue formation.

**Minimum width:** Students state the function of mitochondria (energy release) and describe one example of a specialised cell whose organelle complement reflects its function.

**Typical width:** Students describe mitochondria as the site of cellular respiration (energy release for cell activity); ribosomes as protein synthesis sites; explain that the number and type of organelles varies with cell function — muscle cells have many mitochondria, secretory cells have many ribosomes, root hair cells have no chloroplasts; connect organelle complement to the cell's role in the organism; introduce the concept that specialisation is built on the same DNA base.

**Wider:**
- Investigate a cell disorder that results from organelle malfunction — e.g. mitochondrial disease — and explain how failure at the organelle level affects cell, tissue and organism function
- Explore how augmented or virtual reality tools allow 'zooming in' to explore organelle scale within cells

**Success criteria:**
- States the function of mitochondria and ribosomes
- Explains why a named specialised cell has more or fewer of a particular organelle
- Describes cell specialisation as variation in organelle complement reflecting cell function

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U02
