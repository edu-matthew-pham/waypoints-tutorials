# AC9S10U04 — Global climate change

**Year 10**
**Strand:** Earth and Space Sciences

## ACARA Descriptor

use models of energy flow between the geosphere, biosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere to explain patterns of global climate change

## Prior Knowledge

Students can represent the carbon cycle and explain how combustion, photosynthesis and respiration depend on interactions between Earth's spheres (AC9S9U03). They can describe how Earth's tilt and revolution produce seasonal change (AC9S6U02) and model food web energy flow through ecosystems (AC9S7U02). They have not yet examined energy exchange mechanisms between spheres (radiation, conduction, convection), the greenhouse effect as a quantitative balance, or how perturbations to that balance produce measurable global-scale patterns.

## Progression Endpoint

Students use models of energy flow between the geosphere, biosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere to explain patterns of global climate change, including analysing indicators of change and evaluating strategies to reduce or mitigate effects.

## Waypoint Sequence (6 waypoints, 3 hinges)

#### 1. Solar radiation and Earth's energy input ⚑

Students can describe how solar radiation reaches Earth and identify the factors that determine how much energy is absorbed versus reflected by the surface and atmosphere.

**⚑ Hinge:** All subsequent nodes build on the concept of an energy input that can be absorbed, reflected, or re-emitted. Students who treat solar energy as uniformly heating Earth without understanding the absorbed/reflected distinction cannot grasp why surface changes (melting ice, deforestation) alter the energy balance in N2, or why the greenhouse effect is about re-emitted rather than incoming radiation in N3.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students commonly assume the sun simply 'heats the Earth' without differentiating absorbed vs reflected energy. Making the energy input split explicit — and anchoring it to a concrete surface comparison — is the foundation the energy balance model in N2 requires.

**Minimum width:** Students identify the sun as Earth's primary energy source and distinguish between absorbed and reflected radiation using one surface type as an example (e.g. ocean vs ice).

**Typical width:** Students compare absorption and reflection across surface types (ocean, ice, land, vegetation); introduce albedo as a concept; describe why the angle of incidence affects energy input at different latitudes.

**Wider:**
- Investigate albedo data for different surfaces and predict how land-use change affects the local energy budget
- Compare solar energy input at the equator vs poles and explain why this drives atmospheric and oceanic circulation

**Success criteria:**
- Identifies the sun as the primary energy input to Earth's climate system
- Distinguishes absorbed from reflected solar radiation with at least one surface example
- Explains why different surfaces absorb or reflect different proportions of solar radiation

**Related standards:**
- AC9S6U02
- AC9S7U02

#### 2. Energy balance between Earth's spheres ⚑ ★

Students can describe how energy flows between the geosphere, biosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere and explain what it means for Earth to be in energy balance.

**⚑ Hinge:** The energy balance concept is the structural model the entire descriptor is built on. Students who cannot describe multi-sphere energy exchange as a system in balance cannot explain what 'disrupting the balance' means in N3, cannot interpret climate indicators as evidence of imbalance in N5, and cannot evaluate mitigation strategies in N6 in terms of restoring balance.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students commonly think of energy exchange as one-directional (sun heats Earth, Earth cools at night) rather than as a dynamic multi-sphere system in balance. Explicitly building a flow model — even a simple one — is the scaffold N3's greenhouse mechanism requires.
- ↔ Widen contexts: Once the balance concept is secured, varied contexts (ocean heat storage, polar vs tropical energy budgets) build the model literacy students need before interpreting climate indicators. These contexts are not acceleration — they are legitimate width at this year band.

**Minimum width:** Students describe energy flowing between at least two spheres and explain that energy balance means incoming solar energy equals outgoing energy over time.

**Typical width:** Students map energy exchange pathways between all four spheres; describe conduction, convection and radiation as the mechanisms of transfer; explain how the ocean stores and redistributes heat; represent the system as a simplified flow diagram.

**Wider:**
- Use a visualisation or simulation to trace energy through all four spheres and identify where delays or stores exist
- Investigate the role of deep ocean currents in redistributing heat globally and explain their effect on regional climates

**Success criteria:**
- Describes energy exchange between at least three of the four spheres
- Explains energy balance as the condition where incoming and outgoing energy are equal over time
- Identifies the ocean or atmosphere as a store and distributor of heat energy

**Related standards:**
- AC9S9U03
- AC9S7U02

#### 3. The greenhouse effect — trapping outgoing radiation ⚑

Students can explain how greenhouse gases in the atmosphere trap outgoing infrared radiation and describe the role of this mechanism in maintaining Earth's surface temperature.

**⚑ Hinge:** The greenhouse mechanism is the conceptual link between the energy balance model (N2) and the explanation of climate change (N4). Students who conflate the greenhouse effect with ozone depletion, or who cannot locate the mechanism within the energy flow model, cannot explain how increasing greenhouse gas concentrations alter the balance, which is the core demand of the descriptor.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: The greenhouse effect carries two persistent misconceptions: conflation with ozone depletion, and the belief that greenhouse gases trap incoming (rather than outgoing) radiation. These must be addressed explicitly before students can correctly place the mechanism in the energy balance model. Explicit comparison of natural vs enhanced greenhouse effect helps here.

**Minimum width:** Students describe that Earth emits infrared radiation and that greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit it, slowing energy loss to space.

**Typical width:** Students name key greenhouse gases (CO₂, CH₄, H₂O vapour, N₂O); distinguish natural vs enhanced greenhouse effect; explain why Earth would be far colder without the natural greenhouse effect; describe how the atmosphere's greenhouse function fits within the N2 energy balance model.

**Wider:**
- Compare the greenhouse effects of different gases using global warming potential data and explain why CH₄ has greater short-term impact than CO₂ despite lower concentration
- Trace how a molecule of CO₂ emitted by combustion eventually affects the energy balance model built in N2

**Success criteria:**
- Explains that Earth emits infrared radiation and that greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit it
- Distinguishes natural greenhouse effect from enhanced greenhouse effect
- Locates the greenhouse mechanism correctly within the energy balance model from N2 (atmosphere slows outgoing, not incoming, energy)

**Related standards:**
- AC9S9U03

#### 4. Human activity, greenhouse gas concentrations and energy imbalance ★

Students can explain how human activities increase atmospheric greenhouse gas concentrations and describe how this creates an energy imbalance that drives warming.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts: Students have prior knowledge of the carbon cycle from AC9S9U03. Connecting that knowledge to greenhouse gas concentrations via concrete data sources (ice cores, emissions data) builds model literacy and consolidates the prior-year learning in a new context.

**Minimum width:** Students link at least two human activities to increased CO₂ or CH₄ concentrations and explain that higher concentrations trap more outgoing radiation, creating a positive energy imbalance.

**Typical width:** Students map human activity sources (combustion, agriculture, deforestation) to specific gases; describe how the carbon cycle (AC9S9U03) is perturbed by additional CO₂ from fossil fuels; explain feedback: warming → more water vapour → more greenhouse warming; represent the imbalance in the energy flow model.

**Wider:**
- Investigate historical CO₂ concentration data (ice cores, Keeling Curve) and connect concentration changes to energy imbalance magnitude
- Model the carbon cycle perturbation: where does anthropogenic CO₂ go, and why does concentration still increase despite ocean and biosphere uptake?

**Success criteria:**
- Identifies at least two human activities that increase atmospheric greenhouse gas concentrations
- Explains how increased greenhouse gas concentration leads to a positive energy imbalance
- Connects the perturbation of atmospheric CO₂ to the energy flow model from N2 and N3

**Related standards:**
- AC9S9U03

#### 5. Indicators of climate change across Earth's spheres ★

Students can identify and interpret multiple indicators of climate change and explain each as evidence of the energy imbalance described in N4.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts: Indicators span all four spheres and multiple data types. Working across varied contexts — not just atmospheric temperature — builds the 'patterns' reading the descriptor demands and prevents reductive climate change understanding (change = warming only).

**Minimum width:** Students name at least three indicators of climate change (e.g. rising atmospheric temperature, sea level rise, reduced sea ice) and explain one in terms of the energy imbalance model.

**Typical width:** Students interpret real climate data for multiple indicators; explain the mechanism behind each (ocean warming → thermal expansion → sea level rise; melting ice → albedo feedback; warming → species distribution shifts); recognise that indicators are interdependent within the Earth system.

**Wider:**
- Analyse a visualisation of multiple climate indicators over time and identify which sphere each primarily reflects; explain why some indicators lag behind the energy imbalance
- Investigate one indicator in depth — e.g. permafrost thaw — and trace its contribution to a feedback loop within the energy model

**Success criteria:**
- Names at least three distinct climate change indicators across different spheres
- Explains the mechanism behind at least two indicators using the energy flow model
- Interprets a climate data visualisation as evidence of energy imbalance

**Related standards:**
- AC9S9U03
- AC9S7U02

#### 6. Using energy flow models to explain and evaluate responses to climate change

Students can use the energy flow model to explain patterns of global climate change and evaluate strategies designed to reduce or mitigate its effects in terms of the model.

**Response modes:**
- ⊕ Integration point: This node integrates all prior nodes — solar input (N1), energy balance (N2), greenhouse mechanism (N3), human drivers (N4), and indicators (N5) — into a full explanatory and evaluative account. The response mode is synthesis: students must bring the model to bear on novel patterns and strategies, not just recall it. This is the descriptor's full demand.

**Minimum width:** Students use the energy flow model to explain at least one climate change pattern and describe one mitigation or adaptation strategy in terms of how it addresses the energy imbalance or a specific indicator.

**Typical width:** Students explain multiple climate patterns (e.g. why polar regions warm faster, why droughts intensify in some regions) using sphere-interaction reasoning; evaluate strategies (renewable energy, reforestation, carbon capture, geoengineering) against the model; distinguish mitigation (reducing imbalance) from adaptation (responding to effects).

**Wider:**
- Evaluate a proposed geoengineering strategy (e.g. stratospheric aerosol injection) using the energy flow model — what component of the model does it target, and what are the risks of altering that component?
- Predict the trajectory of two climate indicators under different emissions scenarios and justify using the energy balance model

**Success criteria:**
- Applies the energy flow model to explain at least two distinct patterns of global climate change
- Evaluates at least one strategy to reduce climate change and one to mitigate its effects using the model
- Distinguishes mitigation from adaptation and explains each in terms of the energy balance

**Related standards:**
- AC9S9U03
- AC9S7U02
- AC9S10U07
