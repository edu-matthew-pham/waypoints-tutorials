# AC9S9U01 — Body systems and negative feedback

**Year 9**
**Strand:** Biological Sciences

## ACARA Descriptor

compare the role of body systems in regulating and coordinating the body's response to a stimulus, and describe the operation of a negative feedback mechanism

## Prior Knowledge

Students can analyse structure-function relationships across cells, tissues and organs in body systems (AC9S8U02). They have not yet examined how multiple systems coordinate responses to stimuli, or modelled negative feedback as a regulatory mechanism.

## Progression Endpoint

Students compare how the nervous and endocrine systems coordinate responses to stimuli — using models of receptor-command centre-effector pathways — and describe how negative feedback maintains homeostasis, including the effects of feedback system disorders.

## Waypoint Sequence (7 waypoints, 4 hinges)

#### 1. Stimuli and receptors — detecting change ⚑

Students can distinguish internal from external stimuli and identify the receptor type involved in detecting a named stimulus.

**⚑ Hinge:** Receptors are the entry point of the pathway model all subsequent nodes build on. Students who conflate stimulus with receptor, or cannot place the receptor correctly in the pathway, cannot construct the receptor → command centre → effector sequence in N2 or locate where deviation is detected in the feedback loop in N4.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students commonly treat stimulus-response as undifferentiated — 'the body just reacts.' Making the receptor explicit as a distinct, specific detection component is the scaffold that allows the pathway model to be built in N2.

**Minimum width:** Students identify the stimulus type (internal/external) and name an appropriate receptor for one stimulus example.

**Typical width:** Students sort multiple stimuli into internal/external categories; match stimuli to receptor types; explain why receptor specificity matters; distinguish sensory modalities.

**Wider:**
- Investigate what happens when receptor function is impaired — e.g. sensory neuropathy, colour blindness — and predict consequences for the response pathway
- Compare receptor sensitivity thresholds across modalities — why can we detect very faint sounds but not very faint tastes?

**Success criteria:**
- Distinguishes internal from external stimuli with examples
- Identifies an appropriate receptor for a named stimulus and describes its role

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U02

#### 2. Receptor → command centre → effector pathway ⚑

Students can trace the receptor → command centre → effector pathway for a named stimulus, describing the role of each component.

**⚑ Hinge:** The three-component pathway is the structural scaffold N3 and N4 apply directly. Students who cannot place command centre and effector correctly cannot distinguish where nervous vs endocrine signalling differs, and cannot locate the corrective response within the feedback loop.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students commonly skip the command centre and treat stimulus-response as a direct receptor-to-effector connection. Making the command centre explicit — and its role in processing and directing — is the scaffold for comparing neural and endocrine coordination in N3.

**Minimum width:** Students trace the three-component pathway for one named stimulus and describe what each component does.

**Typical width:** Students apply the pathway to multiple stimuli; represent pathways as correctly sequenced flow diagrams; explain why all three components are necessary; identify where signals travel between components.

**Wider:**
- Compare the receptor → command centre → effector model to a technological control system (thermostat, cruise control) — identifying structural parallels and limits of the analogy
- Investigate how pathway disruption at each component produces different failure modes — receptor damage vs command centre damage vs effector damage

**Success criteria:**
- Traces the receptor → command centre → effector pathway for a named stimulus
- Describes the distinct role of each component in the response

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U02

#### 3. Nervous and endocrine systems as coordination mechanisms — signal types and timescales

Students can describe how the nervous and endocrine systems each implement the receptor → command centre → effector pathway, distinguishing signal type, speed, and duration.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students commonly treat the nervous and endocrine systems as entirely separate rather than as two implementations of the same pathway model. Explicitly mapping each system onto the receptor → command centre → effector structure builds the parallel representation needed for comparison in N4.

**Minimum width:** Students describe the nervous system as using electrical signals (fast, short duration) and the endocrine system as using hormonal signals (slower, longer duration) for one named example of each.

**Typical width:** Students trace the nervous and endocrine implementations of the pathway for multiple stimuli; explain why electrical signals travel faster than hormonal signals; identify glands as endocrine command centres and target organs as effectors; explain why some responses use both systems.

**Wider:**
- Map a dual-system response (e.g. the stress response — adrenaline and rapid nerve signals) showing staged timelines for each system
- Examine how drugs affecting synaptic transmission (local anaesthetics, nerve agents) or hormone receptors (hormone therapy, endocrine disruptors) disrupt specific points in each system's pathway

**Success criteria:**
- Describes the nervous system pathway for a named stimulus, identifying electrical signalling and fast, short response
- Describes the endocrine system pathway for a named stimulus, identifying hormonal signalling, bloodstream transport, and slower, sustained response

**Related standards:**
- AC9S8U02

#### 4. Comparing nervous and endocrine coordination ⚑ ★

Students can compare the nervous and endocrine systems on signal type, speed, duration, and specificity, and predict which system coordinates a given stimulus.

**⚑ Hinge:** Comparison of the two systems is the explicit demand of the descriptor's 'compare' verb and the progression endpoint. Students who cannot compare beyond surface-level listing cannot apply the model predictively or evaluate why disorders of one system produce different consequences than disorders of the other.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students commonly list features of each system separately rather than constructing a genuine comparison. Structured comparison tasks — same stimulus, both systems, same dimensions — build the analytical habit the descriptor requires.
- ↔ Widen contexts: Encountering the comparison across multiple stimulus types (temperature, blood glucose, stress, exercise) builds a more generalisable model than a single comparison task.

**Minimum width:** Students produce a comparison of the two systems on at least three dimensions: signal type, response speed, and response duration.

**Typical width:** Students compare across signal type, speed, duration, specificity, and reversibility; explain why different stimuli are coordinated by different systems or both; predict which system coordinates a novel stimulus and justify; use annotated diagrams or comparison tables.

**Wider:**
- Analyse the stress response as a case where both systems coordinate simultaneously — explaining the advantage of dual coordination
- Examine how imaging technologies (MRI, PET scans) have enabled real-time observation of nervous and endocrine activity — connecting technology advances to scientific knowledge (AC9S9H02)

**Success criteria:**
- Compares the nervous and endocrine systems on signal type, speed, and duration
- Predicts which system coordinates a given novel stimulus and justifies the prediction

**Related standards:**
- AC9S9H02

#### 5. Negative feedback architecture — set point, deviation, corrective response, return detection ⚑

Students can describe the four components of a negative feedback mechanism and explain why it maintains stability rather than amplifying change.

**⚑ Hinge:** The four-component abstract model is the scaffold N6 applies to biological cases and N7 applies to disorder analysis. Students who cannot identify set point, deviation, corrective response, and return detection in a generic model cannot locate these elements in a specific biological system or identify which component fails in a disorder.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students commonly understand feedback from examples without being able to articulate the abstract architecture. Making the four components explicit — and distinguishing 'negative' as opposing rather than bad — is the scaffold that allows transfer to novel biological cases in N6 and disorder analysis in N7.

**Minimum width:** Students label and describe the role of each of the four components in a negative feedback loop using a generic or diagrammatic model.

**Typical width:** Students represent negative feedback as a complete flow diagram; explain why the mechanism is called 'negative'; distinguish negative from positive feedback; explain why homeostasis is essential for cell function.

**Wider:**
- Compare negative and positive feedback — identifying where positive feedback is used in biological systems (childbirth, blood clotting) and why amplification is appropriate in those contexts
- Model the feedback architecture using a non-biological analogy and evaluate the strengths and limits of the analogy

**Success criteria:**
- Labels and describes the role of each of the four components of a negative feedback loop
- Explains why the mechanism is described as 'negative' — it opposes rather than amplifies deviation

#### 6. Negative feedback in canonical biological cases — tracing complete loops ★

Students can trace a complete negative feedback loop for at least two named biological examples, correctly identifying all four components in each.

**Response modes:**
- ◆ Practise here: Tracing complete loops with all four components correctly placed requires precision. Common errors — confusing set point with corrective response, omitting return detection — are worth targeted practice before disorder analysis in N7.
- ↔ Widen contexts: Encountering the same four-component architecture across temperature, blood glucose, and blood pressure builds transferability to novel cases and disorder analysis.

**Minimum width:** Students trace the complete negative feedback loop for one biological example (temperature regulation or blood glucose), identifying all four components.

**Typical width:** Students trace feedback loops for multiple homeostatic systems (temperature, blood glucose, blood pressure, hydration); explain why homeostasis is essential for cell function; use annotated flow diagrams with correct directionality.

**Wider:**
- Develop a model, animation or presentation representing a named feedback loop — applying digital communication skills to represent dynamic biological processes (AC9S9I08)
- Investigate how pharmaceutical interventions target specific points in the feedback loop — e.g. beta blockers reducing heart rate response, metformin improving glucose uptake

**Success criteria:**
- Traces a complete negative feedback loop for a named biological example, correctly identifying all four components
- Names at least two biological examples of negative feedback and identifies the set point in each

**Related standards:**
- AC9S9I08

#### 7. Feedback system disorders — locating failure in the loop ★

Students can explain how a disorder of a feedback system disrupts homeostasis by identifying which component of the loop has failed and describing the consequence.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students commonly describe disorders as 'the body not working' without locating the failure within the loop architecture. Explicitly asking 'which component has failed?' applies the four-component model diagnostically and consolidates N5 understanding.
- ↔ Widen contexts: Analysing multiple disorders across different systems reinforces that the same feedback architecture underlies all homeostatic regulation, and that failure at different components produces predictably different consequences.

**Minimum width:** Students explain one feedback system disorder, identifying the affected component (receptor, command centre, effector, or return detection) and describing the homeostatic consequence.

**Typical width:** Students analyse multiple disorders (type 1 diabetes, hypothyroidism, hypertension) by locating the failure point in the loop; explain why the same disorder produces different consequences depending on which component fails; connect disorder analysis to treatment approaches.

**Wider:**
- Investigate how medical devices (insulin pumps, pacemakers, CPAP machines) artificially replace or supplement the failed component — mapping device function onto the four-component architecture
- Evaluate claims about a health product using the feedback model as a diagnostic lens — identifying what feedback mechanism the product claims to support and whether the claim is coherent

**Success criteria:**
- Identifies which component of the feedback loop fails in a named disorder
- Explains the homeostatic consequence of that failure

**Related standards:**
- AC9S9H02
