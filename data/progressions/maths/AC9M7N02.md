# AC9M7N02 — Prime factorisation and exponent notation

**Year 7**
**Strand:** Number

## ACARA Descriptor

represent natural numbers as products of powers of prime numbers using exponent notation

## Prior Knowledge

Students can identify prime and composite numbers and know their basic properties (from AC9M6N02). Students can use factor trees to find factors of numbers.

## Progression Endpoint

Students represent any natural number as a product of powers of prime factors using exponent notation, and apply this to find lowest common multiples and greatest common divisors.

## Waypoint Sequence (3 waypoints, 3 hinges)

#### 1. Exponent notation for repeated multiplication ⚑

Students understand exponent notation as shorthand for repeated multiplication and can convert between expanded and exponent forms.

**⚑ Hinge:** Students who cannot fluently read and write exponent notation cannot record or interpret prime factorisation results in Node 2 — the symbolic system is the medium for all subsequent work in this standard.

**Response modes:**
- ◆ Practise here: Exponent notation is a new symbolic system. Students need fluency reading and writing it before applying it to prime factorisation.

**Minimum width:** Write 2×2×2×2 as 2⁴ and vice versa for small bases and exponents.

**Typical width:** Convert fluently between repeated multiplication and exponent notation for bases 2, 3, 5, 7 and small exponents; state what the base and exponent each mean.

**Wider:**
- Investigate the sequence 2¹, 2², 2³, ... and connect to powers of 2 in computing contexts (bytes, storage).
- Explore 10¹, 10², 10³ and connect to place value notation (links to AC9M7N03).

**Success criteria:**
- Correctly converts between repeated multiplication and exponent notation.
- States the meaning of the base and exponent in a given expression.
- Does not confuse aⁿ with a×n (e.g. does not evaluate 2³ as 6).

**Related standards:**
- AC9M7N03

#### 2. Prime factorisation via factor trees ⚑ ★

Students can express any natural number as a product of prime factors using a factor tree, and write the result using exponent notation.

**⚑ Hinge:** Students who believe prime factorisation is non-unique cannot apply the LCM/GCD algorithm in Node 3 correctly — the entire method depends on there being one canonical factorisation to compare.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts: Students often believe different factor trees can produce different prime factorisations. Seeing multiple paths to the same result across several numbers resolves this misconception.

**Minimum width:** Use a factor tree to find the prime factorisation of a given composite number (e.g. 36 = 2² × 3²).

**Typical width:** Find prime factorisations for a range of composite numbers including larger ones (e.g. 360), write results in exponent notation, and verify by expanding.

**Wider:**
- Show that different factor trees for the same number always yield the same prime factorisation (Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic — informally).
- Investigate which numbers have only one prime factor (powers of primes) and what their factor trees look like.

**Success criteria:**
- Produces a correct factor tree and writes the result in exponent notation.
- Verifies the factorisation by expanding and multiplying.
- Understands that the prime factorisation of a number is unique.

#### 3. LCM and GCD from prime factorisations ⚑ ★

Students use prime factorisations to find the lowest common multiple and greatest common divisor of pairs of natural numbers.

**⚑ Hinge:** Students who cannot apply the systematic LCM/GCD algorithm from prime factorisations revert to listing methods — this blocks the connection to AC9M7N04 fraction simplification and AC9M7N06 common denominator work where the prime factorisation method is the efficient path.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: The algorithm (take highest power of each prime for LCM; lowest power for GCD) requires an explicit systematic scaffold. Students who proceed by listing multiples/factors miss the prime factorisation connection.
- ⊕ Integration point: LCM and GCD require coordinating prime factorisation, exponent comparison, and contextual interpretation simultaneously.

**Minimum width:** Find the LCM and GCD of two numbers using their prime factorisations.

**Typical width:** Apply the LCM and GCD in context: e.g. find when two cyclic events coincide (LCM), or find the largest equal shares (GCD).

**Wider:**
- Explore the relationship LCM(a,b) × GCD(a,b) = a × b and verify for several pairs.
- Apply LCM to scheduling problems: if two buses leave every 12 and 18 minutes, when do they next leave together?

**Success criteria:**
- Correctly finds LCM and GCD for a given pair using prime factorisations.
- Can explain why the method works (not just apply it).
- Applies LCM or GCD appropriately when presented with a contextual problem.

**Related standards:**
- AC9M7N06
