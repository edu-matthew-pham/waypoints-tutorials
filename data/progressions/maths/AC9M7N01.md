# AC9M7N01 — Perfect squares and square roots

**Year 7**
**Strand:** Number

## ACARA Descriptor

describe the relationship between perfect square numbers and square roots, and use squares of numbers and square roots of perfect square numbers to solve problems

## Prior Knowledge

Students can multiply and divide whole numbers fluently, use arrays to represent multiplication, and understand that area of a square = side × side (from Y6 measurement and number work).

## Progression Endpoint

Students describe the relationship between perfect square numbers and their square roots, and apply this relationship to solve problems including finding the perimeter of squares given area.

## Waypoint Sequence (4 waypoints, 2 hinges)

#### 1. Squares as area models ⚑

Students can connect squaring a number to the area of a square with that side length, and list perfect square numbers from 1 to 144.

**⚑ Hinge:** Students who lack automatic recall of perfect squares to 144 cannot access the inverse relationship in Node 2 — the square root concept is not legible without the forward mapping being secure.

**Response modes:**
- ◆ Practise here: Students need automatic recall of perfect squares to 144 before the square root relationship is legible. Fluency here is prerequisite to node 2.

**Minimum width:** List perfect squares 1–144 and represent at least three using area diagrams.

**Typical width:** List perfect squares 1–400, connect each to its area diagram, and identify patterns in the sequence (e.g. differences between consecutive squares).

**Wider:**
- Investigate the last-digit pattern in perfect squares (last digits cycle: 0,1,4,9,6,5,6,9,4,1,0) and use this to quickly rule out non-perfect-squares.
- Explore why perfect squares always have an odd number of factors, connecting to divisibility.

**Success criteria:**
- Lists perfect square numbers from 1² to 12² without error.
- Represents a given perfect square as an area model and states its side length.
- Does not confuse the square number with its root (e.g. does not say '9 squared is 3').

**Related standards:**
- AC9M7M01

#### 2. Square root as inverse operation ⚑ ★

Students understand that square root is the inverse of squaring and can find the exact square root of any perfect square up to 144.

**⚑ Hinge:** The squaring/doubling and √/halving misconceptions, if unresolved, produce systematic errors in Node 3 (bounding non-perfect squares) and Node 4 (applied problems) — students cannot reason about intervals or side lengths if the inverse relationship is confused.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts: Students commonly confuse squaring with doubling (thinking 5² = 10) or treat √ as halving. Multiple concrete representations — area diagrams, calculator verification, number line — help resolve this.

**Minimum width:** Find the square root of any perfect square from 1 to 144 and explain what it means.

**Typical width:** Use √ notation fluently, explain the inverse relationship, and verify: √(n²) = n for concrete examples.

**Wider:**
- Explore perfect squares beyond 144 (e.g. 169, 196, 225) using the area model and extend the pattern.
- Investigate √(a × b) = √a × √b for perfect square cases (e.g. √36 = √4 × √9), building intuition without formalising the rule.

**Success criteria:**
- States the square root of any perfect square from 1 to 144 when prompted.
- Explains in words what √25 means ('the number that when multiplied by itself gives 25').
- Does not conflate squaring with multiplying by 2.

#### 3. Locating non-perfect square roots

Students can identify which two consecutive integers a square root falls between for numbers that are not perfect squares.

**Response modes:**
- ◎ Scaffold reasoning: Students need an explicit reasoning scaffold: 'find the perfect squares either side, take their roots, the answer lies between'. Without this structure students often guess randomly or over-rely on a calculator without understanding the bounds.

**Minimum width:** For a given non-perfect-square (e.g. 43), identify the two consecutive perfect squares it lies between and state the corresponding root interval.

**Typical width:** Estimate √n to one decimal place for non-perfect squares by reasoning about the interval, e.g. √43 ≈ 6.5.

**Wider:**
- Use a number line marked with perfect squares to plot approximate positions of several non-perfect square roots.
- Investigate: between which perfect squares does √200 fall? Extend to three-digit numbers.

**Success criteria:**
- For any given number, correctly identifies the two consecutive perfect squares it lies between.
- States the corresponding square root interval (e.g. '√43 is between 6 and 7').
- Explains the reasoning, not just the answer.

#### 4. Applying square roots to problems ★

Students use the relationship between perfect square numbers and square roots to solve practical problems, including finding the side length of a square given its area.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts: The transition from abstract square root to applied problem-solving requires varied contexts — students who only practise decontextualised calculations often fail to recognise when square root is the required operation in a problem.
- ⊕ Integration point: This node connects squaring, square roots, area, and perimeter simultaneously — coordinating these is the learning.

**Minimum width:** Given the area of a square, find its side length and perimeter using square root reasoning.

**Typical width:** Solve a range of problems involving squares and square roots in practical contexts (tile layouts, garden beds, square frames) including problems requiring identification of perfect square areas.

**Wider:**
- Design a problem involving square tiles covering a floor area that requires checking whether a given area is a perfect square.
- Connect to Pythagoras preparation: identify whether a² + b² is a perfect square for simple triples (3,4,5).

**Success criteria:**
- Given the area of a square, correctly determines the side length using square root reasoning.
- Solves a two-step problem involving squares and square roots in a practical context.
- Checks that their answer is reasonable (e.g. side length must be positive).
