# AC9M7A04 — Graphs of functions

**Year 7**
**Strand:** Algebra
**Planning mode:** A — Concept-certifying

## ACARA Descriptor

describe relationships between variables represented in graphs of functions from authentic data

## ACARA Elaborations

- using graphs to analyse a building's electricity or gas usage over a period of time, the value of shares on a stock market, or the temperature during the day
- using travel graphs to compare the distance travelled to and from school, interpreting and discussing features of travel graphs such as the slope of lines
- telling the story behind what is being represented in graphs of functions; for example, graphs representing pouring rates or distance versus time
- exploring how functions are used in machine learning to model relationships; for example, in linear regression models
- using graphs of evaporation rates to explore and discuss First Nations Australians' methods of water resource management

## Prior Knowledge

Students can locate points in all four quadrants of the Cartesian plane and describe coordinate changes (from AC9M6SP02). Students understand dependent and independent variables in patterns.

## Progression Endpoint

Students describe relationships between variables in graphs from authentic data, identifying key features and interpreting what the graph communicates about the relationship.

## Width Tiers

**Width varies by:** phenomena and contexts

**Minimum width:** Read values from a graph of authentic data and describe the overall trend and relationship between the two variables in plain language.

**Typical width:** Describe relationships between variables in graphs from authentic data, including direction, rate of change, peaks, plateaus and notable features; interpret what unusual features indicate in context.

**Wider:**
- Analyse electricity or gas usage graphs over time — identify and explain periods of high/low usage and what real events might explain the shape.
- Use travel graphs to compare journeys: identify periods of travel, stopping and return; estimate average speed from the graph.
- Explore how functions are used in machine learning to model relationships — describe what a linear regression model graph communicates about the data.
- Use graphs of evaporation rates to explore First Nations Australians' methods of water resource management.

## Waypoint Sequence (2 waypoints, 1 width opportunity)

#### 1. Reading and interpreting graphs of real data

Students read values from graphs of authentic data and describe what the graph shows in context.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts — more examples resolve misconceptions here: Interpreting a graph requires understanding the specific context of the data. Students who only practise with constructed graphs often fail to interpret authentic messy data. Varied authentic contexts build flexible reading skills.

**Minimum width:** Read specific values from a graph and describe the overall trend in plain language.

**Typical width:** Identify increases, decreases, peaks, plateaus, and interpret what these mean in the context of the data (e.g. energy usage, stock value).

**Wider:**
- Investigate travel graphs: identify periods of travel, stopping, and return; compute average speed from slope.
- Compare two graphs representing the same phenomenon (e.g. gas vs electricity usage) and discuss what each reveals.

**Success criteria:**
- Reads specific values from a graph accurately.
- Describes trends (increasing, decreasing, constant, periodic) in contextual terms.
- Does not interpret a graph in isolation from its axes labels and units.

**Related standards:**
- AC9M7ST01 — Range, median, mean and mode
- AC9M7ST02 — Stem-and-leaf plots

#### 2. Identifying and describing the relationship between variables ★

Students identify and describe the relationship between the two variables in a graph, including direction, rate of change, and any notable features.

**★ Width opportunity:** Authentic data graphs offer rich comparative and narrative opportunities — advancing students can move from describing to explaining the story behind the shape, including non-linear patterns.

**Response modes:**
- ↔ Widen contexts — more examples resolve misconceptions here: Relating graph shape to contextual events (the 'story' behind a graph) requires experiencing many different authentic contexts. Students who only describe slope fail to interpret contextual meaning.

**Minimum width:** State the relationship between the two variables in a graph (e.g. 'as time increases, distance increases at a constant rate').

**Typical width:** Describe the relationship, rate of change, turning points, and intervals; explain what unusual features (sudden changes, plateaus) indicate in context.

**Wider:**
- Compare linear and non-linear relationships in graphs from authentic data — informally describe what makes one 'linear looking'.
- Tell the 'story' behind a graph of a function: describe what events might explain the shape (e.g. a graph representing water level in a bathtub).

**Success criteria:**
- Describes the relationship between variables including direction and approximate rate of change.
- Identifies and interprets at least one notable feature (e.g. peak, plateau, sudden change) in context.
- Uses the variable names from the axes in their description.

**Related standards:**
