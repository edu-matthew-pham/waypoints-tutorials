Putting it together: a water plan for one town

This lesson joins the course in one explanation for one place: where its water is, what that means for the people there, why it is hard to keep going, and how a strategy could help. The place is a made-up town in the real Murray–Darling Basin, with a made-up evidence pack. Students diagnose the problem before choosing a strategy, and use the words distribution, variability, scarcity and sustainability.

A town wants a long pipeline to bring in water from somewhere else. Is that the first thing it should decide?

What you should already know:
  • Making water last · Strategies try to make water last.

A good water plan has four parts, in order: where the water is (its distribution), what it means for people, why it is hard to keep going (sustainability), and how a strategy responds to the cause. To diagnose is to work out what is causing the problem. A plan that starts with the strategy skips the diagnosis. A catchment is the area of land that drains into a river.

The case. Tarnbridge is a made-up town in New South Wales, inside the real Murray–Darling Basin: the land drained by the Murray and Darling rivers and the rivers that flow into them (Bureau of Meteorology). Tarnbridge takes its water from a river in the Basin and stores it in a dam. Everything below about Tarnbridge is made up.

Evidence (made up)
Rainfall Some years are wet and some are very dry. The river runs low in dry years.
Storage The town's dam is old and loses water through leaks.
People The town is growing, and uses more water each year.
Sharing Farms and a wetland downstream use the same river.
Proposal Build a long pipeline to bring water from another catchment.

For teachers

Students join the course in one explanation for one place: a made-up town, Tarnbridge, in the real Murray–Darling Basin, with a made-up evidence pack. They diagnose the causes before choosing a strategy, check who else is affected, and use distribution, variability, scarcity and sustainability accurately. More than one strategy is defensible, so the final explanation is read by a partner or the teacher, not marked. About 45 minutes.

Open the teacher guide: answers, solutions, slides and a worksheet.

Students will be able to

  • I can explain where a place's water comes from and where it is in Australia.
  • I can say what a shortage means for the people there and why it is hard to sustain.
  • I can choose a strategy that targets a cause and use the course's words correctly.

Before class

This is the integration node (K02 n5). The node's first criterion asks for "a specific Australian water context". Tarnbridge is made up so that its evidence can be clear and nothing dates or leans, but it sits inside the real Murray–Darling Basin, and the only real facts used are the Basin's definition and that it covers parts of NSW (Bureau of Meteorology, National Water Account). Say this aloud: the place is invented; the Basin is real. A teacher who wants a fully real case can swap in their own catchment, with sources. What is real and what is made up, against the node's first criterion ("a specific Australian water context"): the distribution part is real (the Basin, its states and rivers on the lesson map, and the Vary sort and Make step ask students to name them); the implications and strategy are practised on made-up evidence, so they are not claims about any real Basin town.

The node's wider item, a Murray–Darling Basin case with the Basin Plan and water trading, is not used in student steps: it is contested and its details date. If your class goes there, present it as competing views with dated sources, not as settled.

Shape: primer, so the stages read Think first, The idea, Check and Apply. The lesson is mostly weighing and explaining. Only the diagnosis, the order of parts, the meanings of terms and recognisable errors are marked; the strategy choice is open.

Running the lesson

  1. Think first: vote, then ask what the evidence pack says is causing the shortage.
  2. The idea: list the causes from the evidence on the board before the bug task.
  3. Vary: check each word's meaning against an example from the case.
  4. Apply: accept different strategies if the four parts are there and a cause is targeted.

The hard part (on purpose)

Students reach for a strategy first, usually the pipeline in the case. Ask 'What is causing this?' and 'Who else uses this water?' before any strategy is named.

When students get stuck

  • The plan starts with the pipeline. Ask: What is causing the shortage? Would the pipeline fix the leaks? Fix: Diagnose from the evidence first, then choose.
  • 'Sustainable' is used to mean 'there is water now'. Ask: Would this still work in five dry years, with more people? Fix: Sustainable means meeting needs without running the source down.
  • The explanation leaves out other users. Ask: Who else uses the river? What happens to them? Fix: Name the farms and the wetland, and what they would get.

Stretch and support

Stretch: Test your plan against a run of very dry years and a doubling population. Same level, more width.

Support: Give the four-part frame on a card: Where → What it means for people → Why it is hard → Strategy and the cause it targets.

What each step is for

  • Think first: strategy or diagnosis first?: Commit to an order before seeing why the diagnosis comes first.
  • Try: the four parts: A full explanation has four parts in order.
  • The idea: diagnose from the evidence: Use all the evidence to name the causes before choosing a response.
  • Vary: the right words: Use the course's words accurately, with the meaning each one carries.
  • Check: a strong explanation: A strong explanation joins all four parts and uses the words accurately.
  • Apply: your plan for Tarnbridge: Writing the whole explanation in your own words is the integration the course builds to.
  • Typical: two strategies, explicit criteria: Comparing two strategies on stated criteria shows reasonable people can choose differently.
  • Wider: will it last?: A strategy is a bet on the future. Test it against change.
