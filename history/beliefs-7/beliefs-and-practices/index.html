What did they believe, and what did they do about it?

Describe key beliefs held by many people in Han China (duty to ancestors and parents, Heaven and the Mandate of Heaven, an afterlife much like this life) and explain how each belief led to practices such as offerings, careful funerals and furnished tombs. The beliefs fitted together like a system, so a practice that looks strange makes sense once you know the belief behind it.

Why would a family put dishes of food, a set of clothes and a little clay model of a granary into a grave?

A belief is something people hold to be true about the world: what happens after death, who gives a ruler the right to rule, what you owe your family. A practice is something people do. Historians look for how the two connect, because a belief usually explains a practice.

Many people in Han China held beliefs that fitted together:

What many people believed What they did because of it
The spirits of ancestors lived on, and could help their descendants Families made regular offerings of food and drink to their ancestors
Children owe their parents care and respect, while they live and after they die (Confucian teaching on filial piety, which means duty to parents) Careful funerals, and offerings that kept going for years
Heaven gave a ruler the right to rule only while he ruled well: the Mandate of Heaven Advisers read floods and droughts as Heaven's warnings, and told the emperor to change how he ruled
Life after death was much like life before it Tombs were filled with food, clothes, dishes and models of houses and granaries

When Confucius was asked about duty to parents, the Analects (a collection of his sayings) record him answering that parents should be served properly while they are alive, buried properly when they die, and given offerings properly after that. In 136 BCE Emperor Wu gave official posts to scholars of the Confucian classics, and in 124 BCE he founded an academy to train officials in them. Later Han writers described this as Confucian teaching winning out; many historians think the change was slower. Dong Zhongshu, a Confucian adviser to Emperor Wu, taught that floods and droughts were Heaven's way of warning the emperor.

See how the beliefs link up. If you believe duty to your parents does not stop when they die, then funerals and offerings matter. If the dead live on much as before, they need what the living need, so the tomb is stocked. Together the beliefs work like a system: each one helps explain the others, and together they explain what people did.

This is a simplified picture. Beliefs varied between places and families and changed over the 400 years of the Han, and people also followed other teachings, such as Daoism.

For teachers

Describe key beliefs held by many people in Han China (duty to ancestors and parents, the Mandate of Heaven, an afterlife much like this life) and explain how each led to practices such as offerings, funerals and furnished tombs. About 40 minutes.

Open the teacher guide: answers, solutions, slides and a worksheet.

Students will be able to

  • I can describe two beliefs held by many people in an ancient society.
  • I can explain how a belief led to a practice.
  • I can explain why a practice that looks odd makes sense once you know the belief behind it.

Before class

AC9HH7K11 n1, a hinge. The Kit's reason: students who treat beliefs as exotic details rather than coherent systems that produce practices cannot connect lesson 2's funerary practices to the beliefs and values behind them, and end up describing without understanding. So the check before moving on is the practise sort (each practice back to its belief) and the class plan question.

Beliefs are always described as what people held ("many people believed"), never as true or false, and no step asks students about their own beliefs or religion. Keep it that way in discussion.

Real content (checked 1 Nov 2026): ancestor offerings and the belief that ancestral spirits looked after descendants; Confucius on serving, burying and making offerings to parents (Analects 2.5, Legge's translation, paraphrased); posts for scholars of the Confucian classics from 136 BCE and an academy to train officials in them from 124 BCE (Indiana University teaching page on the canonisation of the classics, opened 1 Nov 2026, which dates the start to 136 BCE and names Dong Zhongshu as proposing the academy; Michael Loewe, Dong Zhongshu, a "Confucian" Heritage and the Chunqiu fanlu, Brill 2011, and Michael Nylan, The Five "Confucian" Classics, Yale 2001, for the view that the change was slower than later Han writers said, not checked online). An earlier draft said Confucian teaching "became the official basis of Han government" in 136 BCE; that overclaims. Dong Zhongshu's teaching that floods and droughts were Heaven's warnings to the emperor (Britannica); the Mandate of Heaven from the early Zhou, conditional on the ruler's conduct (Britannica); Han tomb models as provision for an afterlife pictured as an extension of this world (Met). Nib's notes are made up (labelled once).

Simplified: the table presents four beliefs as one neat system. Real belief varied by region, class and period across four centuries; Daoist ideas, local gods and ideas about two souls (hun and po) are left out. The lesson says so briefly.

Running the lesson

  1. Predict: take the hook literally. Who would need a granary in a grave?
  2. Try: read each belief aloud and ask 'so what would you do?' before students match.
  3. Worked example: let Nib's mistake sit. Ask what the family thought the model was for.
  4. Vary: the same move with a different belief. Accept 'Heaven's warning' in students' own words.
  5. Make: students write in their own book; a partner checks for a 'because' in each link.

The hard part (on purpose)

Students list beliefs and practices side by side without the 'because'. Keep asking: what did they believe that made this worth doing?

When students get stuck

  • A student says the practices were 'just weird' or 'just tradition'. Ask: If you believed the dead needed food, what would you put in the grave? Fix: The practice follows from the belief; describe the belief first.
  • A student says 'everyone in China believed...'. Ask: Would every family, in every place, over 400 years, believe exactly the same? Fix: Say 'many people believed'; beliefs varied.
  • A student argues about whether a belief was true. Ask: What did people at the time believe, and what did it lead them to do? Fix: Historians describe what people held and what followed from it; they do not rule on whether it was true.

Stretch and support

Stretch: Which belief in the table do you think shaped the most practices? Give two practices it explains.

Support: Give a two-column frame headed 'They believed...' and 'So they...' and fill one row together before pairs do the rest.

What each step is for

  • why stock a tomb?: Committing first shows whether students reach for a belief behind a practice, or treat it as an odd custom.
  • Try: which belief, which practice?: Matching each belief to the practice it led to makes the link between believing and doing the first habit.
  • Worked example: beliefs explain practices: Separating beliefs from practices, then fixing Nib's 'they just liked decorating', shows a practice as the result of a belief.
  • Vary: a flood and the Mandate of Heaven: A different belief, about rulers, shows the same move: find the belief that makes a practice make sense.
  • beliefs as a system: Practising the link from each practice back to its belief.
  • two beliefs and what they led to, in your book: Explaining two beliefs and the practices they led to, for the society their class studies, makes the belief-to-practice link their own.
  • Typical: household gods in Rome: The same move, belief then practice, works for a society with different beliefs.
  • Wider: asking the gods in Greece: Religious authority could shape big decisions, such as going to war.
