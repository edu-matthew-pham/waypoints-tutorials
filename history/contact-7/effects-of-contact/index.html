What did contact and conflict lead to?

Describe the effects of contacts and conflicts between the Han and their neighbours in three kinds: conquest (Han commanderies along the Hexi Corridor), the expansion of trade (silk going west; horses, grapes and other goods coming east) and peace agreements (the heqin agreements with the Xiongnu from about 198 BCE), and explain what each one changed, for every society involved, not only the winner.

For about sixty years, a Han emperor sent silk, food and a bride to the ruler of his enemies every so often. What did each side get out of that?

Contacts and conflicts had effects: things that changed because of them. Historians look for three big kinds:

Kind of effect What it means
Conquest One society takes over land and the people living on it
Trade Goods, animals and ideas start to move between societies
Peace agreement Rulers agree terms to stop fighting, for a while at least

And they ask the same question every time: who changed, and how? The answer is usually both sides.

A peace agreement: the heqin (from about 198 BCE). In 200 BCE the first Han emperor led an army north and was trapped by the Xiongnu at a place called Baideng. He got away, but he could not beat them. About two years later the Han agreed the first heqin ("peace through kinship") agreement: a woman of the royal family married the Xiongnu ruler, and the Han sent gifts of silk, food and other goods. The agreement treated the two rulers as equals. Han writers say raids did not stop completely, but the Han kept to this kind of agreement until Emperor Wu changed course in the 130s BCE.

Conquest: the Hexi Corridor (from 121 BCE). West of the Han heartland is the Hexi Corridor, a long, narrow strip of land between mountains and desert. In 121 BCE a Han army drove the Xiongnu out of it. Over the following years (from about 111 BCE) the Han set up four commanderies there, including Dunhuang at the far western end, built farming garrison towns for soldiers and settlers, and by about 100 BCE had extended their walls as far as Dunhuang. The Xiongnu lost grazing land and a route they had controlled.

Trade: goods both ways. With the corridor held, Han envoys and merchants could travel west more safely. Chinese silk went west, passed from trader to trader across Central Asia. Horses, grapes, alfalfa (a crop for feeding horses), wool and other goods came east. Today these routes are often called the Silk Roads, but that is a modern name, made up by European writers in the 1800s. People at the time did not use it.

Before class

AC9HH7K12 n2. Builds on lesson 1's causes: the heqin came from Han weakness after Baideng (security), the Hexi conquest from security and the wish for horses and routes west (resources), and trade from all of these.

Real cases (checked 1 Nov 2026): the Han army trapped at Baideng in 200 BCE; the first heqin agreement about 198 BCE (a woman of the royal clan married to the Xiongnu ruler, gifts, equal status between the two rulers); peace-buying with gold, silk and princesses until about 135 BCE (Britannica); Emperor Wu attacking from 133 BCE; the Hexi Corridor taken from 121 BCE, its four commanderies (Wuwei, Zhangye, Jiuquan, Dunhuang) set up over the following years, from about 111 BCE (International Dunhuang Project; Silk Road Seattle dates the Dunhuang commandery decree to 111 BCE), farming garrison towns, and walls reaching Dunhuang by about 100 BCE; silk west and horses, grapes and alfalfa east; Wu's wars draining the treasury, leading to new taxes and monopolies on salt, iron and wine. Heqin sources: Nicola Di Cosmo, Ancient China and Its Enemies (Cambridge 2002), p. 193, and Yü Ying-shih, "Han foreign relations", in The Cambridge History of China vol. 1 (1986) (not checked online). Xiongnu raids are "Han writers say" once in the lesson. The heqin gifts are described in general terms ("silk, food and other goods") because sources differ on exact amounts. The Han sometimes sent a relative, not the emperor's daughter; the lesson says "a woman of the royal family".

The name "Silk Road" (German Seidenstraße) is modern: once credited to Ferdinand von Richthofen in 1877, it was used earlier, by Carl Ritter in 1838 (Mertens, The Silk Road Journal). The lesson says only "made up by European writers in the 1800s".

Simplified: the effects on the Xiongnu come from Han writers; the Xiongnu left no writing of their own. The match task's "traders in between" stands for the many peoples of Central Asia who passed goods on. Effects on the women sent as brides are not covered; a teacher may raise them with care.

Usually Year 7; not yet tried with a class. No board or clips. Conquest is described factually, without violent detail.

Relevant ACARA AC9HH7K12 elaborations (examples, not a checklist): "explaining the nature of contact and conflict with other societies, such as trade with Cyprus, Crete and Greece, and the Battle of Kadesh in the New Kingdom that concluded with Ramses II's peace treaty with the Hittites"; "analysing the causes and effects of the rise and expansion of the Egyptian Empire"; "describing the furthest extent of the Roman Empire and the influence of foreign cults on Roman religious beliefs and practices; for example, the Pantheon of Gods (Greece), Isis (Egypt), Mithras (Persia) and Judaism and early Christianity (Palestine)".

First Nations: AC9HH7K12 has no First Nations elaborations; we write none.

Running the lesson

  1. Predict: ask the 'only the Han' voters what the Xiongnu did with that land before 121 BCE.
  2. Try: put the three kinds on the board; ask which one surprised them most.
  3. Worked example: let Nib's mistake sit. Ask: who paid for Emperor Wu's wars?
  4. Vary: ask why a strong ruler would agree to treat an enemy as an equal.
  5. Make: students write in their own book; a partner gives feedback.

The hard part (on purpose)

Students describe the winner's gains and stop. Keep asking: and what changed for the other side, and for the people in between?

When students get stuck

  • A student describes only the winner's gains. Ask: What did the other side lose, or gain? Fix: Both sides changed: the Xiongnu lost grazing land; the Han paid in taxes.
  • A student says 'things changed' without saying what. Ask: What could people do, or not do, afterwards that they could before? Fix: Name the specific change: new crops, new towns, lost land, new taxes.
  • A student thinks a peace agreement changes nothing. Ask: What did each side get under the heqin? Fix: Gifts and equal status for the Xiongnu; fewer raids, for a time, for the Han.

Stretch and support

Stretch: The heqin treated two rulers as equals. Why might a later Han emperor like Wu have found that hard to accept? What does that suggest about causes of the next conflict?

Support: Give a three-row frame (conquest, trade, peace agreement) with two columns (the Han; the other side) and fill the heqin row together.

What each step is for

  • who changed?: Committing first shows whether students think only the winner changes.
  • Try: what kind of effect?: Sorting effects into conquest, trade and peace agreement shows the range of what contact led to.
  • Worked example: both sides changed: Matching each effect to what it changed for each side, then catching Nib's 'only the winner changed', shows effects are many-sided.
  • Vary: a peace agreement's effects: Looking at a peace agreement from both sides shows that a treaty also changes relationships, not only borders.
  • effects and change: Practising effects on each side, so 'who changed?' always has more than one answer.
  • describe the effects, in your book: Describing two kinds of effect for both sides of one contact or conflict makes the waypoint their own.
  • Typical: Egypt and the Hittites make peace: ACARA's Egyptian example: a peace treaty after a battle that neither side clearly won.
  • Wider: Rome and Carthage: A long war that ended in conquest shows effects on the winner as well as the conquered.
