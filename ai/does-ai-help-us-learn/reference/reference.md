# Does AI Help Us Learn? — Words and evidence

Use this page to check what a word means or what a study found. It does **not** give one right answer to the course question.

## Doing and learning

| Word | What it means | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| **Doing well with help** | What you can do with hints, examples, people or AI | What does this result show? |
| **Learning** | A change in what you know, understand, can do or can judge | What has changed for the learner? |
| **Transfer** | Using an idea on something new | Can they do one that's a bit different? |
| **Retention** | Still knowing it later | What would a try next week show? |
| **Meaningful learning** | Depends on the goal: doing it yourself, making things, working with others | What is worth learning here, and why? |

## Thinking and help

| Word | What it means | Watch out |
|---|---|---|
| **Cognitive offloading** | Using a tool to do part of your thinking | Saving effort can free you up, or skip the skill |
| **The skill or the edge** | Is this job the skill you're learning, or on the edge of it? (Nakamura's words: *constitutive* and *peripheral*) | A sliding scale, not two boxes; it changes with the goal and the learner |
| **Metacognition** | Thinking about your own thinking | Feeling sure isn't the same as being able to do it |
| **Confidence that matches** | Feeling as sure as your results justify (researchers say *calibration*) | One try is a small sample |
| **Self-regulation** | Choosing your own goals and next steps, and checking how it's going | It's hard to judge what you don't know yet |
| **Scaffolding** | Support for a skill that is meant to come down | Hints aren't always better than worked examples |
| **Fading support** | Less help each time | A good plan doesn't prove you'll remember |

## What makes an activity useful?

- **What you do:** clicking, answering, talking.
- **What you think about:** remembering, explaining, predicting, fixing.
- **How the help responds:** the same for everyone, a prepared message for a common mistake, or a reply to your own question.
- **Evidence you learned:** a good explanation, a new question you can do, a check a week later.

More clicking, more replies or more struggle does **not automatically** mean more learning. A worked example, a quiz, a computer tutorial, a teacher or an AI tutor can each suit different goals.

## How to read a study about AI and learning

1. **The claim:** what exactly is being said?
2. **Compared with what?** No help? Books? A good non-AI computer tutor? A teacher?
3. **Measured how?** During practice, straight after, weeks later, without help?
4. **Who and where?** Which learners, tasks, tools and how long?
5. **Does the claim fit?** What does the study support, and what is still unknown?

### Studies in Unit 2

| Study | What the course uses it for | Important limit |
|---|---|---|
| [Bastani et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122) | High school maths: AI helped during practice; later, without AI, the plain chatbot group did worse, and the AI tutor group was close to the books-and-notes group | Didn't compare AI with a really good non-AI computer tutor |
| [Kestin et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6) | University physics: an AI tutor beat a teacher-led active class on tests straight after each topic | Every student tried both, in random order; many things changed at once; long-term learning wasn't tested |
| [Fernandes et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108779) | Adults solving logic puzzles with AI did better, but thought they had done even better than they had | Not a study of school learning over time |
| [Nakamura (2026), *Augmentation or Erosion?*](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-026-10224-6) | An idea about why results might depend on the goal, experience and what is handed over | **A theory with predictions to test, not an experiment** |

Research keeps changing. These are the studies used in the course **as of October 2026**, not a final verdict.

## Judging achievement

- **Formative:** feedback that helps you improve.
- **Summative:** a judgement of what you've achieved at a point in time, like a report card.
- **Evidence that fits:** a task is good evidence when it fits the claim being made (researchers say *validity*).
- **Agency:** having a say over your own learning.
- **Incentives:** the way marks and rewards change what people practise.

One task can be both formative and summative. AI help can be useful for learning and still change what your work shows about what you can do alone.

## Questions to ask about any use of AI

**Learning what? For whom? Compared with what? Which thinking did you keep? Under what conditions? What would change your mind?**

These are questions to think with, **not a checklist or a rule that AI always helps or always harms**.
