# AI and your tasks: the example tasks and the words

*Last checked: 19 October 2026.* What AI can do changes quickly. Re-check the "What AI can do now" lines before you teach with them.

## The five example tasks

The lessons come back to these five tasks. Each has a goal, the thinking it's for, and what AI can now do with it.

### English: a persuasive essay (Year 10)

*Should our school start later? Write a persuasive essay of about 800 words.*

- **The thinking it's for:** building an argument from reasons and evidence, answering the strongest objection, in the student's own voice.
- **What AI can do now:** write a fluent, well-structured essay on the topic in seconds, in any voice it's asked for, and rewrite a draft to sound more like the student.

### Science: a practical report (Year 9)

*Which antacid neutralises the most acid? Plan a fair test, run it, and write a report.*

- **The thinking it's for:** planning a fair test, and explaining what the results show and why.
- **What AI can do now:** write a plan, a method and a conclusion; make up plausible results; draw a graph from numbers it's given.

### Maths: a problem set (Year 8)

*Ten problems on percentages. Show your working.*

- **The thinking it's for:** choosing a method and carrying it out, so the student can do the next one alone.
- **What AI can do now:** solve the problems and show working. Many tools write and run code to check their own arithmetic, so "AI makes maths slips" is no longer something to design on.

### History: a source task (Year 9)

*A political cartoon from 1916. Who made it, when and why? What does it show? How useful is it for studying the conscription debate?*

- **The thinking it's for:** asking where a source came from separately from what it shows, then judging its usefulness for a question.
- **What AI can do now:** read the image, describe it, and write an analysis of origin, purpose and usefulness. It may also give sources and links, some of which may not exist.

### Design: a design folio (Year 8)

*Design a phone stand for a younger student. Folio: research, design ideas, a chosen design, a prototype, an evaluation.*

- **The thinking it's for:** making design decisions from the needs of a real user, and evaluating the result honestly against them.
- **What AI can do now:** write the research summary, suggest and draw design ideas, and write an evaluation. It can't make or test the student's prototype, but it can write as if it had.

## Where AI fits: three groups

The same words students use in *AI at our school*:

| Group | What it means | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| **Keep the thinking** | This part *is* the learning | If AI did it, what would the student not learn? |
| **AI can help** | Not the goal of this task | Does it free time for the thinking? |
| **Depends on the goal** | Help or crutch, depending on the student and the goal | Is this skill being learned now, or already learned? |

A part can move between groups from Year 7 to Year 10: support that helps a beginner can get in the way once the skill is learned.

## AI levels (a design lens)

Generic levels, used in the examples. **If your school has its own scale, use that instead.**

| Level | The task is designed so that… |
|---|---|
| **No AI** | the work is done where you can see it (in class, on paper, in conversation) |
| **AI for ideas** | AI may help plan or brainstorm; the work itself is the student's |
| **AI for feedback** | the student writes it; AI may comment on a draft, and the student decides what to change |
| **AI throughout, acknowledged** | AI may help with any part; the student says how, and is judged on their decisions |

A level describes **a kind of task**, not a permission. Putting a level on an unchanged task does little: students can use AI at home whatever the label says. Only **No AI** can be made to hold, by doing the work where you can see it. So redesign the task first, then choose the level that fits it.

## A task instruction card

What students read. Plain words; the level name is optional.

1. **What this task is for:** the thinking, in one sentence.
2. **AI may:** what it can help with.
3. **AI may not:** the parts that are yours.
4. **Say what you used:** what to acknowledge, and how (a sentence, or a note with what you asked it).
5. **Saying so is never in itself a reason for a lower mark.** Skipping the thinking the task is for can still affect your mark.

## The four layers

Where AI rules live in a school (from *AI at our school*):

1. **The AI statement:** a thin page of principles, a map of where each rule lives, who holds the map, when it's reviewed.
2. **AI lines in existing policies:** integrity and assessment, wellbeing and behaviour, privacy, inclusion, devices and ICT use, staff conduct.
3. **Guidelines** (updated each term): approved tools, settings, year-level expectations.
4. **Task instructions** (set by each teacher): how much AI this task allows, and what to say about it. **This course lives here.**

Assessed work follows **outside rules first**: in IB courses the IB's academic integrity policy and its AI guidance; for ACT senior students the BSSS. Your school's rules sit inside those.

## Rule problem or know-how problem?

If the person who knows a rule best still can't apply it to a task, **the rule needs fixing**: tell whoever holds the school's map. If they can and others can't, it's **know-how**: guidance, worked examples and practice, which is what this course is. A new rule doesn't fix a know-how gap.
