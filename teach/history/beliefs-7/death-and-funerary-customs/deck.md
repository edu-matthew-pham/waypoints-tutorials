## Why it matters

> One Han tomb held so much that, fifty years after it was opened, the museum was still counting. What would you want to know before using it as evidence?

<!-- Speaker note: An ancient society's **death and funerary customs** are what people did when someone died: how they buried them, what they put with them, and what they kept doing afterwards. For Qin and Han China, this is the area with the most evidence, because tombs were built to last and many have been dug up by archaeologists. > **Mawangdui** is a group of three Han tombs near the city of Changsha, opened in 1972 to 1974. They belonged to a noble family of the 2nd century BCE: Li Cang, the Marquis of Dai (died 186 BCE), his wife Xin Zhui, often called Lady Dai, and a man thought to be their son (buried in 168 BCE). The tombs held lacquer dishes and trays, silk clothes, food, wooden figures of servants and musicians, books written on silk and bamboo, and bamboo slips listing the names and numbers of the objects buried. A painted silk banner on Lady Dai's coffin shows her funeral and her journey after death. In 2024 the Hunan Museum finished counting: 26,937 items. > **Tomb models.** Many Han tombs, especially in the later Han, held pottery models of houses, granaries, wells, mills, watchtowers, pig pens and farm animals. Museums explain them as things to provide for the dead person in the afterlife. > **The terracotta army.** The First Emperor of Qin (died 210 BCE) was buried in a huge tomb complex. In pits nearby stood thousands of life-size clay soldiers and horses, with wooden war chariots and bronze weapons, facing outward from the tomb. No written source from the time says why they were made; historians think they were probably meant to guard the emperor after death. Each piece of evidence tells you something about beliefs and values. A tomb stocked like a home suggests a belief that the dead live on and need what the living need. Bamboo lists of the buried objects show the family kept a careful record of what they gave the dead, which fits the duty to family from the last lesson. A clay army suggests the emperor expected to rule and need protecting after death. But think about **whose** tombs these are. A marquis's family and an emperor had wealth and workers to build tombs like this. Most people in Han China were farmers, and their graves were much simpler, so far fewer of their burials have been studied. -->

## Why 'probably'?

**Historians think the terracotta army was probably meant to guard the First Emperor. Why do they say 'probably'?**

- The soldiers were made long after the emperor had died
- Historians are not sure the clay soldiers really exist at all
- Saying 'probably' is just a polite way of saying it is true
- No written source from the time says why the soldiers were made

* Answer: No written source from the time says why the soldiers were made
