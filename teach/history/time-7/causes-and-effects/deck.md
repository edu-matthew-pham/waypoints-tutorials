## Today

**We are learning to** explain an event with several causes, sorted into long-term and short-term, and describe its short-term and long-term effects.

- I can give at least two causes of a historical event.
- I can say which causes are long-term, which are short-term and which is the trigger.
- I can describe a short-term and a long-term effect.

## Why it matters

> In 230 BCE there were seven rival states in China. Nine years later there was one. Why Qin?

<!-- Speaker note: For hundreds of years, China was split into rival states that fought each other. In 221 BCE one of them, **Qin**, had conquered the rest. Its king took a new title: the **First Emperor**. Historians rarely explain an event like this with one reason. They look for **several causes**, working over different lengths of time: - **Long-term causes** (also called *underlying* causes) build up over many years. They make the event possible. - **Short-term causes** happen just before the event. - A **trigger** is the spark that sets the event off at that moment. It explains *when*, but not on its own *why*. They also look at **effects**: what the event changed straight away (short-term), and what it changed for a long time afterwards (long-term). -->

## Cause or effect?

**Which of these is an effect of Qin unifying China, not a cause?**

- Reforms rewarding farmers and soldiers in the 300s BCE
- One written script used across the whole empire
- King Zheng's campaigns against the six states
- Grain from the irrigated Chengdu Plain

* Answer: One written script used across the whole empire

<!-- Speaker note: A cause comes before the event and helps explain it; an effect comes after and is changed by it. -->

## Vary: what was the trigger?

In 209 BCE, the year after the First Emperor died, a revolt began. **According to Sima Qian**, a Han court historian writing about a century later, a group of men called up for service were held up by heavy rain. They knew they would arrive late and feared being punished, so they rebelled instead. Revolts spread across the old states, and by 206 BCE the Qin dynasty had fallen.

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: The rain story is a clear trigger; separating it from the underlying causes practises the second distinction in the checkpoint. For students who need more support: Give a two-column card (Before 221 BCE / After 221 BCE) and have students place each statement before naming it a cause or an effect. For early finishers: Rank the three causes in the table from most to least important and give a reason for the top one. What evidence would change your mind? The hard part, on purpose: Students settle on one cause, usually a person (the king) or the last thing that happened. Keep asking 'and what made that possible?' When students get stuck: A student gives only King Zheng (or only the last battle) as the cause. Ask: What did he need before he could win? Where did his soldiers and their food come from? Fix: Add the long-term causes that made the campaigns possible. / A student calls the trigger the main cause. Fix: The trigger explains when; the underlying causes explain why it grew. / A student lists effects as causes. Ask: Did this happen before 221 BCE or after? Fix: Causes come before the event, effects after. -->

## Short-term or long-term effects?

**Sort each effect of Qin unifying China in 221 BCE.**

Groups: Short-term effect · Long-term effect

The six rival kings lost their thrones · Officials were sent to run the commanderies · Later dynasties kept one written script · Later dynasties kept the Qin way of running an empire

* Answer: The six rival kings lost their thrones → Short-term effect; Officials were sent to run the commanderies → Short-term effect; Later dynasties kept one written script → Long-term effect; Later dynasties kept the Qin way of running an empire → Long-term effect

## Make: Typical: Rome gets an emperor
