## Today

**We are learning to** describe how changes in rainfall, deserts and sea levels are thought to have moved early humans out of Africa over many generations.

- I can describe one environmental change and how it could move people.
- I can explain why migration happened over many generations, not as a planned trip.
- I can tell a push from a pull.

## Why it matters

> Picture people leaving Africa. Did you imagine a group packing up and walking off towards a new continent? Scientists think it happened very differently.

<!-- Speaker note: Our species spread out of Africa over tens of thousands of years, starting well over 100,000 years ago. No one at the time knew there were other continents to reach. So why did people end up all over the world? Most scientists think **changes in the environment** played a big part: - **Rainfall shifted.** For long stretches, the Sahara and Arabia were much wetter than today, with grassland, lakes and rivers. Then they dried out again into desert (**desertification**). - **Food and water moved with the rain.** Groups of hunter-gatherers followed the animals and plants they lived on. When a wet corridor opened, people could spread along it. When it dried, they moved on or were cut off. - **Sea levels rose and fell.** In the coldest times of the ice ages, so much water was locked up in ice sheets that the sea was far lower than today (about 125 metres lower at the coldest point, about 20,000 years ago). Coasts stretched further out, and some sea crossings became shorter. It happened **over many generations**. Each group might only shift its range a little in a lifetime, but over thousands of years those small shifts added up to a journey across continents. Scientists still argue about how much was pushed by climate and how much by other things, such as growing numbers of people or rich food along coasts. Here is a made-up model of one valley over 500 years, to show how it could work (the valley and the numbers are made up): | Years from now | Rain | The lake | Animals to hunt | Where the group spends most of the year | |---|---|---|---|---| | 0 | Plenty | Full | Many | Around the lake | | 200 | Less | Shrinking | Moving north | Further north, following the herds | | 500 | Very little | Dry | Gone from the valley | In the next valley to the north | -->

## Push or pull?

**Which of these is a pull (something drawing people to a place) rather than a push (something driving them away)?**

- A desert spreading slowly towards their camp
- New grassland and a lake in a corridor that just turned green
- The home valley's lake drying up over many years
- The animals they hunt leaving the area for good

* Answer: New grassland and a lake in a corridor that just turned green

<!-- Speaker note: Pushes drive people away from where they are; pulls draw them to somewhere new. Environmental change can do both. -->

## Vary: a green Arabia

In 2018, scientists reported a fossil finger bone of our species found at Al Wusta, in what is now desert in Saudi Arabia. It is about 85,000 years old or more. The same layers held signs of a lake and grassland, from a time when the region was much wetter than it is today (Groucutt and others, *Nature Ecology & Evolution*).

## Push, pull or barrier?

**Sort each environmental change by what it did to people's movement.**

Groups: Push away · Pull towards · Barrier

Desert spreading into a group's home range · A new lake and grassland to the north · A wide, dry desert with no water stops · Herds leaving the area as the grass dies · A coastline rich in shellfish, newly exposed as the sea falls · Deep sea with no land in sight

* Answer: Desert spreading into a group's home range → Push away; A new lake and grassland to the north → Pull towards; A wide, dry desert with no water stops → Barrier; Herds leaving the area as the grass dies → Push away; A coastline rich in shellfish, newly exposed as the sea falls → Pull towards; Deep sea with no land in sight → Barrier
