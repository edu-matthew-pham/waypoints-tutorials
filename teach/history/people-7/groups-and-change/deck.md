## Why it matters

> The First Emperor's tomb, the walls and the roads were not built by the emperor. Who built them, and did that give those people any power?

<!-- Speaker note: Near the bottom of Qin and Han society were many people whose work everything else rested on: - **Farmers**, most of the population, who grew the food, paid grain tax and owed labour and army service. - **Conscripted labourers**: ordinary men called up to build walls, roads, canals and the emperor's tomb. - **Convicts**, who were punished with years of hard labour. - **Slaves**, owned by the government or by private families, who worked in households, workshops and fields. In the Han they were a small part of the population. - **Merchants**, who could become rich, but whom many officials looked down on, because Confucian thinkers ranked trade below farming and crafts. That ranking was an ideal order, not a list of who had the most money. The order between these groups was not fixed. Some changes: > **209 BCE.** According to Sima Qian, writing about a century later, a group of about 900 conscripts led by Chen Sheng and Wu Guang were held up by heavy rain on their way to the northern frontier. Late, and fearing punishment, they rebelled. Their revolt spread, and the Qin dynasty fell within a few years. > **Under Emperor Wu (141 to 87 BCE).** The Han government taxed merchants heavily, and in 119 BCE took over making and selling salt and iron, which had made private traders rich. > **9 CE.** Wang Mang, who had seized the throne, banned buying and selling land and slaves. Powerful families opposed it, and he cancelled the ban in 12 CE. -->

## Lasting change?

**In 9 CE Wang Mang banned buying and selling slaves. Powerful families opposed it, and he cancelled the ban in 12 CE. What does that show about change over time?**

- A change in the rules could be undone
- Nothing ever changed in Han China
- Slavery in China ended for good in 9 CE
- Once a law is made, it lasts for good

* Answer: A change in the rules could be undone
