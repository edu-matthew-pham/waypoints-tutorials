## Why it matters

> The emperor lived in the capital. A village paying grain tax could be weeks of travel away. So who made sure the grain arrived?

<!-- Speaker note: Most ancient societies had a **hierarchy**: layers of people, with a few at the top holding most of the power and most people near the bottom. In Qin and Han China it looked roughly like this: | Layer | Who | What they did | |---|---|---| | The ruler | The emperor | Made laws, appointed officials, led the army | | Nobles | Relatives and allies of the ruling family, given titles and, in the early Han, whole kingdoms to rule | Ruled their lands, often passing them to their sons | | Officials (the bureaucracy) | Governors, county magistrates, clerks, tax collectors | Ran the country day to day for the emperor | | Most people | Farmers | Grew the food, paid grain tax, and owed labour each year and a period of army service | | Others | Craftworkers, merchants, soldiers | Made, traded and fought | | At the bottom | Convicts and slaves | Did forced labour | The **officials** are the key to how power worked. After the Qin united China in 221 BCE, the First Emperor divided the land into 36 **commanderies**, each split into **counties**. Instead of leaving each area to a noble family, he sent officials to run them. Officials were appointed by the emperor, paid by him, and could be moved or dismissed. The early Han kept the officials, though for a time it also gave kingdoms to the emperor's relatives. Here is how officials carried the emperor's power out to the villages: - **Counting**: registers of every household and its land, so the state knew who could pay tax and who could be called up for labour or the army. - **Tax**: grain collected from farmers (the surplus from *Shaped by the land*), which fed officials and soldiers. - **Law**: written rules, like the ones on the Qin bamboo slips, told officials what to do and set punishments. - **Labour and armies**: men were called up to build walls, roads and canals, or to serve as soldiers. > **A made-up county official's day** (made up for this lesson): check the grain store against the register; hear a dispute about a field boundary; send ten men to repair the road; write a report to the commandery. -->

* Answer: line 3

## Nobles or officials?

**What was the main difference between a noble and an official in Qin China?**

- A noble's power came from family; an official's from the emperor
- Nobles did all the paperwork and officials fought in the army
- Officials were richer, so they had more power than nobles
- There was no difference: both inherited their jobs

* Answer: A noble's power came from family; an official's from the emperor

<!-- Speaker note: An official was appointed by the emperor and could be dismissed, so he depended on the emperor. That is one reason the Qin used officials instead of noble families. -->
