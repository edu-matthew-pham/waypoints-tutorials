## Today

**We are learning to** explain how the land of ancient China created both opportunities and constraints, and how people responded.

- I can say how one feature of the land can be both an opportunity and a constraint.
- I can tell a natural feature from a change people made to the land.
- I can write a balanced explanation that includes what people did, not only what the land did.

## Why it matters

> The Yellow River fed millions of people. It also drowned whole villages when it burst its banks. So was it a gift or a curse?

<!-- Speaker note: The last two lessons showed what the land **gave** people. But the same feature can also **hold them back**. - An **opportunity** is something the environment makes possible: farming, travel, trade, protection. - A **constraint** is something it makes hard: floods, distance, mountains to cross, land too dry to farm. People did not just accept what the land gave them. They changed it: dykes, canals, walls and roads. So to explain a society, historians look at the land **and** at what people did about it. > **The Yellow River.** Its floods spread fresh, fertile soil over the North China Plain. But a big flood could destroy crops and villages, and over the centuries the river has changed its course many times. People built dykes along its banks again and again. In English it is sometimes called "China's Sorrow" because of its floods. > **The mountains and deserts of the west.** They protected the heartland from attack from the west. They also made overland travel to the west slow and hard, so traffic was squeezed into narrow routes such as the **Hexi Corridor**, between the mountains and the desert. > **The Lingqu Canal.** Built on the First Emperor's orders and finished in 214 BCE, it joined two rivers (the Xiang and the Li) across a range of hills in the south. Boats could now carry grain and soldiers between the Yangtze and rivers further south. People made it; it is not a natural feature. -->

## Natural or human-made?

**Is the Lingqu Canal a natural feature of the land?**

- Yes: it was there before the Qin, and they just made use of it
- No: it is a road over the hills, not a waterway
- No: people dug it to join two rivers through a barrier of hills
- Yes: it carries water like a river, so it is part of the landscape

* Answer: No: people dug it to join two rivers through a barrier of hills

<!-- Speaker note: The canal is a human response to a constraint: hills that blocked river travel between north and south. -->

## Cut off?

**Did the mountains and deserts of the west cut ancient China off from other societies?**

- No: they were easy to cross, so they made no real difference
- Yes: deserts and mountains always stop trade, wherever they are
- No: they made contact harder, but people still crossed by narrow routes
- Yes: no one ever crossed them, so China had no contact with the west

* Answer: No: they made contact harder, but people still crossed by narrow routes

<!-- Speaker note: Barriers shape where and how contact happens, through narrow routes such as the Hexi Corridor. They rarely stop it altogether. -->

## Vary: a route to the west

In the Han period, in the 2nd century BCE, an official named **Zhang Qian** was sent west on a mission to the lands of Central Asia. Routes through the Hexi Corridor became part of the long trade routes later called the **Silk Roads**, which UNESCO lists as World Heritage.

## Both sides of one feature

**Match each feature of ancient China's land to what it made possible and what it made hard.**

| | |
|---|---|
| `Yellow River` | A. Protection from attack, but slow trade with the west |
| `Mountains in the west` | B. Grazing land for herders, but too dry for farming villages |

| `Northern steppe` | D. Fertile soil for farms, but deadly floods |
