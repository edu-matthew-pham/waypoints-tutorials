## Today

**We are learning to** identify groups whose voices are thin in the historical record, explain why, and say what limited evidence can still show about them.

- I can name groups whose voices are missing from the record, and say why.
- I can say what records made about a group can and cannot show.
- I can explain how a gap in the evidence can itself tell us something.

## Why it matters

> The Xiongnu fought the Han for over a century. No writing of theirs has been found. How can we know anything about them?

<!-- Speaker note: Think about who could write in Qin and Han China. Mostly officials, scholars and the families of the rich. Most people could not, and even when ordinary people were written about, someone else usually held the brush. So some groups are **thin in the record**: - **farmers**, who were most of the population - **labourers, convicts and slaves** - **conquered peoples**, such as the peoples of the far south, whom the Qin conquered around 214 BCE - **neighbours with no writing of their own**, such as the Xiongnu - **women**, as [What did women do, and how do we know?](../../people-7/womens-roles/) shows Their perspectives are hard to reach. But "hard to reach" is not "nothing". Historians use three kinds of evidence: 1. **Records others made about them.** Officials counted people, set them to work and punished them, and wrote it down. 2. **Objects and burials** that the people themselves made or used. 3. **Other people's accounts, read carefully.** A hostile writer can still report how people lived (you met this with Sima Qian on the Xiongnu). -->

## Records made about them

**The Liye records were written by Qin officials. What can they best show about labourers?**

- Nothing, because the labourers did not write the records
- How officials counted and used them, though not what the labourers thought
- That labourers were happy, because they did not complain in writing
- What the labourers thought about the Qin and their work

* Answer: How officials counted and used them, though not what the labourers thought

<!-- Speaker note: An official record shows how power reached people: who was counted, sorted and set to work. -->

## What each kind can show

**Match each kind of evidence to what it can best show about a group with little writing of its own.**

| | |
|---|---|
| `Officials' records about them` | A. What they made, used or were buried with |
| `Graves and objects` | B. Who could write, and who held power |
| `A rival's description, read carefully` | C. How they lived, seen from outside |
| `The lack of their own writing` | D. How those in power counted and used them |

* Answer: Officials' records about them → D. How those in power counted and used them; Graves and objects → A. What they made, used or were buried with; A rival's description, read carefully → C. ## Make: Typical: a register of absences

In the Egyptian village of **Deir el-Medina** lived the craftsmen who built the royal tombs. The British Museum holds a flat piece of limestone from the village, used as a writing surface, with a register of about forty workmen over a year in the reign of Ramesses II (about 3,250 years ago). Next to each absence is a reason: being ill, eye trouble, a death in the family, building a house, a festival.
