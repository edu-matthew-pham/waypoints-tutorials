# Who made it, and what does it show?

Source origin and purpose · Who made it, and what does it show?

<!-- Speaker note: Students learn the first two questions to ask of any source: origin (who made it, when and where) and content (what it shows or says). The first of two hinge lessons on this skill: students who read the maker off the content (a soldier made it because it shows a soldier) carry the mistake into every later question about context and purpose. About 40 minutes (Predict and Try 8 min · Worked example 12 min · Vary 8 min · Practise 6 min · Make 6 min. Typical and wider steps are extras and not counted in this.). -->

---

## Today

- I can say who made a source, when and where.
- I can say what a source shows or says, without mixing that up with who made it.
- I can keep origin and content apart in my own words.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: This is a hinge lesson. Lesson 3 (context and purpose) only works if students can keep origin and content apart. If this lesson goes thinly, the later questions will be answered from the content alone. Spend the time here. The worked example is ancient China in the Qin and Han period. It is a model: for your own depth study, swap in sources from Egypt, Greece, Rome, India or another, with the same two questions. A silver Roman coin (Source E, typical) shows how one is swapped in. The source cards are plain-words descriptions, not quotations. The inscription (Source C) is the exception worth knowing: the original stones are mostly lost, and the words survive as copied in Sima Qian's history. The card summarises what the inscriptions are known to say, in our own words. The clay warriors, the bamboo slips from Shuihudi (1975) and the museum label are real or described accurately. The first lesson (optional) covers primary and secondary. A class that has not done it can still use this lesson, though they will not yet have met the words primary and secondary. Teacher: Try: stop at 'It was buried near the First Emperor's tomb'. A place can feel like content. It is origin. Teacher: Worked example: let Nib's mistake sit before explaining it. Ask which line is wrong, then why. Teacher: Vary: ask students to give the reason as well as the answer. -->

## Why it matters

> A clay warrior stands in a tomb in China. It is dressed as a soldier. So was it made by a soldier? Let's look at what the figure tells us, and what it doesn't.

<!-- Speaker note: Every source has two things you can ask about straight away. **Origin** is where the source came from: **who** made it, **when** and **where**. **Content** is what is in the source: **what** it shows, says or records. They sound close, but they are different questions. A source can show one thing and have been made by someone quite different. > **Source A: a clay warrior.** A life-size figure made of baked clay, standing about 1.8 metres tall. It is dressed as a soldier, with armour and a belt, and each figure has a different face. It was one of thousands buried near the tomb of the First Emperor of Qin, in what is now Shaanxi province, China. It was made by craftsmen in workshops, around the time the emperor died (210 BCE). -->

- If a historian wanted to know who made it, what would help?

## Ask the questions

**Source B is below. Is each fact about its origin or its content?**

Groups: Origin · Content

It was buried with a Qin official in 217 BCE · It lists rules for looking after grain stores · It was written by officials of the Qin government · It has rules and punishments for people who break them

* Answer: It was buried with a Qin official in 217 BCE → Origin; It lists rules for looking after grain stores → Content; It was written by officials of the Qin government → Origin; It has rules and punishments for people who break them → Content

## Find Nib's mistake

**Nib is looking at Source A, the clay warrior. Click the line where Nib's thinking goes wrong.**

```
 1  Nib is looking at a clay warrior from the First Emperor's tomb (Source A).
 2  Nib sees that the figure is dressed as a soldier, in armour.
 3  Nib asks "Who made it?" and writes: "Soldiers, because it shows a soldier."
 4  Nib asks "What does it show?" and writes: "A soldier in armour."
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: Nib has answered 'who made it?' by looking at what the figure shows. The warrior is dressed as a soldier, but that is its content. The origin is the craftsmen who made it in workshops. -->

## Why keep them apart

**Why is it useful to ask origin and content as two separate questions?**

- Because content is always wrong
- Because origin is more important
- Because what a source shows is not always who made it
- Because there are two kinds of source

* Answer: Because what a source shows is not always who made it

<!-- Speaker note: A source can show one thing and be made by someone else. Asking both questions keeps them apart. -->

## Dev's reply

**Dev says: 'The inscription praises the emperor, so the emperor wrote it.' What is the best reply?**

- Praise means it is false

## Exit ticket

**A clay warrior shows a soldier in armour. What does that tell you about its origin, and what does it tell you about its content?**

* Nothing about its origin: who made it, when and where are separate questions. It tells you its content: what it shows.
