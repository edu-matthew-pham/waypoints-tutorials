## Why it matters

> A ruler’s inscription says a town prospered. A dig finds burnt roofs. Does one source have to be thrown away?

<!-- Speaker note: An **inscription** is writing cut or painted onto something hard, such as stone or metal. Laws, letters and accounts are written sources too. Reading ancient writing is not always easy: words can be broken off, a word can have more than one meaning, and it has to be translated. Even when we can read every word, we still ask who wrote it, why, and what was going on at the time. These two source cards are **made up**, set in an invented ancient town. They are not translations of a real Qin inscription. - **Written card:** a ruler's stone inscription praises a new storehouse and says the town is prosperous. One damaged word could mean “gift” or “payment”. - **Physical card:** a dig finds a storehouse floor with burnt roof wood lying on top of it. Written sources, objects and spoken accounts each need their own questions. Writing is not always the most reliable kind. Checking a claim against different kinds of source is sometimes called **triangulation**. -->

## Check Nib’s claim

**Nib says: “It was carved in stone, so the town must have been prosperous for everyone.” Which reply is best?**

- The burnt floor tells the whole story, so use that instead
- Writing in stone can never be used as evidence, so ignore it
- It shows what the ruler claimed; check it against other evidence

* Answer: It shows what the ruler claimed; check it against other evidence

<!-- Speaker note: An inscription is good evidence of what someone chose to record. Whether it was true for everyone needs more evidence. -->

## Vary: two readings of one word

Now change one thing. One reader translates the damaged word as “gift”. Another translates it as “payment”. Both explain their reasons. The card itself does not settle it.

## An order to repair a road

**A second made-up inscription orders a road to be repaired. What does it show most directly?**

- That the road was broken by a war or a flood
- That the road was repaired, since the order was carved
- That a repair was ordered, not that it was done

* Answer: That a repair was ordered, not that it was done

<!-- Speaker note: An order is not proof that it was carried out. The remains of the road, or later records, could help. What this step is for: Students apply the same reasoning to a new written source on their own. -->

## Exit ticket

**An edict orders road repairs. What can it show, what remains uncertain, and what evidence could help?**

* It records an order. Whether repairs happened remains uncertain. Archaeological evidence of repairs or later records could help.
