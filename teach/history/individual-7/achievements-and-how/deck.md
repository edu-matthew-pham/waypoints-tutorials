## Today

**We are learning to** describe the First Emperor's main achievements with evidence and explain how each one changed the society, including who carried it out.

- I can describe two of the First Emperor's achievements with evidence.
- I can explain how an achievement changed things, not just that it did.
- I can name the other people who made the changes happen.

## Why it matters

> An order from the emperor says: from now on, everyone measures grain the same way. How does one sentence written in the capital change what a farmer does a thousand kilometres away?

<!-- Speaker note: Saying someone *did* something is only half the answer. A historian also explains **how** it changed things: the action, the way it worked, and what was different afterwards. **Action → how it worked → what changed** | Action (from 221 BCE) | How it worked | What changed | |---|---|---| | **One way of writing.** Each old kingdom had written characters in its own way. The Qin made one standard script. | Officials in every commandery wrote and read orders and reports in the same characters. | An order from the capital could be read in the same way across the empire. | | **The same weights, measures and coins.** One set of standard weights and lengths, the same width between cart wheels, and one round bronze coin with a square hole. | Officials used the standard measures to collect grain tax and check goods. | Tax and trade worked the same way everywhere, so it was harder to cheat and easier to trade. | | **Roads, canals and walls.** New roads and canals; older walls in the north joined into a long line of defences. | Huge numbers of men were called up to build them, organised by officials using the registers. | Armies, officials and messages moved faster; the northern frontier was guarded. | | **One system of laws.** The Qin's written laws applied in every commandery and county. | Appointed officials, who could be dismissed, ran each area and applied the laws (you met them in *People and power*). | The old kingdoms' rulers lost power; people across the empire answered to the same rules. | **Evidence from more than one kind of source.** Some Qin weights that survive have the emperor's order of 221 BCE, to make measures the same, written on them. Qin coins survive in large numbers. The bamboo slips from a Qin official's tomb show the laws he worked with. The emperor's own stone inscriptions praise him for one set of laws, weights and measures. Sima Qian, writing about a century later, describes the changes too. **A story told with care.** According to Sima Qian, in 213 BCE the chief minister Li Si advised the emperor to burn books of older histories and teachings that could be used to criticise him, keeping books on farming, medicine and fortune-telling. Sima Qian also reports that the next year more than 460 scholars who criticised the emperor were killed. Historians test these details. Many historians accept there was an order against some books in 213 BCE, but they argue about how much was lost. Many think the story of the scholars grew bigger in the telling: Sima Qian wrote for the Han, the dynasty that replaced the Qin, and some historians point out that no surviving earlier text mentions it. -->
