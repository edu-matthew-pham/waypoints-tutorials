## Today

**We are learning to** describe the effects of contacts and conflicts involving Han China (conquest, trade and peace agreements) and explain what each changed for every society involved.

- I can describe effects of contact and conflict of more than one kind.
- I can explain what an effect changed, not just that something changed.
- I can show that contact changed more than one society, not only the winner.

## Why it matters

> For about sixty years, a Han emperor sent silk, food and a bride to the ruler of his enemies every so often. What did each side get out of that?

<!-- Speaker note: Contacts and conflicts had **effects**: things that changed because of them. Historians look for three big kinds: | Kind of effect | What it means | |---|---| | **Conquest** | One society takes over land and the people living on it | | **Trade** | Goods, animals and ideas start to move between societies | | **Peace agreement** | Rulers agree terms to stop fighting, for a while at least | And they ask the same question every time: **who changed, and how?** The answer is usually *both sides*. > **A peace agreement: the heqin (from about 198 BCE).** In 200 BCE the first Han emperor led an army north and was trapped by the Xiongnu at a place called Baideng. He got away, but he could not beat them. About two years later the Han agreed the first **heqin** ("peace through kinship") agreement: a woman of the royal family married the Xiongnu ruler, and the Han sent gifts of silk, food and other goods. The agreement treated the two rulers as equals. Han writers say raids did not stop completely, but the Han kept to this kind of agreement until Emperor Wu changed course in the 130s BCE. > **Conquest: the Hexi Corridor (from 121 BCE).** West of the Han heartland is the Hexi Corridor, a long, narrow strip of land between mountains and desert. In 121 BCE a Han army drove the Xiongnu out of it. Over the following years (from about 111 BCE) the Han set up four commanderies there, including Dunhuang at the far western end, built farming garrison towns for soldiers and settlers, and by about 100 BCE had extended their walls as far as Dunhuang. The Xiongnu lost grazing land and a route they had controlled. > **Trade: goods both ways.** With the corridor held, Han envoys and merchants could travel west more safely. Chinese silk went west, passed from trader to trader across Central Asia. Horses, grapes, alfalfa (a crop for feeding horses), wool and other goods came east. Today these routes are often called the **Silk Roads**, but that is a modern name, made up by European writers in the 1800s. People at the time did not use it. -->

## For the Han

**Emperor Wu's wars against the Xiongnu were very expensive. What did that change inside Han China?**

- The government ran short of money and brought in new taxes
- The emperor stopped collecting taxes so farmers would join the army
- Nothing, because the Han won, and winners do not pay for wars
- The Xiongnu paid for the wars, so Han people paid less tax
