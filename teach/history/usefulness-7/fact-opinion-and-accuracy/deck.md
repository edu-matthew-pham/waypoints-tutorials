## Today

**We are learning to** distinguish factual claims from opinions and apply the four accuracy questions to test a source's reliability.

- I can tell the difference between a factual claim and an opinion in a historical source.
- I can ask the four accuracy questions to judge how reliable a source is.
- I can explain how bias, purpose or limited information affects a source's accuracy.

## Why it matters

> A Roman writer says: 'Some gladiators wore bronze helmets, and all gladiators were brave.' One half of that sentence can be checked in a museum case. What about the other half?

<!-- Speaker note: In *Source origin and purpose*, you asked four questions of a source: who made it (**origin**), what it shows (**content**), what was going on (**context**), and why it was made (**purpose**). Now you take the next step: judging **accuracy**. Accuracy means how factually reliable a source's claims are. Most sources mix two kinds of statements: - A **fact** (or factual claim) is something that can be checked against evidence, such as *"Some gladiators wore helmets"* or *"The Qin set one standard for weights and measures."* - An **opinion** is a judgement, belief or feeling that cannot be proved true or false by a single check, such as *"All gladiators were brave"* or *"The First Emperor was a wise and kind ruler."* To test a source's **accuracy**, historians ask four questions: 1. **Where does it come from?** (Who made it, and were they in a position to know?) 2. **What information is provided?** 3. **Is the information factual or an opinion?** 4. **What other sources might support or challenge it?** -->

- Why can a single sentence in a primary source contain both a checkable fact and a biased opinion?

## Predict

**Source C, the First Emperor's mountain inscription (219 BCE), claims that he set one system of weights and measures, and that his rule is 'glorious and without equal'. How should a historian treat those two claims?**

- Ignore the whole inscription, because any source with an opinion in it has no facts
- Treat the weights-and-measures claim as a factual claim that can be checked against archaeological finds, and 'glorious and without equal' as an opinion
- Treat both as facts, because the stone was carved during the First Emperor's reign
- Treat both as opinions, because the inscription is more than two thousand years old

## Testing Source C with the four accuracy questions

**Historians want to check whether Source C is accurate when it claims the Qin introduced one standard system of weights and measures. Which other source would best support or challenge that claim?**

- Another copy of the same praise poem carved by the same court officials
- Bronze weights and measuring vessels excavated from different parts of the Qin empire, stamped with the same official measure
- A modern textbook paragraph that repeats Source C's words without citing other evidence
- Sima Qian's moral judgement that the First Emperor was a harsh ruler

* Answer: Bronze weights and measuring vessels excavated from different parts of the Qin empire, stamped with the same official measure

<!-- Speaker note: Checking one source against a different kind of evidence — such as excavated bronze weights — is how historians test whether a factual claim is accurate. -->

## Find Nib's mistake

**Nib is checking the accuracy of Source C, the mountain inscription. Click the line where Nib's thinking goes wrong.**

```
 1  Source C is a stone inscription set up for the First Emperor around 219 BCE.
 2  It says the emperor united the states, set one system of weights and measures, and is the greatest ruler of all time.
 3  Nib says: "Every sentence in a primary source from the time is a fact, because the writer lived back then."
 4  Nib says: "So 'the greatest ruler of all time' is a factual claim, just like the rule about weights and measures."
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: Nib assumes every sentence in a primary source is a fact just because the writer lived at the time. Primary sources were made by real people with purposes and biases, so they contain opinions, boasting and one-sided claims alongside facts. -->

## How purpose affects accuracy

**Why might Source C be less accurate when it claims that 'all people across the empire are happy and at peace'?**

- It is a secondary source written long after the Qin empire fell
- Public stone monuments were only allowed to record neutral statistics
- It gives only the perspective of conquered peasant farmers and leaves out the court's view
- Its purpose was official propaganda to win support after a war of conquest, so its makers had a strong reason to leave out discontent

* Answer: Its purpose was official propaganda to win support after a war of conquest, so its makers had a strong reason to leave out discontent

<!-- Speaker note: A source's purpose and bias affect its accuracy: an official monument made to celebrate a conquest is not a neutral survey of how conquered people felt. -->

## Working record vs public boast

**Mei reads a Qin bamboo slip (Source B) that records how many sacks of grain were stored in a county warehouse and the fine for losing them. Why is this likely to be more accurate about day-to-day rules than a mountain praise inscription?**

- Because it is a secondary source written by neutral modern historians
- Because government officials never have a point of view or make mistakes
- It was a working record written for officials who actually had to count the grain and apply the fines, not a public boast
- Because it is older than the mountain inscription, and older sources are always more accurate

* Answer: It was a working record written for officials who actually had to count the grain and apply the fines, not a public boast

<!-- Speaker note: A working administrative record needed to be accurate enough for officials to use, whereas a public monument was designed to impress. -->

## Exit ticket

**Give one example of a fact and one example of an opinion about the ancient past, and name two questions you can ask to test a source's accuracy.**

* Fact: 'Some gladiators wore helmets' (or 'Qin laws were written on bamboo slips'). Opinion: 'All gladiators were brave' (or 'The First Emperor was the greatest ruler'). Accuracy questions: Where does it come from? What information is provided? Is it factual or an opinion? What other sources support or challenge it?
