## Today

**We are learning to** distinguish factual claims from opinions and apply the four accuracy questions to test a source's reliability.

- I can tell the difference between a factual claim and an opinion in a historical source.
- I can ask the four accuracy questions to judge how reliable a source is.
- I can explain how bias, purpose or limited information affects a source's accuracy.

## Why it matters

> Imagine a made-up Roman writer who says: 'Some gladiators wore bronze helmets, and all gladiators were brave.' One half of that sentence can be checked in a museum case. What about the other half?

<!-- Speaker note: In *Source origin and purpose*, you asked four questions of a source: who made it (**origin**), what it shows (**content**), what was going on (**context**), and why it was made (**purpose**). Now you take the next step: judging **accuracy**. Accuracy means how far you can trust what a source claims. Most sources mix two kinds of statements: - A **factual claim** is something that could be checked against evidence, such as *"Some gladiators wore helmets"* or *"The Qin set one standard for weights and measures."* A factual claim can still be wrong. What makes it factual is that you could check it. - An **opinion** is a judgement, belief or feeling. No single check can prove it true or false. For example: *"All gladiators were brave"* or *"The First Emperor was a wise and kind ruler."* To test a source's **accuracy**, historians ask four questions: 1. **Where does it come from?** (Who made it, and were they in a position to know?) 2. **What information is provided?** 3. **Is the information factual or an opinion?** 4. **What other sources might support or challenge it?** -->

## Think, pair, share

**A stone monument ordered by an ancient king says: 'I built forty granaries across the capital, and every citizen loved my wise rule.' Which part of that sentence can archaeologists test with a spade, and which part is an opinion?**

- What physical evidence would be left behind if forty granaries were really built?
- Why can a single sentence in a primary source contain both a checkable fact and a biased opinion?

**Source C, the First Emperor's mountain inscription (around 219 BCE), says he set one system of weights and measures. It also praises him as a ruler without equal. How should a historian treat those two statements?**

- Both are facts, because the stone was carved during the First Emperor's reign
- The praise is a fact too, because the emperor really did unite the kingdoms
- Both are opinions, because the inscription was made to praise the emperor

## Testing Source C with the four accuracy questions

**Historians want to check Source C's claim that the Qin brought in one standard system of weights and measures. Which other source would best support or challenge that claim?**

- Bronze weights from different parts of the empire, stamped with the same measure
- Another of the emperor's mountain inscriptions, which makes the same claim
- Sima Qian's history, which says the First Emperor was a harsh ruler
- A modern textbook that repeats Source C's claim without saying where it got it

* Answer: Bronze weights from different parts of the empire, stamped with the same measure

<!-- Speaker note: Checking a source against a different kind of evidence, such as bronze weights that have been dug up, is how historians test a factual claim. -->

## How purpose affects accuracy

**Why might Source C be inaccurate when it claims that everyone now lives in peace?**

- Its carvers could not have known how people felt across the empire
- It gives only the view of conquered farmers, not the court's view
- It was probably made to win support after a conquest, so it would leave out unrest
- It is a secondary source, written long after the Qin empire fell

* Answer: It was probably made to win support after a conquest, so it would leave out unrest

<!-- Speaker note: 'Everyone now lives in peace' is a factual claim: you could check it. It is almost certainly false. Big revolts broke out soon after the emperor died, and the stone was made to persuade, so unrest was left out. -->

## Working rule or public boast?

**Mei reads a rule on a Qin bamboo slip (Source B). It sets the fine for an official whose grain store comes up short. Why is this likely to be more accurate about everyday rules than a mountain inscription that praises the emperor?**

- Officials wrote it, and officials had no reason to get things wrong
- It was buried in a tomb, so no one could change it later
- It was a working rule officials had to use, not a public boast
- It is older than the mountain inscription, and older means more accurate

* Answer: It was a working rule officials had to use, not a public boast

<!-- Speaker note: Officials used these rules every day, so they had to state the real rules. A public monument was made to impress. -->

## Exit ticket

**Give one example of a fact and one example of an opinion about the ancient past, and name two questions you can ask to test a source's accuracy.**

* Fact: 'Some gladiators wore helmets' (or 'Qin laws were written on bamboo slips'). Opinion: 'All gladiators were brave' (or 'The First Emperor was a ruler without equal'). Accuracy questions: Where does it come from? What information is provided? Is it factual or an opinion? What other sources support or challenge it?
