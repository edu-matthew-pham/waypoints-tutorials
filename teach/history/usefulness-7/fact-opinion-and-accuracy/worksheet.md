# Fact, opinion and accuracy

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Imagine a made-up Roman writer who says: 'Some gladiators wore bronze helmets, and all gladiators were brave.' One half of that sentence can be checked in a museum case. What about the other half?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students distinguish factual claims from opinions and apply the four accuracy questions ('Where does it come from?', 'What information is provided?', 'Is it factual or an opinion?', 'What other sources might support or challenge it?') to assess a source's reliability. This is the first of two hinges in AC9HH7S04: without a clear grasp of accuracy, students conflate accuracy and usefulness in lesson 2.

**Students should be able to say:** I can tell the difference between a factual claim and an opinion in a historical source. I can ask the four accuracy questions to judge how reliable a source is. I can explain how bias, purpose or limited information affects a source's accuracy.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: checking Source C's accuracy


:::keep-together

### Testing Source C with the four accuracy questions

**Historians want to check Source C's claim that the Qin brought in one standard system of weights and measures. Which other source would best support or challenge that claim?**

☐ Bronze weights from different parts of the empire, stamped with the same measure

☐ Another of the emperor's mountain inscriptions, which makes the same claim

☐ Sima Qian's history, which says the First Emperor was a harsh ruler

☐ A modern textbook that repeats Source C's claim without saying where it got it

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Bronze weights from different parts of the empire, stamped with the same measure

Checking a source against a different kind of evidence, such as bronze weights that have been dug up, is how historians test a factual claim.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find Nib's mistake

**Nib is checking the accuracy of Source C, the mountain inscription. Click the first line where Nib's thinking goes wrong.**

```
 1  Source C is a stone inscription set up for the First Emperor around 219 BCE.
 2  It says he joined the kingdoms into one empire, set one system of weights and measures, and is a ruler without equal.
 3  Nib says: "Every sentence in a primary source from the time is a fact, because the writer lived back then."
 4  Nib says: "So 'a ruler without equal' is a factual claim, just like the rule about weights and measures."
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: Nib thinks every sentence in a primary source is a fact because the writer lived at the time. Primary sources were made by real people with purposes. They hold opinions, boasts and one-sided claims as well as factual claims.

:::


:::keep-together

### How purpose affects accuracy

**Why might Source C be inaccurate when it claims that everyone now lives in peace?**

☐ Its carvers could not have known how people felt across the empire

☐ It gives only the view of conquered farmers, not the court's view

☐ It was probably made to win support after a conquest, so it would leave out unrest

☐ It is a secondary source, written long after the Qin empire fell

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** It was probably made to win support after a conquest, so it would leave out unrest

'Everyone now lives in peace' is a factual claim: you could check it. It is almost certainly false. Big revolts broke out soon after the emperor died, and the stone was made to persuade, so unrest was left out.

:::

### What limits accuracy?

**What is affecting accuracy in each example?**

Groups: Purpose / bias, Limited information, Opinion stated as fact

- A king's victory monument leaves out a battle his army lost: {{field-15}}
- A traveller writes about a whole empire after visiting only one port town for two days: {{field-15}}
- A farmer's private diary says 'The new canal is the most beautiful thing ever made': {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** A king's victory monument leaves out a battle his army lost → Purpose / bias; A traveller writes about a whole empire after visiting only one port town for two days → Limited information; A farmer's private diary says 'The new canal is the most beautiful thing ever made' → Opinion stated as fact

:::keep-together

### Working rule or public boast?

**Mei reads a rule on a Qin bamboo slip (Source B). It sets the fine for an official whose grain store comes up short. Why is this likely to be more accurate about everyday rules than a mountain inscription that praises the emperor?**

☐ Officials wrote it, and officials had no reason to get things wrong

☐ It was buried in a tomb, so no one could change it later

☐ It was a working rule officials had to use, not a public boast

☐ It is older than the mountain inscription, and older means more accurate

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** It was a working rule officials had to use, not a public boast

Officials used these rules every day, so they had to state the real rules. A public monument was made to impress.
