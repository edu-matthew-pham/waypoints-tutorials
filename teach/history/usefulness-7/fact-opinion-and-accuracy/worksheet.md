# Fact, opinion and accuracy

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** A Roman writer says: 'Some gladiators wore bronze helmets, and all gladiators were brave.' One half of that sentence can be checked in a museum case. What about the other half?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students distinguish factual claims from opinions and apply the four accuracy questions ('Where does it come from?', 'What information is provided?', 'Is it factual or an opinion?', 'What other sources might support or challenge it?') to assess a source's reliability. This is the first of two hinges in AC9HH7S04: without a clear grasp of accuracy, students conflate accuracy and usefulness in lesson 2.

**Students should be able to say:** I can tell the difference between a factual claim and an opinion in a historical source. I can ask the four accuracy questions to judge how reliable a source is. I can explain how bias, purpose or limited information affects a source's accuracy.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: checking Source C's accuracy


:::keep-together

### Testing Source C with the four accuracy questions

1. $\text{1. Where does it come from?}$ — Ordered by the First Emperor around 219 BCE, soon after conquering six rival kingdoms.
2. $\text{2. What information is provided?}$ — Claims he united the land, standardised laws and measures, and brought universal peace and happiness.
3. $\text{3. Fact or opinion?}$ — Standardising laws and measures is a factual claim; 'glorious rule' and 'everyone is happy' are opinions and praise.
4. $\text{4. What other sources check it?}$ — Excavated Qin bronze weights and the Shuihudi legal slips support the standardisation claim; records of rebellions and harsh punishments challenge the 'everyone was happy' claim.

Now: how do you test a factual claim in Source C, where does Nib go wrong, and why does purpose affect accuracy?

**Historians want to check whether Source C is accurate when it claims the Qin introduced one standard system of weights and measures. Which other source would best support or challenge that claim?**

☐ Another copy of the same praise poem carved by the same court officials

☐ Bronze weights and measuring vessels excavated from different parts of the Qin empire, stamped with the same official measure

☐ A modern textbook paragraph that repeats Source C's words without citing other evidence

☐ Sima Qian's moral judgement that the First Emperor was a harsh ruler

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Bronze weights and measuring vessels excavated from different parts of the Qin empire, stamped with the same official measure

Checking one source against a different kind of evidence — such as excavated bronze weights — is how historians test whether a factual claim is accurate.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find Nib's mistake

**Nib is checking the accuracy of Source C, the mountain inscription. Click the line where Nib's thinking goes wrong.**

```
 1  Source C is a stone inscription set up for the First Emperor around 219 BCE.
 2  It says the emperor united the states, set one system of weights and measures, and is the greatest ruler of all time.
 3  Nib says: "Every sentence in a primary source from the time is a fact, because the writer lived back then."
 4  Nib says: "So 'the greatest ruler of all time' is a factual claim, just like the rule about weights and measures."
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: Nib assumes every sentence in a primary source is a fact just because the writer lived at the time. Primary sources were made by real people with purposes and biases, so they contain opinions, boasting and one-sided claims alongside facts.

:::


:::keep-together

### How purpose affects accuracy

**Why might Source C be less accurate when it claims that 'all people across the empire are happy and at peace'?**

☐ It is a secondary source written long after the Qin empire fell

☐ Public stone monuments were only allowed to record neutral statistics

☐ It gives only the perspective of conquered peasant farmers and leaves out the court's view

☐ Its purpose was official propaganda to win support after a war of conquest, so its makers had a strong reason to leave out discontent

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Its purpose was official propaganda to win support after a war of conquest, so its makers had a strong reason to leave out discontent

A source's purpose and bias affect its accuracy: an official monument made to celebrate a conquest is not a neutral survey of how conquered people felt.

## Judge the accuracy


:::keep-together

### What limits accuracy?

**Which factor is affecting accuracy in each example?**

Groups: Purpose / bias, Limited information, Opinion stated as fact

- A king's victory monument leaves out a battle his army lost: {{field-15}}
- A traveller writes about a whole empire after visiting only one port town for two days: {{field-15}}
- A chronicler writes 'Our city's laws are the fairest in the world': {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** A king's victory monument leaves out a battle his army lost → Purpose / bias; A traveller writes about a whole empire after visiting only one port town for two days → Limited information; A chronicler writes 'Our city's laws are the fairest in the world' → Opinion stated as fact

:::


:::keep-together

### Working record vs public boast

**Mei reads a Qin bamboo slip (Source B) that records how many sacks of grain were stored in a county warehouse and the fine for losing them. Why is this likely to be more accurate about day-to-day rules than a mountain praise inscription?**

☐ Because it is a secondary source written by neutral modern historians

☐ Because government officials never have a point of view or make mistakes

☐ It was a working record written for officials who actually had to count the grain and apply the fines, not a public boast

☐ Because it is older than the mountain inscription, and older sources are always more accurate

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** It was a working record written for officials who actually had to count the grain and apply the fines, not a public boast

A working administrative record needed to be accurate enough for officials to use, whereas a public monument was designed to impress.
