# Weighing a source as evidence

Source usefulness · Weighing a source as evidence

## Today

**We are learning to** weigh both the accuracy and the usefulness of a source in one reasoned conclusion for an inquiry question.

- I can assess both the accuracy and the usefulness of a source for a specific inquiry question.
- I can join accuracy and usefulness into one reasoned conclusion rather than two separate labels.
- I can state both what a source contributes to an inquiry and what it cannot tell us on its own.

## Why it matters

> When a historian uses Sima Qian's history or the Shuihudi bamboo slips in an argument, they don't just write 'Accurate: yes' or 'Useful: yes'. How do you join accuracy and usefulness into one clear judgement?

<!-- Speaker note: You now have both lenses from this course: 1. **Accuracy:** how factually reliable the source is (separating fact from opinion, checking origin and purpose for bias or limits, and asking what other sources corroborate it). 2. **Usefulness:** what the source can contribute to answering your **specific inquiry question** (even when it is biased or partial), and what it **cannot** tell you on its own. In this final lesson, you put both together into an **integrated evidence assessment** — not two separate one-word labels, but a joined explanation of what a source is worth for an inquiry. -->

## Predict

**A student is investigating: 'How harsh was Qin law for ordinary people?' They look at Source B (the Shuihudi bamboo slips of Qin laws). Which assessment of Source B is best?**

## Integrated assessment: Source C on Qin rule

**Inquiry question: 'How did the First Emperor control ideas and opposition in the Qin empire?' Sima Qian (writing 100 years later for the Han court) writes that the First Emperor burned histories and executed scholars who criticised him. How should a historian assess Sima Qian's accuracy and usefulness here?**

- Because Sima Qian was a famous historian, every detail is completely accurate and needs no other sources
- Because Sima Qian wrote under the Han, nothing in his book can be used for this question
- His accuracy may be affected by Han bias against the Qin and the 100-year gap, so his numbers and details need checking against Qin records — but his account is still useful evidence that the First Emperor used censorship and harsh punishment against critics
- Because his account is useful for this question, we do not need to check its accuracy or bias

* Answer: His accuracy may be affected by Han bias against the Qin and the 100-year gap, so his numbers and details need checking against Qin records — but his account is still useful evidence that the First Emperor used censorship and harsh punishment against critics

## Combining two sources

**To build a fuller answer to 'How did the Qin government enforce its laws?', why is it better to combine Source B (the bamboo law slips) with Source C (the mountain inscription) than to use either one alone?**

- Because Source C is a secondary source and Source B is a primary source
- Because using two sources automatically makes every opinion in both sources true
- Because Source B has no purpose or context of its own, so it needs Source C to give it one
- Source C shows how the emperor publicly justified his laws as bringing peace, while Source B shows the actual fines and punishments officials used on the ground

* Answer: Source C shows how the emperor publicly justified his laws as bringing peace, while Source B shows the actual fines and punishments officials used on the ground

<!-- Speaker note: Where one source is strong (official public message), the other fills in what it leaves out (day-to-day legal penalties). -->

## A joined assessment

**What must a complete evidence assessment of a source include?**

- How accurate its claims are, what it usefully contributes to the specific inquiry question, and what it cannot tell us on its own
- Only the date the source was found and the name of the museum it sits in
- A single word saying either 'true' or 'false'
- A list of every word in the source without mentioning the inquiry question

* Answer: How accurate its claims are, what it usefully contributes to the specific inquiry question, and what it cannot tell us on its own

## Vary: match the assessment part



<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Checking that every part of a reasoned evidence assessment is present and connected. For students who need more support: Three-sentence frame: '1. Accuracy: Because [source] was made by [who/when] to [purpose], its claims about [X] are [accurate / biased / limited]. 2. Usefulness: For the question [inquiry question], it is useful evidence of [what it shows]. 3. Limit: However, it cannot tell us [what is missing], so we would also need [other source].' For early finishers: Take two sources from your depth study — one written and one archaeological — and explain in three sentences how using both together overcomes the limits of each one on its own. The hard part, on purpose: Students often treat accuracy and usefulness as two separate one-word boxes ('Accurate: no. Useful: yes.') without linking them. Push for a joined sentence: 'Because this source was made by [origin] to [purpose], it is accurate/limited on [X], which makes it useful for showing [Y], though it cannot tell us [Z].' When students get stuck: A student writes two disconnected sentences: 'It is biased. It is useful.' Ask: How does the bias connect to what it is useful for? Fix: Join them with 'so' or 'which means': 'It is biased towards the emperor, so it cannot prove people were happy, but it is useful for showing how the court used propaganda.' / A student forgets to mention the inquiry question. Ask: Useful for finding out what? Fix: Always name the inquiry question in the usefulness sentence. / A student says a source tells us everything we need to know. Ask: Whose view or what part of the story is missing from this single source? Fix: Every single source has a limit. Name one thing it cannot show and what kind of second source would help. -->

## Write a complete evidence assessment

**Inquiry question: 'How did the Qin state govern its people?' Choose either Source B (the Shuihudi bamboo law slips, 217 BCE) or Source C (the First Emperor's mountain inscription, 219 BCE). Assess both its accuracy and its usefulness as evidence for this question, stating what it can and cannot contribute.**

* Answer: Assessing Source B (Shuihudi bamboo slips): Source B is a working set of Qin laws buried with a local official in 217 BCE. Because officials used these slips to run granaries and courts, they are accurate evidence of the rules and fines the Qin state set, though they do not prove every official obeyed them. That makes Source B very useful for answering how the Qin governed: it shows the state controlled local officials through strict inspections, measurements and penalties. However, it cannot tell us how ordinary villagers felt about those laws.; Assessing Source C (Mountain inscription): Source C was carved around 219 BCE on the First Emperor's orders to celebrate uniting the empire. Its claim to have standardised laws and weights is accurate (backed up by excavated Qin weights), while its claim that everyone lived in happy peace is biased court praise. For our inquiry question, it is useful evidence both of standardisation and of how the state used public monuments to persuade conquered people — but it cannot tell us what life under Qin laws actually felt like for ordinary families.

## Exit ticket

**In three sentences, assess Source C (the First Emperor's mountain inscription) as evidence for the question: 'How did the First Emperor unite and rule the Qin empire?'**

* Source C was ordered by the First Emperor around 219 BCE to celebrate his rule; its claim about standardising laws and measures is accurate (backed by excavated weights), while its claim that everyone was happy is biased praise. It is useful evidence of how the state standardised measures and used public propaganda to claim authority. However, it cannot tell us how ordinary people actually experienced Qin rule, so we need legal records and archaeological evidence alongside it.
