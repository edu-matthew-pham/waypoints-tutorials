## Today

**We are learning to** weigh both the accuracy and the usefulness of a source in one reasoned conclusion for an inquiry question.

- I can assess both the accuracy and the usefulness of a source for a specific inquiry question.
- I can join accuracy and usefulness into one reasoned conclusion rather than two separate labels.
- I can state both what a source contributes to an inquiry and what it cannot tell us on its own.

## Why it matters

> When a historian uses Sima Qian's history or the Shuihudi bamboo slips in an argument, they don't just write 'Accurate: yes' or 'Useful: yes'. How do you join accuracy and usefulness into one clear judgement?

<!-- Speaker note: You now have two ways of looking at a source: 1. **Accuracy:** how far you can trust its claims. You separate factual claims from opinions, look at its origin and purpose for bias or limits, and ask what other sources back it up or challenge it. 2. **Usefulness:** what the source can add to answering your **inquiry question** (even when it is biased or partial), and what it **cannot** tell you on its own. In this lesson you put both together. Not two one-word labels, but one joined explanation of what a source is worth for your question. -->

## Predict

**A student's inquiry question is: 'How harsh was Qin law for ordinary people?' They look at Source B (the Shuihudi bamboo slips of Qin laws). Which assessment of Source B is best?**

- The slips were written by the Qin state, which wanted to look strong, so they are biased and cannot be used here
- As working rules, the slips can be trusted on the written punishments, so they help here, but they cannot show how often the rules were used
- As working rules from a Qin official's tomb, the slips are primary, so they show exactly how harsh life was for everyone
- The slips are accurate because they are primary, and useful because they are about Qin law, so they are a good source

## One joined assessment: Source C

**Inquiry question: 'How did the First Emperor deal with ideas he did not like?' Sima Qian (writing about 100 years later for the Han court) reports that in 213 BCE the emperor ordered some books to be burned. How should a historian weigh this?**

- Useless: he wrote for the Han, who disliked the Qin, so none of it can be used
- Accurate: he used older court records, so his details need no other checking
- Useful, as he reports an order against books; check his details, as Han writers disliked the Qin
- Useful for this question, so there is no need to check its accuracy as well

* Answer: Useful, as he reports an order against books; check his details, as Han writers disliked the Qin

<!-- Speaker note: Many historians accept there was an order against some books in 213 BCE, but they argue about how much was lost. A good assessment uses Sima Qian's account and tests its details against other evidence. -->

## Combining two sources

**To answer 'How did the Qin government enforce its laws?', why is it better to use Source B (the bamboo law slips) together with Source C (the mountain inscription) than either one alone?**

- Source C is biased, so Source B shows what really happened instead
- Source C gives the public message; Source B the written fines officials were meant to apply
- Source C is a secondary source, so Source B is needed as the primary one
- When two sources agree, everything both of them say can be treated as fact

* Answer: Source C gives the public message; Source B the written fines officials were meant to apply

<!-- Speaker note: Each fills a gap in the other: the public message and the written rules. Neither shows how harshly the rules were applied every day. -->

## A joined assessment

**What must a complete evidence assessment of a source include?**

- How far to trust it, what it adds to the question, and what it cannot tell us
- Whether it is primary or secondary, and when and where it was made
- How accurate it is, with reasons, since accuracy decides usefulness
- Whether it is useful, and which inquiry question it helps answer

* Answer: How far to trust it, what it adds to the question, and what it cannot tell us

## Vary: match the assessment part



<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Checking that every part of a reasoned assessment is there and linked. For students who need more support: Three-sentence frame: '1. Accuracy: Because [source] was made by [who/when] to [purpose], its claims about [X] are [accurate / biased / limited]. 2. Usefulness: For the question [inquiry question], it is useful evidence of [what it shows]. 3. Limit: However, it cannot tell us [what is missing], so we would also need [other source].' The hard part, on purpose: Students often treat accuracy and usefulness as two separate one-word boxes ('Accurate: no. Useful: yes.') without linking them. When students get stuck: A student writes two disconnected sentences: 'It is biased. It is useful.' Ask: How does the bias connect to what it is useful for? Fix: Join them with 'so' or 'which means': 'It is biased towards the emperor, so it cannot prove everyone lived in peace, but it is useful for showing how the court used propaganda.' / A student forgets to mention the inquiry question. Ask: Useful for finding out what? Fix: Always name the inquiry question in the usefulness sentence. / A student says a source tells us everything we need to know. Ask: Whose view or what part of the story is missing from this single source? Fix: Every single source has a limit. Name one thing it cannot show and what kind of second source would help. -->

## Write a complete assessment

**Inquiry question: 'How did the Qin state govern its people?' Choose either Source B (the Shuihudi bamboo law slips, 217 BCE) or Source C (the First Emperor's mountain inscription, about 219 BCE). Assess both its accuracy and its usefulness for this question, and say what it can and cannot add.**

* Answer: Assessing Source B (the Shuihudi bamboo slips): Source B is a set of Qin laws buried with a local official in 217 BCE. Officials used these rules to run grain stores and courts, so they can be trusted on the rules and fines the Qin state set, though they do not prove every official obeyed them. That makes Source B very useful for how the Qin governed: it shows the state controlled local officials through strict checks, measuring and fines. However, it cannot tell us how ordinary villagers felt about those laws.; Assessing Source C (the mountain inscription): Source C was carved around 219 BCE on the First Emperor's orders to celebrate joining the kingdoms into one empire. Its claim about one set of laws, weights and measures is backed up by Qin weights that have been dug up, but its claim that everyone now lives in peace is almost certainly false and was written to persuade. For our question it is useful evidence of the single set of laws and measures, and of how the state used public monuments to win over conquered people. But it cannot tell us what life under Qin laws was like for ordinary families.

## Exit ticket

**In three sentences, assess Source C (the First Emperor's mountain inscription) as evidence for the question: 'How did the First Emperor unite and rule the Qin empire?'**

* Source C was ordered by the First Emperor around 219 BCE to celebrate his rule; its claim about one set of laws and measures is backed up by Qin weights that have been dug up, while its claim that everyone now lives in peace is almost certainly false and written to persuade. It is useful evidence of how the state standardised measures and used public propaganda to claim authority. However, it cannot tell us how ordinary people actually experienced Qin rule, so we need legal records and archaeological evidence alongside it.
