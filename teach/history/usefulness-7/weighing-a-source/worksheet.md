# Weighing a source as evidence

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** When a historian uses Sima Qian's history or the Shuihudi bamboo slips in an argument, they don't just write 'Accurate: yes' or 'Useful: yes'. How do you join accuracy and usefulness into one clear judgement?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students join accuracy and usefulness into one reasoned assessment for an inquiry question, saying what the source can and cannot add. This is the lesson that brings AC9HH7S04 together.

**Students should be able to say:** I can assess both the accuracy and the usefulness of a source for a specific inquiry question. I can join accuracy and usefulness into one reasoned conclusion rather than two separate labels. I can state both what a source contributes to an inquiry and what it cannot tell us on its own.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::


## Predict


:::keep-together

**A student's inquiry question is: 'How harsh was Qin law for ordinary people?' They look at Source B (the Shuihudi bamboo slips of Qin laws). Which assessment of Source B is best?**

☐ The slips were written by the Qin state, which wanted to look strong, so they are biased and cannot be used here

☐ As working rules, the slips can be trusted on the written punishments, so they help here, but they cannot show how often the rules were used

☐ As working rules from a Qin official's tomb, the slips are primary, so they show exactly how harsh life was for everyone

☐ The slips are accurate because they are primary, and useful because they are about Qin law, so they are a good source

My prediction, and why:

{{lines-3}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**What happens:** The first one. A strong assessment says why the source can be trusted on one point, how that helps with the inquiry question, and what gap is left. The third gives reasons, but never says what the slips can and cannot show about this question.

:::

## Worked example: weighing Sima Qian


:::keep-together

### One joined assessment: Source C

**Inquiry question: 'How did the First Emperor deal with ideas he did not like?' Sima Qian (writing about 100 years later for the Han court) reports that in 213 BCE the emperor ordered some books to be burned. How should a historian weigh this?**

☐ Useless: he wrote for the Han, who disliked the Qin, so none of it can be used

☐ Accurate: he used older court records, so his details need no other checking

☐ Useful, as he reports an order against books; check his details, as Han writers disliked the Qin

☐ Useful for this question, so there is no need to check its accuracy as well

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Useful, as he reports an order against books; check his details, as Han writers disliked the Qin

Many historians accept there was an order against some books in 213 BCE, but they argue about how much was lost. A good assessment uses Sima Qian's account and tests its details against other evidence.

:::


:::keep-together

```
 1  Inquiry question: "How did the Qin government enforce its laws across the empire?"
 2  Source B is the Shuihudi bamboo slips (217 BCE), which list official rules and fines for handling grain.
 3  Nib says: "Accuracy: accurate. Usefulness: useful."
 4  Nib says: "That is a complete historical assessment because I wrote both words."
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: 'Accuracy: accurate. Usefulness: useful' is two bare labels. There is no reason, no link to the inquiry question, and nothing about what the source can and cannot show.

:::

### Combining two sources

**To answer 'How did the Qin government enforce its laws?', why is it better to use Source B (the bamboo law slips) together with Source C (the mountain inscription) than either one alone?**

☐ Source C is biased, so Source B shows what really happened instead

☐ Source C gives the public message; Source B the written fines officials were meant to apply

☐ Source C is a secondary source, so Source B is needed as the primary one

☐ When two sources agree, everything both of them say can be treated as fact

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Source C gives the public message; Source B the written fines officials were meant to apply

Each fills a gap in the other: the public message and the written rules. Neither shows how harshly the rules were applied every day.

## Write a complete assessment

Write it in your book first, then compare with a model answer.

:::keep-together

**Inquiry question: 'How did the Qin state govern its people?' Choose either Source B (the Shuihudi bamboo law slips, 217 BCE) or Source C (the First Emperor's mountain inscription, about 219 BCE). Assess both its accuracy and its usefulness for this question, and say what it can and cannot add.**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Assessing Source B (the Shuihudi bamboo slips): Source B is a set of Qin laws buried with a local official in 217 BCE. Officials used these rules to run grain stores and courts, so they can be trusted on the rules and fines the Qin state set, though they do not prove every official obeyed them. That makes Source B very useful for how the Qin governed: it shows the state controlled local officials through strict checks, measuring and fines. However, it cannot tell us how ordinary villagers felt about those laws.; Assessing Source C (the mountain inscription): Source C was carved around 219 BCE on the First Emperor's orders to celebrate joining the kingdoms into one empire. Its claim about one set of laws, weights and measures is backed up by Qin weights that have been dug up, but its claim that everyone now lives in peace is almost certainly false and was written to persuade. For our question it is useful evidence of the single set of laws and measures, and of how the state used public monuments to win over conquered people. But it cannot tell us what life under Qin laws was like for ordinary families.

:::

## Exit ticket

:::keep-together

**In three sentences, assess Source C (the First Emperor's mountain inscription) as evidence for the question: 'How did the First Emperor unite and rule the Qin empire?'**

{{lines-3}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Source C was ordered by the First Emperor around 219 BCE to celebrate his rule; its claim about one set of laws and measures is backed up by Qin weights that have been dug up, while its claim that everyone now lives in peace is almost certainly false and written to persuade. It is useful evidence of how the state standardised measures and used public propaganda to claim authority. However, it cannot tell us how ordinary people actually experienced Qin rule, so we need legal records and archaeological evidence alongside it.

:::
