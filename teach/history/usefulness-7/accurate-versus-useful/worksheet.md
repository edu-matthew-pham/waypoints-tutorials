# Accurate versus useful

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Suppose you find an ancient inscription where a king boasts about a victory that never happened. It is completely inaccurate about the battle. Why would a museum and a historian still treasure it?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students separate accuracy (is it factually reliable?) from usefulness (does it help answer our specific inquiry question?), and see why a biased, one-sided or limited source is still valuable evidence — including for revealing official propaganda or the power structures of an ancient society. This is the second hinge in AC9HH7S04.

**Students should be able to say:** I can explain the difference between accuracy and usefulness. I can explain why a biased or inaccurate source can still be useful evidence for the right inquiry question. I can explain how limited evidence about a group can still show how power worked in an ancient society.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

☐ No: because it is biased propaganda, a historian cannot use it for any question

☐ No: inscriptions are only useful for studying stone-carving tools

☐ Yes, but only if we assume every word carved on the stone is literally true

## Worked example: why Nib throws away good evidence


:::keep-together

### One source, two questions

1. $\text{Source C}$ — The First Emperor's mountain inscription (219 BCE).
2. $\text{Accuracy check}$ — High accuracy on what the official court message was; low accuracy on whether conquered people actually felt happy and peaceful.
3. $\text{Usefulness for Question 1}$ — "Did conquered people welcome Qin rule?" -> Low usefulness on its own (it hides opposition).
4. $\text{Usefulness for Question 2}$ — "How did the Qin state present its power in public?" -> High usefulness (it is a surviving piece of official public propaganda).

Now: what separates accuracy from usefulness, where does Nib go wrong, and what can limited evidence still reveal about power structures?

**What is the key difference between asking whether a source is *accurate* and asking whether it is *useful*?**

☐ Accuracy asks whether its claims are factually reliable; usefulness asks whether it helps answer a specific inquiry question

☐ Accuracy is only for primary sources; usefulness is only for secondary sources

☐ There is no difference: an inaccurate source is never useful

☐ Accurate sources are written down; useful sources are always objects

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Accuracy asks whether its claims are factually reliable; usefulness asks whether it helps answer a specific inquiry question

Accuracy is about factual reliability; usefulness is about value as evidence for the question you are asking.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find Nib's mistake

**Nib is deciding whether to use Source C in an investigation of Qin rule. Click the line where Nib's thinking goes wrong.**

```
 1  Source C is a mountain inscription ordered by the First Emperor around 219 BCE to praise his rule.
 2  Because it was made to boast, its claim that everyone felt happy and peaceful is biased and not accurate.
 3  Nib says: "If a source is biased or inaccurate, it is completely useless to a historian and should be thrown away."
 4  Nib says: "So Source C cannot be used as evidence for any historical question at all."
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: Nib equates 'biased' with 'useless'. A biased source cannot be taken at face value, but it is still very useful evidence of its maker's perspective, beliefs and propaganda.

:::


:::keep-together

### Limited evidence and power structures

**Very few written sources from ancient China were written by peasant farmers themselves; most surviving texts were written by court officials and scholars. What can those official sources still usefully tell us about peasant farmers?**

☐ Nothing at all, because a source written by officials is biased and therefore useless for studying peasants

☐ Every private thought and opinion peasant families had about the emperor, just as if peasants had written it themselves

☐ Only how officials lived at court, because official records never mention taxation, grain or labour service

☐ How the state taxed, conscripted and governed peasants, and how rulers viewed their place in the power structure

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** How the state taxed, conscripted and governed peasants, and how rulers viewed their place in the power structure

Even when evidence for a group is limited or written from above, it provides useful insight into how power and obligations worked in that society.

:::

## Pick the useful evidence


:::keep-together

### Evidence for an interpretation

**A student is testing the interpretation: 'The Qin state used strict legal punishments to force officials to obey the central government.' Which detail from the sources is the most useful evidence to support this interpretation?**

☐ Sima Qian lived during the Han dynasty

☐ Farmers found the first clay warriors while digging a well in 1974

☐ The clay warriors in the First Emperor's tomb were originally painted in bright colours

☐ The Shuihudi bamboo slips specify exact fines and punishments for officials whose grain stores are short or whose reports are late

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** The Shuihudi bamboo slips specify exact fines and punishments for officials whose grain stores are short or whose reports are late

Useful evidence must directly support the specific interpretation being made.
