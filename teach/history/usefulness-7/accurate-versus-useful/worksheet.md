# Accurate versus useful

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Suppose you find an ancient inscription where a king boasts about a victory that never happened. It is completely wrong about the battle. Why would a museum and a historian still treasure it?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students separate accuracy (can we trust its claims?) from usefulness (does it help answer our inquiry question?), and see why a biased, one-sided or limited source is still valuable evidence, for example of official propaganda or of the power structures of an ancient society. This is the second hinge in AC9HH7S04.

**Students should be able to say:** I can explain the difference between accuracy and usefulness. I can explain why a biased or inaccurate source can still be useful evidence for the right inquiry question. I can explain how limited evidence about a group can still show how power worked in an ancient society.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::


## Predict


:::keep-together

**A historian's inquiry question is: 'How did the First Emperor try to convince people that he had the right to rule?' They look at Source C, the mountain inscription that praises him. Is Source C useful for this question?**

☐ No: his own officials made it, so it only shows what they wanted

☐ Yes, but only for the parts of it that we can prove are true

☐ No: it is propaganda, so a historian cannot use it for any question

☐ Yes: its praise shows the message the emperor wanted people to believe

My prediction, and why:

{{lines-3}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**What happens:** Yes. When your question is about how a ruler presented himself, a biased official monument is just the evidence you need. 'It only shows what they wanted' is exactly why it fits this question.

## Worked example: why Nib throws away good evidence


:::keep-together

### One source, two questions

**What is the key difference between asking whether a source is *accurate* and asking whether it is *useful*?**

☐ Accuracy asks if other sources agree; usefulness asks if it is easy to read

☐ There is no real difference: if a source is inaccurate, it cannot be useful

☐ Accuracy asks whether it is primary; usefulness asks whether it is secondary

☐ Accuracy asks if you can trust its claims; usefulness asks if it helps answer your question

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Accuracy asks if you can trust its claims; usefulness asks if it helps answer your question

Accuracy is about how far you can trust the claims. Usefulness is about whether it is evidence for the question you are asking.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find Nib's mistake

**Nib is deciding whether to use Source C in an investigation of Qin rule. Click the first line where Nib's thinking goes wrong.**

```
 1  Source C is a mountain inscription ordered by the First Emperor around 219 BCE to praise his rule.
 2  Because it was made to boast, its claim that everyone now lives in peace is biased and not accurate.
 3  Nib says: "If a source is biased or inaccurate, it is completely useless to a historian and should be thrown away."
 4  Nib says: "So Source C cannot be used as evidence for any historical question at all."
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: Nib treats 'biased' as 'useless'. You cannot take a biased source at face value, but it is still very useful evidence of its maker's view, beliefs and propaganda.

:::


:::keep-together

### Limited evidence and power

**Very few written sources from ancient China were written by farmers themselves. Most were written by officials and scholars. What can those official sources still tell us about farmers?**

☐ How the state taxed farmers, made them work for it, and governed them

☐ What farming families thought of the emperor, as officials knew their views

☐ Nothing at all, because sources written by officials are biased and useless

☐ Only how officials lived, because officials' records are always about officials

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** How the state taxed farmers, made them work for it, and governed them

Even when evidence about a group is limited, or written from above, it shows how power and duties worked in that society.

## Pick the useful evidence


:::keep-together

### Evidence for an explanation

**A student is testing a historian's explanation: 'The Qin state used strict punishments to make its officials obey.' Which detail is the most useful evidence for it?**

☐ Sima Qian, writing later, says the First Emperor was harsh and cruel

☐ Source C says everyone lives under one set of laws, weights and measures

☐ The Shuihudi slips set fines for officials whose grain is short or reports are late

☐ The clay warriors stand in neat, ordered ranks, like a real army

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** The Shuihudi slips set fines for officials whose grain is short or reports are late

Useful evidence must directly support the explanation you are testing.

## Exit ticket

:::keep-together

**Can a biased source that exaggerates a ruler's achievements still be useful to a historian? Explain why in two sentences.**

{{lines-3}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Yes. Even if its claims are not accurate about what really happened, it is still useful evidence for an inquiry into the ruler's propaganda, beliefs, and how they wanted to be seen by their subjects.
