## Today

**We are learning to** separate accuracy from usefulness and explain why a biased or limited source can still be useful evidence.

- I can explain the difference between accuracy and usefulness.
- I can explain why a biased or inaccurate source can still be useful evidence for the right inquiry question.
- I can explain how limited evidence about a group can still show how power worked in an ancient society.

## Why it matters

> Suppose you find an ancient inscription where a king boasts about a victory that never happened. It is completely inaccurate about the battle. Why would a museum and a historian still treasure it?

<!-- Speaker note: Last lesson you tested **accuracy**: whether a source's claims are factually reliable. Students often think that once they spot bias or inaccuracy in a source, their job is done: *"It's biased, so it's useless."* Historians do the opposite. They keep **accuracy** and **usefulness** separate: - **Accuracy** asks: *Are the claims in this source factually reliable?* - **Usefulness** asks: *Does this source give us evidence that helps answer our specific inquiry question?* A source with low accuracy for one question can have **high usefulness** for another. An inscription full of royal boasting is inaccurate if your question is *"How did conquered villagers really feel?"*, but it is **very useful** if your question is *"How did the emperor use public monuments to justify his power?"* Its bias is the very thing that makes it useful evidence of propaganda. -->

## One source, two questions

**What is the key difference between asking whether a source is *accurate* and asking whether it is *useful*?**

- Accuracy asks whether its claims are factually reliable; usefulness asks whether it helps answer a specific inquiry question
- Accuracy is only for primary sources; usefulness is only for secondary sources
- There is no difference: an inaccurate source is never useful
- Accurate sources are written down; useful sources are always objects

* Answer: Accuracy asks whether its claims are factually reliable; usefulness asks whether it helps answer a specific inquiry question

<!-- Speaker note: Accuracy is about factual reliability; usefulness is about value as evidence for the question you are asking. -->

## Limited evidence and power structures

**Very few written sources from ancient China were written by peasant farmers themselves; most surviving texts were written by court officials and scholars. What can those official sources still usefully tell us about peasant farmers?**

- Nothing at all, because a source written by officials is biased and therefore useless for studying peasants
- Every private thought and opinion peasant families had about the emperor, just as if peasants had written it themselves
- Only how officials lived at court, because official records never mention taxation, grain or labour service
- How the state taxed, conscripted and governed peasants, and how rulers viewed their place in the power structure

* Answer: How the state taxed, conscripted and governed peasants, and how rulers viewed their place in the power structure

<!-- Speaker note: Even when evidence for a group is limited or written from above, it provides useful insight into how power and obligations worked in that society. -->

## Accurate vs useful

**Why can a biased royal inscription still be useful evidence for a historian?**

- It shows how the ruler wanted to be seen and how power was justified, even if its boasts are not factually accurate
- Because historians must believe everything carved in stone
- Because bias only happens in modern newspapers, never in ancient sources
- It is only useful for weighing how heavy the stone is

* Answer: It shows how the ruler wanted to be seen and how power was justified, even if its boasts are not factually accurate

- The clay warriors in the First Emperor's tomb were originally painted in bright colours

## Exit ticket

**Can a biased source that exaggerates a ruler's achievements still be useful to a historian? Explain why in two sentences.**
