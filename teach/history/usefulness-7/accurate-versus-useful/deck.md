## Today

**We are learning to** separate accuracy from usefulness and explain why a biased or limited source can still be useful evidence.

- I can explain the difference between accuracy and usefulness.
- I can explain why a biased or inaccurate source can still be useful evidence for the right inquiry question.
- I can explain how limited evidence about a group can still show how power worked in an ancient society.

## Why it matters

> Suppose you find an ancient inscription where a king boasts about a victory that never happened. It is completely wrong about the battle. Why would a museum and a historian still treasure it?

<!-- Speaker note: Last lesson you tested **accuracy**: how far you can trust what a source claims. Once students spot bias in a source, many decide their job is done: *"It's biased, so it's useless."* Historians do the opposite. They keep **accuracy** and **usefulness** apart. The question your investigation is trying to answer is called the **inquiry question**. - **Accuracy** asks: *Can I trust the claims in this source?* - **Usefulness** asks: *Does this source give me evidence that helps answer my inquiry question?* A source can be inaccurate for one question and very useful for another. An inscription full of royal boasting is a poor guide if your question is *"How did conquered villagers really feel?"* But it is **very useful** if your question is *"How did the emperor use public monuments to show his power?"* Its bias is what makes it good evidence of propaganda (a message made to persuade). -->

## Predict

**A historian's inquiry question is: 'How did the First Emperor try to convince people that he had the right to rule?' They look at Source C, the mountain inscription that praises him. Is Source C useful for this question?**

- No: his own officials made it, so it only shows what they wanted
- Yes, but only for the parts of it that we can prove are true
- No: it is propaganda, so a historian cannot use it for any question
- Yes: its praise shows the message the emperor wanted people to believe

<!-- Speaker note: What happens: Yes. When your question is about how a ruler presented himself, a biased official monument is just the evidence you need. 'It only shows what they wanted' is exactly why it fits this question. Take two or three predictions before anyone runs it. -->

## One source, two questions

**What is the key difference between asking whether a source is *accurate* and asking whether it is *useful*?**

- Accuracy asks if other sources agree; usefulness asks if it is easy to read
- There is no real difference: if a source is inaccurate, it cannot be useful
- Accuracy asks whether it is primary; usefulness asks whether it is secondary
- Accuracy asks if you can trust its claims; usefulness asks if it helps answer your question

* Answer: Accuracy asks if you can trust its claims; usefulness asks if it helps answer your question

<!-- Speaker note: Accuracy is about how far you can trust the claims. Usefulness is about whether it is evidence for the question you are asking. -->

## Limited evidence and power

**Very few written sources from ancient China were written by farmers themselves. Most were written by officials and scholars. What can those official sources still tell us about farmers?**

- How the state taxed farmers, made them work for it, and governed them
- What farming families thought of the emperor, as officials knew their views
- Nothing at all, because sources written by officials are biased and useless
- Only how officials lived, because officials' records are always about officials

* Answer: How the state taxed farmers, made them work for it, and governed them

<!-- Speaker note: Even when evidence about a group is limited, or written from above, it shows how power and duties worked in that society. -->

## Accurate vs useful

**Why can a biased royal inscription still be useful evidence for a historian?**

- It shows how the ruler wanted to be seen, even if its boasts are untrue
- Its boasts must be true, because it was carved at the time
- It is useful only for the parts that other sources agree with
- It is not useful: a biased source cannot be used for anything

* Answer: It shows how the ruler wanted to be seen, even if its boasts are untrue

<!-- Speaker note: A biased source is poor evidence if taken at face value, but strong evidence of the maker's purpose, beliefs and propaganda. ## Evidence for an explanation

**A student is testing a historian's explanation: 'The Qin state used strict punishments to make its officials obey.' Which detail is the most useful evidence for it?**

- Sima Qian, writing later, says the First Emperor was harsh and cruel
- Source C says everyone lives under one set of laws, weights and measures
- The Shuihudi slips set fines for officials whose grain is short or reports are late
- The clay warriors stand in neat, ordered ranks, like a real army

* Answer: The Shuihudi slips set fines for officials whose grain is short or reports are late

## Exit ticket

**Can a biased source that exaggerates a ruler's achievements still be useful to a historian? Explain why in two sentences.**

* Yes. Even if its claims are not accurate about what really happened, it is still useful evidence for an inquiry into the ruler's propaganda, beliefs, and how they wanted to be seen by their subjects.
