# Judge a tool

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Every new tool restarts the argument unless you judge it the same way each time: what it's for, what it does to the thinking, and where the data goes.


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** An optional route through Choosing AI tools: teachers judge a proposed tool with three questions (what it's for, whether it helps learning, whether it can be trusted), sort a vendor's claims, and write a trial question.

**Students should be able to say:** I can judge a proposed AI tool by its purpose, its effect on learning and its data. I can tell a claim about doing with help from evidence of learning. I can write a question a small trial could answer.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

My prediction, and why:

{{lines-3}}

:::teacher-note

**What happens:** Very little on its own. Higher homework scores with the tool show students did better *with help*, not that they learned more: what matters is what they can do later without it. It may still be worth a trial, but the claim isn't the evidence.

:::


## The idea

Judge a proposed tool with three questions, in this order. The guide has a section for each.

1. **What is it for?** The learning purpose, and the thinking it touches (lesson 1's question, for a tool). Would a well-prompted general tool the school already has do the same job? (Guide: *Start with the learning purpose*; *Compare against a well-prompted general LLM*.)
2. **Does it help learning, or just doing?** Does it give answers, or hints that keep the thinking with the student? What does its evidence measure: doing with help, or learning later? (Guide: *Three kinds of tools to evaluate*.)
3. **Can we trust it?** Where student work goes, what's switched on by default, the minimum age, who gains and who loses. (Guide: *Can we trust it?*)

Then **a small, fair trial**, with a question it answers, before anyone adopts it. (Guide: *Choose a small trial*; *Example trial questions*.) And the decision goes to whoever decides on tools at your school, against its AI principles: you recommend; the school decides.

## Apply


**Three questions and a trial.** Use the guide's sections for each question.

**Who decides.** Recommend; don't adopt on your own.

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?
