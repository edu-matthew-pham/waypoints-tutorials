# The misconception and the opening move

> **Key question:** A student who already believes something wrong can sit through a clear explanation, pass the quiz, and still believe it next week.

## Think first


:::keep-together

**Ask a class why it's hotter in summer. What's the answer you'll hear most?**

☐ The Sun gives out more heat in summer

☐ The Earth's axis is tilted

☐ The Earth is closer to the Sun in summer

☐ There are fewer clouds

My prediction, and why:

{{lines-3}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**What happens:** "We're closer to the Sun." It's the classic misconception, held by many adults too. The example lesson never asks, so a student can read about the tilt, get every check right, and still believe it's distance.

:::


## The idea

Students don't arrive empty. If a lesson explains the right idea without first bringing out the wrong one, many students keep both: the right one for the quiz, the wrong one for everything else.

So the lesson's first move is a question students answer **before** being taught, where **the misconception gives a definite wrong answer**. Students lock in their answer, then see what really happens. The surprise is what makes the idea stick.

A good opening question:

- can be answered with no teaching (a prediction, a puzzle, a choice);
- has the misconception as one of its options, worded the way students would say it;
- has an answer that clearly contradicts the misconception, so it can't be quietly kept.

**Where to find the misconception:** students' wrong answers in your own classes; and, when you start from a curriculum waypoint, Create's **Possible misconceptions** list (from Waypoints Kit's hinge reasons and response modes), each with **Use this**.

## Worked example

Rewriting the example lesson's opening, one step at a time:

1. **The misconception:** summer is when the Earth is closer to the Sun.
2. **A question where it gives a definite answer:** the Earth is closest to the Sun in early January. If distance caused seasons, January would be summer everywhere.
3. **Make it bite:** ask about the other hemisphere. In Australia, January *is* summer, so asking about Sydney would seem to confirm the misconception. Ask about London instead: it's winter there.
4. **What students see after locking in:** "Winter. In January the Earth is at its closest to the Sun, but it's winter in the north. Distance can't be the cause."

The site's own seasons lesson, [Why is summer hot?](../../../science/earth-7/seasons/), opens the same way and then lets students test it on a model of the Earth.

:::keep-together

**Which opening question would bring out the distance misconception best?**

☐ In early January the Earth is at its closest to the Sun. What season is it in London then?

☐ Draw the Earth going around the Sun.

☐ How many degrees is the Earth's axis tilted?

☐ What causes the seasons?

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** In early January the Earth is at its closest to the Sun. What season is it in London then?

Students who think distance causes seasons will say summer. It's winter in London: closest to the Sun, and cold. The misconception gives a definite wrong answer, and the real answer contradicts it.

:::


## Check


:::keep-together

**Many students believe multiplying always makes a number bigger. Which opening question brings that out?**

☐ Is multiplying the same as repeated adding?

☐ Without working it out: which is bigger, 8 × 0.5 or 8?

☐ Write the rule for multiplying decimals.

☐ What is 8 × 0.5?

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Without working it out: which is bigger, 8 × 0.5 or 8?

A student who believes multiplying makes bigger picks 8 × 0.5, commits, and then sees it's 4: half of 8.

:::
