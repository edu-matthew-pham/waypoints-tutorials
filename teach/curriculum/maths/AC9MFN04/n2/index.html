← AC9MFN04 Part-Part-Whole to 10

Partitioning a collection in different ways

AC9MFN04 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Foundation Maths · Number

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can partition a collection of up to 10 objects into two parts in more than one way, naming both parts and the whole each time.

Success criteria

  • Student partitions a collection of up to 10 objects into at least two different pairs of parts
  • Student names both parts and the whole for each partition using part-part-whole language

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Recognising parts within a whole.

Width

Minimum
Partition a collection of 6 objects into two parts in at least two different ways, naming both parts and the whole each time.
Typical
Partition collections of up to 10 objects in multiple ways using physical or virtual materials; name each part-part-whole relationship; demonstrate that the same whole can be partitioned into different pairs of parts.
Wider
  • Investigate all the ways to partition a number to 10 systematically, recording part-part pairs and looking for patterns
  • Explore partitioning in First Nations Australians' counting and grouping systems — applying this to quantities of objects in the environment on Country/Place

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepA key misconception is that a number has only one partition (often the canonical split students see first, e.g. 5 as 4+1). Seeing a collection partitioned multiple ways — into different part pairs — is what builds the flexible part-part-whole thinking the descriptor requires. Breadth of examples is the resolution.
