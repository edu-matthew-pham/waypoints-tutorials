Recognising parts within a whole

AC9MFN04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Foundation Maths · Number · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can look at a structured representation of a number up to 10 and identify and name two parts that make the whole.

Success criteria

  • Student identifies two parts within a structured representation (e.g. ten-frame, domino) for at least three different numbers up to 10
  • Student describes the part-part-whole relationship using appropriate language

Prior knowledge

Students can count collections to at least 20 (AC9MFN03) and subitise collections to 5 (AC9MFN02). They have not yet systematically split a collection into two named parts and used part-part-whole language to describe the relationship.

Width

Minimum
Identify and name two parts in a ten-frame or domino arrangement of a number up to 10 (e.g. '7 — I can see 5 and 2 more').
Typical
Recognise numbers represented in physical or virtual ten-frames, dominoes and dot cards; identify two parts within the arrangement; describe the part-part-whole relationship using language ('___ is ___ and ___ more').
Wider
  • Explore First Nations Australians' number groupings and different ways of representing these groupings to form and partition numbers — connecting cultural representations to part-part-whole thinking
  • Use subitising to identify parts within standard number configurations (dice, dominoes) without counting individual dots

Hinge

Node 2 (partitioning in different ways) requires students to hold the whole in mind while varying the parts — which depends on first being able to see and name a part-part structure at all. Students who attend only to 'how many' cannot partition flexibly or combine in node 3.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents commonly describe 'how many' without attending to the internal structure of the number. Structured representations (ten-frames, dominoes) across several numbers make the part-part structure visible, but students need to encounter the same number in multiple structured arrangements to see that the parts can differ while the whole stays the same.
