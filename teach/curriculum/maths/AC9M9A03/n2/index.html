Parallel and perpendicular lines

AC9M9A03 n2 · waypoint 2 of 5 · Year 9 Maths · Algebra · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can use gradient to determine whether two lines are parallel or perpendicular.

Lessons on this site

  • Parallel and perpendicular

Success criteria

  • Correctly identifies parallel lines by comparing gradients
  • Correctly calculates the gradient of a line perpendicular to a given line
  • Verifies the perpendicular relationship using the product m₁×m₂=−1

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Gradient from two points.

Width

Minimum
State that parallel lines have equal gradients; calculate the gradient of two given lines and determine whether they are parallel.
Typical
Verify using examples that parallel lines have equal gradients and that perpendicular lines have gradients whose product is −1; apply these relationships to identify parallel/perpendicular lines from equations or coordinates; explain why the perpendicular gradient relationship holds.
Wider
  • Use dynamic geometry software to drag a line and observe how the gradient of a perpendicular line changes — visualise the m₁m₂=−1 relationship dynamically
  • Investigate how perpendicularity is used in coordinate geometry proofs (e.g. proving a quadrilateral is a rectangle using gradient)
  • Connect the perpendicular gradient relationship to rotation by 90° on the Cartesian plane

Hinge

Students who confuse the direction convention for perpendicular gradients (or believe all gradients are positive) fail to apply parallel/perpendicular tests correctly in N5 combined problems and in coordinate geometry proofs.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents often memorise 'product is −1' without understanding why. Seeing the relationship through multiple perpendicular pairs — including cases where gradients are fractions — and explaining it geometrically (rotation) builds robust understanding that survives transfer to novel problems.
