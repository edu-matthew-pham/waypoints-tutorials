← AC9MFM01 Measurement attributes

Communicating reasoning about comparisons

AC9MFM01 n3 · waypoint 3 of 3 · Foundation Maths · Measurement

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can compare objects or events on a chosen measurement attribute and communicate their reasoning using attribute-specific language.

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Student compares two objects or events on a specified attribute and correctly states which has more of that attribute
  • Student communicates at least one reason for their comparison using attribute-specific language

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Direct comparison of a single attribute.

Width

Minimum
Compare two objects on a measurement attribute, state the result, and give at least one reason for the comparison (e.g. 'I put them next to each other and this one sticks out further').
Typical
Compare objects and events across all four measurement attributes; explain or demonstrate how they know which has more or less of the attribute; use appropriate measurement language to communicate reasoning in familiar contexts.
Wider
  • Compare objects found in everyday home or community contexts (including objects on Country/Place) on a self-chosen attribute, communicating reasoning to a partner
  • Investigate and discuss situations where direct comparison is tricky (e.g. comparing the mass of a large light object with a small heavy object) and reason about what the comparison shows
  • Compare the duration of two familiar events by starting them at the same time, describe which took longer and explain why duration comparison requires a shared start point

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkCommunicating reasoning about measurement comparisons requires students to articulate not just the result but the method — how did they know? The reasoning scaffold (what did you do? what did you find out? how do you know?) is the specific thinking move Foundation students need to develop. Simply naming the result without method is a very common truncation.
Integration: a combining task at the wider tierThis node integrates attribute naming (node 1) and direct comparison technique (node 2) into coherent communication — students must coordinate knowing the attribute, applying the comparison method, and explaining the result. The coordination of these three moves is the endpoint demand.
