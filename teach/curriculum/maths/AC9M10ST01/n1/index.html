Identifying misleading representations

AC9M10ST01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 10 Maths · Statistics

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify specific features of a statistical graph or claim that could mislead a reader and explain the mechanism by which the misleading effect works.

Success criteria

  • Student identifies a specific misleading feature in a given statistical representation and names it accurately
  • Student explains the mechanism — how the feature creates a false impression — not just that it exists

Prior knowledge

Students can choose and justify appropriate data displays for given contexts (AC9M9ST04). They have not yet systematically analysed statistical claims in media for bias, misleading representations, or ethical considerations — moving from producing displays to critically reading others' statistical arguments.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least one specific misleading feature in a given statistical graph or report (e.g. truncated axis, non-zero baseline, cherry-picked time period) and explain how it creates a false impression.
Typical
Identify multiple types of misleading features across several statistical representations; explain the mechanism for each (how the visual or numerical choice distorts the impression); distinguish between deliberate misleading and ambiguity; recognise when data is not related to the claim being made.
Wider
  • Analyse how the identification of bias is critical in machine learning and deep learning — because biased training data produces systematically unfair or inaccurate AI outputs — connecting statistical critique to contemporary technology
  • Use the concept of Indigenous data sovereignty to critique and evaluate the Australian Government's 'Closing the Gap' report — examining whose data is collected, how it is framed, and what claims are made

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepMisleading representations take many forms — broken axes, cherry-picked endpoints, inappropriate scale choices, correlation presented as causation. Students who only practise with broken axes may not recognise other manipulation types. Multiple examples across different misleading techniques are needed.
