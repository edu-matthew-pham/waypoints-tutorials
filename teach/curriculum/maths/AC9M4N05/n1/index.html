Multiplying by 10 — digit movement

AC9M4N05 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 4 Maths · Number · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain and demonstrate that multiplying by 10 moves each digit one place to the left.

Success criteria

  • Students can multiply a two- or three-digit number by 10 without a calculator and explain the digit movement
  • Students can explain why the rule works using place value language (not just 'add a zero')

Prior knowledge

Students can recognise and represent numbers beyond 10 000 and understand place value conventions (AC9M3N01); they can multiply and divide one- and two-digit numbers (AC9M3N04). They have not yet applied the multiplicative structure of place value to multiply or divide by 10, 100 or 1000.

Width

Minimum
Use materials or a place value chart to show that 34 × 10 = 340; explain why each digit moves one place to the left.
Typical
Use place value charts, numeral expanders or sliders to demonstrate multiplying by 10 across multiple examples; explain why digits move left (each place becomes ten times larger); generalise the pattern.
Wider
  • Use numeral expanders or sliders to make visible the digit movement interpretation — physically sliding digits left and discussing whether 'adding a zero' captures the same idea or misrepresents it
  • Connect multiplication by 10 to metric unit conversions (e.g. 3 m × 10 = 30 dm or 3.0 m = 30 dm) — applying the pattern to measurement

Hinge

Students who hold the 'add a zero' model enter Node 2 with a rule that fails for ×100 and ×1000 edge cases and entirely for decimals. The digit-movement model is the prerequisite conceptual frame for generalising to all powers of 10.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepCommon misconception: 'multiply by 10 means add a zero.' This works for whole numbers but breaks down for decimals (0.3 × 10 ≠ 0.30). The correct conceptual model — digits move left — is the hinge. Seeing the pattern in place value charts, sliders and measurement contexts prevents the 'add a zero' shortcut from calcifying.
