← AC9M6SP02 Cartesian plane — 4 quadrants

Extending coordinates to all four quadrants

AC9M6SP02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 6 Maths · Space · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify and name the four quadrants of the Cartesian plane and locate points with integer coordinates in all four quadrants.

Success criteria

  • Student correctly plots points in all four quadrants.
  • Student reads coordinates of points in all quadrants and correctly identifies the quadrant from coordinate signs.

Prior knowledge

Students can construct and use grid coordinate systems to describe position and movement in the first quadrant (AC9M5SP02). Students can locate integers on a number line (AC9M6N01). They have not yet extended the coordinate system to all four quadrants using negative integers.

Width

Minimum
Label the four quadrants of a Cartesian plane; plot at least eight points, two per quadrant; read the coordinates of plotted points in all quadrants.
Typical
Plot and read coordinates in all four quadrants including on the axes; identify the quadrant from the signs of the coordinates (++, −+, −−, +−); describe the position of a point relative to the origin and the axes.
Wider
  • Use the four-quadrant plane to map a local area, game board, or treasure hunt with reference point at origin
  • Connect four-quadrant coordinates to integer number lines: each axis is a number line — why does this mean four quadrants?

Hinge

Reliable four-quadrant plotting and coordinate reading is the prerequisite for Node 2. Students who confuse coordinate signs or quadrant membership cannot describe coordinate changes for movements that cross axes.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents must learn to identify quadrants from coordinate sign patterns (++, −+, −−, +−) rather than re-counting from the origin each time. This sign-convention reasoning needs explicit scaffolding before students can describe coordinate changes that cross axes in Node 2.
