Real number line and inequalities

AC9M9N01 n3 · waypoint 3 of 4 · Year 9 Maths · Number · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can locate real numbers (including irrationals) on the number line and represent solution intervals for linear inequalities.

Success criteria

  • Correctly locates at least two irrational numbers approximately on a number line relative to known values
  • Solves a linear inequality and represents the solution interval correctly on a number line
  • Uses correct inequality notation and open/closed circle conventions when marking solution intervals

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Exact and approximate representations.

Width

Minimum
Locate √2 and π approximately on a number line by comparing to known benchmarks; represent the solution to one linear inequality (e.g. 2x+7<0) as an interval on the number line.
Typical
Locate a range of rational and irrational values on the number line using benchmarks and approximations; use a real number line to represent solution intervals for inequalities of the forms ax+b<c and ax+b>c; interpret number line representations of solutions.
Wider
  • Use geometric constructions to locate irrational numbers exactly on the number line (e.g. √2 as the diagonal of a unit square, √5 as the hypotenuse of a 1-2 right triangle)
  • Investigate compound inequalities (e.g. a<x<b) and represent solution sets on the number line
  • Connect number line representations of solution intervals to graphical solutions of linear inequalities in two variables

Hinge

Inequality sign-flip errors (when dividing by a negative) and mis-mapping solutions to number line intervals propagate directly into AC9M9A-strand inequality work. Students who cannot represent solution intervals correctly cannot proceed with inequality reasoning.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents often solve inequality mechanics correctly but make errors when negative coefficients require the inequality sign to flip, or when translating an algebraic solution interval into a number line diagram. Scaffolding the reasoning — why does dividing by a negative flip the sign? — prevents a persistent procedural error.
