← AC9M1N01 Numbers to 120

Two-digit numeral recognition

AC9M1N01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 1 Maths · Number · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can read and write two-digit numerals to at least 99, including numerals that look or sound similar (e.g. 16 and 61).

Success criteria

  • Reads two-digit numerals to 99 correctly, including commonly confused pairs such as 16 and 61.
  • Writes the numeral when a two-digit number is stated aloud.

Prior knowledge

Students can name, represent and order numbers to at least 20 (AC9MFN01). They can quantify and compare collections to at least 20 using counting (AC9MFN03). They have not yet read, written or ordered numbers in the two-digit range up to 120, or used patterns in the number system (tens/ones) to navigate across decades.

Width

Minimum
Read and write numerals for numbers 10–99; identify easily confused pairs (e.g. 16/61, 60/16) from a set.
Typical
Read, write and name two-digit numerals to 99 across a range of representations; practise with commonly confused pairs; use number charts to locate and name numerals.
Wider
  • Explore how two-digit numbers are represented in other numeral systems (Japanese kanji, Chinese characters), comparing the role of position versus special characters for tens.
  • Investigate how numbers are named and written in different languages — identifying where the spoken form makes the tens-ones structure explicit and where it is less obvious.

Hinge

Reliable numeral reading is required for Node 2 (hundreds chart navigation) and Node 3 (ordering on a number line) — students who confuse teen and decade numerals produce systematic ordering errors and cannot correctly locate numbers on the chart.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Consolidation: more practise steps, short feedback loopsConfusion between teen numbers (13–19) and decade multiples (30–90) is common and persistent. Fluency with numeral reading requires deliberate practice with the commonly confused pairs before students can reliably navigate the number line.
