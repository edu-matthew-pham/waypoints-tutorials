Uniform informal units

AC9M2M01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 2 Maths · Measurement · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain why the informal unit used for measurement must be uniform (identical) and demonstrate what happens when non-uniform units are used.

Success criteria

  • Explains that the informal unit must be the same size/mass for measurement to be valid
  • Demonstrates or explains what goes wrong when non-uniform units are used

Prior knowledge

Students order and compare objects by length, mass and capacity using direct comparison and a single informal unit (AC9M1M01, AC9M1M02). They have not yet justified unit choice, compared multiple units, or used smaller units for greater accuracy.

Width

Minimum
Measure the same object with two different-sized informal units; compare results; explain why the units must be the same.
Typical
Measure objects using uniform informal units across length, capacity and mass; explain why uniformity matters; demonstrate what goes wrong when units differ (e.g. different shoe sizes give different measurements of the same distance).
Wider
  • Investigate First Nations Australians' use of body parts (e.g. hands) as uniform informal units; discuss how a standard unit is maintained when different people measure
  • Investigate First Nations Australians' use of body measurement to understand animal behaviour: e.g. measuring length, width and depth of animal tracks

Hinge

All measurement activity in Nodes 2 and 3 depends on students understanding why unit uniformity is non-negotiable. Students who accept any same-type unit regardless of size will produce measurements in Nodes 2 and 3 that are meaningless — they cannot compare results across objects or justify unit choice.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepThe need for uniformity is not obvious — students often accept any same-type unit without noticing size differences. Demonstrating failure cases (non-uniform units produce different counts) and then success cases (uniform units produce consistent counts) across multiple contexts makes the principle visible.
