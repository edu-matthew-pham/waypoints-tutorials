Decomposing composite objects

AC9M10M01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 10 Maths · Measurement · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify the component solids in a composite object and describe how they combine (addition or subtraction of volumes/surfaces).

Lessons on this site

  • Break it into solids

Success criteria

  • Student correctly identifies the component solids of a given composite object
  • Student identifies which faces become internal when components join and explains why they are excluded from surface area calculations

Prior knowledge

Students can solve surface area and volume problems for right prisms and cylinders (AC9M9M01). They have not yet worked with composite objects — solids formed by combining or subtracting multiple familiar 3D shapes.

Width

Minimum
Given a composite solid, identify its component prisms or cylinders; state whether surfaces are added, removed, or partially removed when the components join.
Typical
Decompose composite objects into components in multiple ways; identify which faces become internal (no longer exterior) when components join; choose the most efficient decomposition for a given problem.
Wider
  • Explore how the same composite object can be decomposed in more than one way and verify that both decompositions give the same result
  • Investigate real composite structures (e.g. buildings with attached extensions, storage tanks with domed ends) and practise identifying their components from photographs or plans

Hinge

Students who cannot identify which faces become internal when solids join will systematically double-count or include hidden faces in N2 surface area calculations — the decomposition reasoning is the prerequisite for accurate calculation.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe internal face problem — faces that disappear when two solids join — is the main source of surface area errors. Students need the reasoning made explicit: when solids share a face, that face is removed from both surfaces, not just one.
