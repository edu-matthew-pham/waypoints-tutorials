Surface area from nets

AC9M9M01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 9 Maths · Measurement · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can calculate the surface area of a right prism or cylinder by identifying and summing the areas of all faces.

Lessons on this site

  • Surface area from nets

Success criteria

  • Correctly identifies all faces of a right prism or cylinder and their dimensions
  • Calculates the area of each face and sums to give total surface area
  • Uses appropriate units (cm², m²) and maintains consistency throughout

Prior knowledge

Students can solve problems involving the circumference and area of a circle using formulas (AC9M8M03). They have not yet extended these to three-dimensional objects — finding surface area by unfolding nets, or calculating volumes of right prisms and cylinders.

Width

Minimum
Draw or identify the net of a rectangular prism; calculate the area of each face; sum to find total surface area with appropriate units.
Typical
Analyse nets of right prisms (rectangular, triangular) and cylinders; identify all faces and their dimensions; calculate the area of each face and sum to find surface area; derive general surface area formulas from nets.
Wider
  • Investigate objects and technologies of First Nations Australians (e.g. woven baskets, bark containers) — analyse their surface structure and estimate the surface area in context
  • Compare the surface area of different right prisms with the same volume — conjecture which shape minimises surface area and test with calculations
  • Design packaging for a given object and determine the minimum material required, considering waste from cutting

Hinge

Systematic face-by-face accounting is prerequisite for N3 problem-solving. Students who miss curved faces (cylinder lateral surface) or double-count produce wrong surface areas and cannot reliably select the correct measure in applied problems.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkA common error is double-counting faces or missing curved surfaces (for cylinders). Systematic face-by-face accounting using the net — labelling each face, calculating its area, and summing — prevents these errors. The scaffold is the net itself: it makes every face explicit.
