Solving quadratic equations algebraically

AC9M9A04 n3 · waypoint 3 of 4 · Year 9 Maths · Algebra · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can solve monic quadratic equations with integer roots algebraically using factorisation and the null factor law.

Success criteria

  • Rewrites the equation in the form (x+p)(x+q)=0 before applying the null factor law
  • Correctly identifies both roots (including cases where roots are zero, equal, or negative)
  • Verifies at least one solution by substitution back into the original equation

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Graphing quadratic functions and identifying key features.

Width

Minimum
Factorise one monic quadratic equation and apply the null factor law to find the two solutions; verify solutions by substitution.
Typical
Solve a range of monic quadratic equations with integer roots by factorisation and the null factor law; recognise the connection between the factorised form (x−p)(x−q)=0 and the roots x=p, x=q; verify solutions by substitution and by graphical comparison.
Wider
  • Investigate how the null factor law generalises — why does ab=0 imply a=0 or b=0? What property of real numbers does this rely on?
  • Solve equations of the form x²=a using square roots, recognising two solutions; connect to the graph
  • Investigate cases where the quadratic does not factorise over integers and discuss what methods would be needed (preview of quadratic formula)

Hinge

Students who apply the null factor law to non-zero RHS (e.g. (x-2)(x+3)=6 → x=2 or x=-3) cannot solve equations reliably in N4 or in AC9M9A05 modelling contexts. The equation must equal zero — this is non-negotiable.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents commonly find one solution and stop, or misapply the null factor law (e.g. writing x+3=5 when the equation is (x−2)(x+3)=5 instead of 0). The equation must equal zero before the null factor law applies — seeing this across several cases with explicit checking resolves the error.
