← AC9M9A04 Quadratic functions and equations

Graphing quadratic functions and identifying key features

AC9M9A04 n2 · waypoint 2 of 4 · Year 9 Maths · Algebra

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can graph quadratic functions and identify their key features: turning point, axis of symmetry, x-intercepts, y-intercept, and whether the function has a maximum or minimum.

Success criteria

  • Correctly identifies the turning point coordinates and axis of symmetry equation for a graphed quadratic
  • Correctly reads x-intercepts from the graph and states 'no real solutions' when the graph does not cross the x-axis
  • Identifies whether the turning point is a maximum or minimum and explains why based on the direction the parabola opens

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Identifying quadratic relationships.

Width

Minimum
Graph one quadratic function y=ax²+bx+c using a table of values or digital tools; identify the turning point, axis of symmetry, and whether it opens upward or downward.
Typical
Graph a range of quadratic functions using digital tools; identify and interpret the turning point, axis of symmetry, x-intercepts (if they exist), y-intercept, maximum and minimum values; determine when values of the function lie within a given range.
Wider
  • Graph percentages of illumination of moon phases and connect the shape to a quadratic model; discuss this in relation to First Nations Australians' understandings of moon phases
  • Investigate how changing the coefficient of x² affects the width and orientation of the parabola — connect to parameter variation in AC9M9A06
  • Explore projectile motion contexts (e.g. paths of thrown objects, water from a hose) and interpret turning point and intercepts physically

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents commonly conflate the x-coordinate of the turning point with the axis of symmetry (confusing a point with a line), or believe all parabolas cross the x-axis. Seeing a range of quadratics — including those with no real roots, those with a double root, and those opening downward — addresses all three misconceptions.
