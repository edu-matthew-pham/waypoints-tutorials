← AC9M10P02 Simulate conditional probability

Connecting simulation to real-world decision-making

AC9M10P02 n3 · waypoint 3 of 3 · Year 10 Maths · Probability

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify a real-world situation where probability simulation supports decision-making and explain what the simulation contributes that theoretical analysis cannot.

Success criteria

  • Student describes a real decision-making context where simulation is used and explains what the simulation provides
  • Student identifies at least one assumption in the simulation and explains how changing it would affect the result

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Interpreting simulated results.

Width

Minimum
Describe one real situation where simulation is used for decision-making; explain what problem the simulation is solving.
Typical
Identify at least two real contexts where probability simulation is used (supply/demand, insurance, disease modelling, queueing); explain what information the simulation provides; discuss what assumptions are built into the simulation and how those assumptions affect its reliability.
Wider
  • Investigate the use of Monte Carlo simulation in finance or engineering and discuss how repeated random sampling builds an approximate probability distribution for an otherwise analytically intractable problem
  • Analyse a disease spread simulation (e.g. flu or virus infection rate modelling) and discuss the ethical responsibilities involved in communicating probabilistic predictions to the public

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Integration: a combining task at the wider tierConnecting simulation to real-world decision-making integrates simulation design (node 1), result interpretation (node 2), and contextual understanding of why simulation is used. It is the node where probability simulation becomes a reasoning tool rather than a procedural exercise.
