LCM and GCD from prime factorisations

AC9M7N02 n3 · waypoint 3 of 3 · Year 7 Maths · Number · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students use prime factorisations to find the lowest common multiple and greatest common divisor of pairs of natural numbers.

Lessons on this site

  • HCF and LCM

Success criteria

  • Correctly finds LCM and GCD for a given pair using prime factorisations.
  • Can explain why the method works (not just apply it).
  • Applies LCM or GCD appropriately when presented with a contextual problem.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Prime factorisation via factor trees.

Width

Minimum
Find the LCM and GCD of two numbers using their prime factorisations.
Typical
Apply the LCM and GCD in context: e.g. find when two cyclic events coincide (LCM), or find the largest equal shares (GCD).
Wider
  • Explore the relationship LCM(a,b) × GCD(a,b) = a × b and verify for several pairs.
  • Apply LCM to scheduling problems: if two buses leave every 12 and 18 minutes, when do they next leave together?

Hinge

Students who cannot apply the systematic LCM/GCD algorithm from prime factorisations revert to listing methods — this blocks the connection to AC9M7N04 fraction simplification and AC9M7N06 common denominator work where the prime factorisation method is the efficient path.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe algorithm (take highest power of each prime for LCM; lowest power for GCD) requires an explicit systematic scaffold. Students who proceed by listing multiples/factors miss the prime factorisation connection.
Integration: a combining task at the wider tierLCM and GCD require coordinating prime factorisation, exponent comparison, and contextual interpretation simultaneously.
