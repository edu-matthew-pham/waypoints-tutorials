Formulating problems as number sentences

AC9M3N06 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 3 Maths · Number · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can translate a practical additive or multiplicative situation into a number sentence, identifying the operation needed.

Success criteria

  • Students can write a number sentence that correctly represents a practical problem.
  • Students can identify which operation the problem requires and justify the choice.

Prior knowledge

Students can use mathematical modelling to solve practical additive and multiplicative problems, including money (AC9M2N06). They have not yet systematically formulated problems as number sentences for three-digit calculations, or applied modelling across both additive and multiplicative situations with explicit interpretation.

Width

Minimum
Translate one word problem (additive) and one word problem (multiplicative) into number sentences, identifying the operation and unknown.
Typical
Formulate a range of practical problems as number sentences, selecting the appropriate operation; include problems with an unknown in different positions.
Wider
  • Formulate problems involving two-step operations (e.g. buy three items, pay with $20, how much change) — bridging to multi-step modelling
  • Explore how the same real-world situation can be formulated differently depending on what question is asked

Hinge

Correct problem formulation is the entry gate to the modelling cycle — students who cannot identify the operation from a practical situation cannot progress to Node 2 (strategy selection) or Node 3 (interpretation). An incorrectly formulated number sentence invalidates the entire calculation and interpretation that follows.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents often confuse problem type when surface features change (e.g. money vs distance vs quantities). Encountering formulation across varied contexts builds the generalised skill of identifying the underlying operation.
