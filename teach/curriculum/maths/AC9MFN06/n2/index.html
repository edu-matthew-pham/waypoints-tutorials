← AC9MFN06 Equal sharing and grouping

Representing equal sharing situations

AC9MFN06 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Foundation Maths · Number · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can represent a practical equal sharing situation using materials and find how many each person receives using counting or subitising.

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Student distributes a collection of up to 10 objects equally between 2–3 people using a systematic strategy
  • Student counts or subitises each group to confirm equal distribution and states how many each person received

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: The idea of equal.

Width

Minimum
Represent a sharing situation using materials (distribute a collection of up to 10 between 2 or 3 people) and count each group to find how many each person receives.
Typical
Represent equal sharing situations across a range of practical contexts (fruit, cards, coins, beads); distribute collections of up to 10 objects between 2–4 people; use counting or subitising to determine the size of each equal group; confirm equality by comparing groups.
Wider
  • Explore instructive games of First Nations Australians involving equal sharing — e.g. Yangamini of the Tiwi Peoples of Bathurst Island — investigating what makes sharing equal in a game context
  • Represent equal sharing using virtual materials or digital tools, comparing the process with physical sharing

Hinge

Node 3 (grouping contexts) requires students to form equal groups systematically — the same systematic allocation process as sharing. Students who cannot reliably execute the one-at-a-time distribution strategy cannot form equal groups of a specified size.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Consolidation: more practise steps, short feedback loopsThe distribution process (one for you, one for me, one for you...) requires systematic allocation and tracking. Students commonly lose track or distribute unevenly. Repeated practice with the one-at-a-time distribution strategy builds the procedural fluency needed.
