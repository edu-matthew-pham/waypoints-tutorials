← AC9M7N01 Perfect squares and square roots

Square root as inverse operation

AC9M7N01 n2 · waypoint 2 of 4 · Year 7 Maths · Number · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students understand that square root is the inverse of squaring and can find the exact square root of any perfect square up to 144.

Lessons on this site

  • Squares and square roots

Success criteria

  • States the square root of any perfect square from 1 to 144 when prompted.
  • Explains in words what √25 means ('the number that when multiplied by itself gives 25').
  • Does not conflate squaring with multiplying by 2.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Squares as area models.

Width

Minimum
Find the square root of any perfect square from 1 to 144 and explain what it means.
Typical
Use √ notation fluently, explain the inverse relationship, and verify: √(n²) = n for concrete examples.
Wider
  • Explore perfect squares beyond 144 (e.g. 169, 196, 225) using the area model and extend the pattern.
  • Investigate √(a × b) = √a × √b for perfect square cases (e.g. √36 = √4 × √9), building intuition without formalising the rule.

Hinge

The squaring/doubling and √/halving misconceptions, if unresolved, produce systematic errors in Node 3 (bounding non-perfect squares) and Node 4 (applied problems) — students cannot reason about intervals or side lengths if the inverse relationship is confused.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents commonly confuse squaring with doubling (thinking 5² = 10) or treat √ as halving. Multiple concrete representations — area diagrams, calculator verification, number line — help resolve this.
