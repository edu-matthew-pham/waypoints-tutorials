Scale factor relationships for length, area and volume

AC9M9SP02 n2 · waypoint 2 of 2 · Year 9 Maths · Space · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can generalise the relationships between scale factor k and the scaling of length (×k), area (×k²) and volume (×k³).

Lessons on this site

  • Scale factors for area and volume

Success criteria

  • States and applies the correct scaling relationships: lengths ×k, areas ×k², volumes ×k³
  • Verifies the area scaling relationship (×k²) for at least two different scale factors
  • Explains in their own words why area scales as k² (two dimensions each scaled by k)

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Invariant and changing properties under enlargement.

Width

Minimum
Calculate the area of a shape and its image after enlargement by scale factor k for at least two values of k; identify that area scales by k².
Typical
Investigate and generalise the relationships: lengths scale by k, areas scale by k², volumes scale by k³; verify using numerical examples for whole and rational scale factors; explain why the exponent increases for each dimension.
Wider
  • Investigate biological scaling — why animals of different sizes have proportionally different limb thicknesses (surface area grows as k², volume as k³, requiring thicker bones)
  • Explore how the k² area relationship applies to similar figures beyond squares and cubes (e.g. similar triangles, similar irregular polygons)
  • Investigate how the k³ volume relationship applies to 3D objects (e.g. scale models of buildings or aircraft) and what this means for material use

Hinge

Applying k rather than k² for area (and k rather than k³ for volume) is a near-universal error. Students who carry it cannot correctly solve any problem in AC9M9M03 or AC9M9SP03 that involves scaled areas or volumes.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents consistently apply k rather than k² when asked about areas of enlarged figures (and k rather than k³ for volumes). The misconception is resistant because k feels intuitively correct. Multiple examples across different shapes and scale factors — verifying k² each time — is more effective than re-explaining the rule.
