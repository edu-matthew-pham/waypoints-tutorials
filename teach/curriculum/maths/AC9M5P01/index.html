Equally and unequally likely outcomes

AC9M5P01 · Year 5 Maths · Probability

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Descriptor

List the possible outcomes of chance experiments involving equally likely outcomes and compare to those which are not equally likely

Prior knowledge

Students can conduct repeated chance experiments and record results (AC9M4P02). They have not yet systematically listed all possible outcomes or compared equally and unequally likely situations.

By the end

Students list the possible outcomes of chance experiments and compare equally likely with unequally likely outcomes.

ACARA elaborations

Examples from ACARA that illustrate this standard. They are examples, not a checklist, and belong to the standard as a whole.

  • discussing what it means for outcomes to be equally likely and comparing the number of possible and equally likely outcomes of chance events; for example, when drawing a card from a standard deck of cards there are 4 4 possible outcomes if you are interested in the suit, 2 2 possible outcomes if you are interested in the colour or 52 52 outcomes if you are interested in the exact card
  • discussing how chance experiments that have equally likely outcomes can be referred to as random chance events; for example, if all the names of students in a class are placed in a hat and one is drawn at random, each person has an equally likely chance of being drawn
  • investigating how bias and fairness can relate to outcomes being equally and not equally likely, and discussing how this might inform strategies for mitigating bias in AI systems
  • commenting on the chance of winning games by considering the number of possible outcomes and the consequent chance of winning
  • investigating why some games are fair and others are not; for example, drawing a track game to resemble a running race and taking it in turns to roll 2 2 dice, where the first runner moves a square if the difference between the 2 2 dice is zero, one or 2 2 and the second runner moves a square if the difference is 3 , 4 3,4 or 5 5; responding to the questions, “Is this game fair?”, “Are some differences more likely to come up than others?” and “How can you work that out?”
  • comparing the chance of a head or a tail when a coin is tossed, whether some numbers on a dice are more likely to be facing up when the dice is rolled, or the chance of getting a 1 , 2 1,2 or 3 3 on a spinner with uneven regions for the numbers
  • discussing supermarket promotions such as collecting stickers or objects and whether there is an equal chance of getting each of them

Waypoints

  • 1. Sample spaceHingeStudents can systematically list all possible outcomes of a chance experiment.
  • 2. Equally likely outcomesStudents can identify when outcomes are equally likely and use this to describe the probability of each.
  • 3. Comparing equally and unequally likely outcomesStudents can compare experiments with equally likely outcomes to those with unequally likely outcomes and explain the difference.
