Defining and decomposing spatial problems

AC9M10SP03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 10 Maths · Space · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can precisely define a spatial problem and decompose it into sub-problems suitable for algorithmic solution.

Success criteria

  • Student states a spatial problem with precise inputs, constraints and success criteria
  • Student identifies at least two sub-problems and explains how they combine to solve the full problem

Prior knowledge

Students can design, test and refine algorithms based on geometric constructions and theorems (AC9M9SP03). They have not yet applied this capability to the full range of spatial problem types at Y10 — scale models, floor plan optimisation, network routing, and self-similar patterns — or communicated and justified solutions at this level.

Width

Minimum
Define a spatial problem in precise terms (specifying inputs, constraints, and desired output); identify at least two sub-problems that together constitute the full problem.
Typical
Define and decompose spatial problems across different types (floor plan optimisation, network routing, scale model design); identify which decomposition makes the problem most tractable; specify all constraints and success criteria before designing the algorithm.
Wider
  • Explore how geospatial technologies used by First Nations Australians' communities decompose spatial problems of position and transformation — and compare these problem-solving approaches to computational decomposition methods
  • Investigate how floor plan problems (e.g. minimising congestion at key areas in a department store) can be formally specified as optimisation problems with defined constraints

Hinge

Students who cannot precisely define a spatial problem before designing an algorithm produce solutions that do not match the actual constraints — N2 algorithm design and N3 testing are both invalidated by an underspecified problem definition.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkProblem decomposition requires systematic thinking — students who jump to algorithm design before the problem is precisely defined frequently produce solutions that do not match the actual constraints. Explicit problem specification before algorithm design needs scaffolding.
