Dividing decimals by powers of 10

AC9M6N06 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Year 6 Maths · Number · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can divide decimal numbers by 10, 100 and 1000 by shifting digits to the right, and explain the effect in terms of place value.

Success criteria

  • Student correctly divides a decimal number by 10, 100 and 1000.
  • Student explains the relationship between multiplying and dividing by the same power of 10 as inverse operations.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Multiplying decimals by powers of 10.

Width

Minimum
Divide at least five decimal numbers by 10, 100 and 1000; explain what happens to each digit's place value; recognise that dividing by 10 shifts digits one place to the right.
Typical
Divide decimals by 10, 100, 1000 and multiples; apply to unit conversion in context (centimetres to metres, grams to kilograms); connect division by powers of 10 to the inverse of multiplication; apply to price-per-unit calculations.
Wider
  • Investigate patterns: what is the result of 5.2 × 10 ÷ 10? What about 5.2 × 100 ÷ 1000? Generalise the pattern.
  • Apply powers-of-10 division to interpret decimal results from division of whole numbers (e.g. 750 ÷ 1000 = 0.75 kg)

Hinge

Understanding division as the inverse place-value shift is required for Node 3 (multiples of powers of 10), where students must decompose multipliers and apply both operations. Students who cannot reliably shift right for division will produce errors in all subsequent metric conversion and ratio problems.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe inverse relationship between multiplying and dividing by powers of 10 requires explicit reasoning — students need to see why 'shifting right' is the inverse of 'shifting left' and how this produces decimals from whole numbers. Without this scaffold, division errors are difficult to self-correct.
