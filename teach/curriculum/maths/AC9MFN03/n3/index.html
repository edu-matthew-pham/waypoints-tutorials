← AC9MFN03 Quantifying and comparing collections

Comparing collections using counting

AC9MFN03 n3 · waypoint 3 of 3 · Foundation Maths · Number

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can compare the size of two or more collections using counting and justify which has more or fewer.

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Student counts two collections of up to 20 objects accurately and correctly states which has more
  • Student explains reasoning — naming the counts of both collections and using them to justify the comparison

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Counting for a purpose.

Width

Minimum
Count two collections and compare them, stating which has more and how they know.
Typical
Count and compare collections of up to 20 objects; justify which collection has more or fewer using the count; apply comparison to practical situations (e.g. 'Do we have enough scissors for everyone?').
Wider
  • Compare three or more collections, ordering them from fewest to most and justifying the order with counts
  • Investigate comparison situations where counting is needed because subitising is not reliable (collections greater than 5) — connecting comparison strategy to quantity size
  • Explore how different cultures represent and compare quantities — connecting to the universality of counting as a comparison tool

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly compare collections by visual impression (which pile looks bigger) rather than counting. The reasoning scaffold here is: count each collection, then compare the counts. Getting students to explicitly articulate this two-step process — count first, then compare — is the key teaching move at this node.
Integration: a combining task at the wider tierComparison integrates counting principles (node 1) and purposeful counting (node 2) into a reasoning task. Students must count accurately AND use the counts to draw a justified conclusion about relative size. This coordination is the endpoint demand.
