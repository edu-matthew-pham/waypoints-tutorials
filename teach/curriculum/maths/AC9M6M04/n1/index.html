Angles on a straight line

AC9M6M04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 6 Maths · Measurement · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify that angles on a straight line sum to 180° and use this to find an unknown angle.

Lessons on this site

  • Angles on a line and at a point

Success criteria

  • Student states the angle relationship for angles on a straight line.
  • Student correctly finds an unknown angle on a straight line and explains the reasoning.

Prior knowledge

Students can estimate, construct and measure angles in degrees using a protractor (AC9M5M04). They understand acute, obtuse, straight, and reflex angles and can measure them. They have not yet identified geometric relationships between angles at a point or on a line, or used these to determine unknown angles.

Width

Minimum
Identify angles on a straight line; state that they sum to 180°; calculate at least four unknown angles using the straight-line relationship; verify using measurement.
Typical
Apply the straight-line relationship to diagrams with two or three angles on a line; explain why the angles sum to 180° (a straight line is a half-rotation); apply to problem contexts involving straight line segments in diagrams.
Wider
  • Investigate supplementary angles in different geometric figures — in a triangle, in a parallelogram — and identify where the straight-line relationship appears
  • Explore why the straight-line angle sum is 180° using rotation reasoning: a full rotation is 360°, a straight line is half

Hinge

The straight-line relationship is used as a step within the vertically-opposite proof (Node 3) and within all Node 4 multi-step problems. Students who apply 180° as a rote rule without understanding the rotation basis will be unable to identify when and why to apply it in complex diagrams.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents often apply the 180° rule without understanding why it holds. The rotation-based justification (straight angle = half turn = 180°) needs explicit scaffolding before students can communicate reasoning rather than just state the rule.
