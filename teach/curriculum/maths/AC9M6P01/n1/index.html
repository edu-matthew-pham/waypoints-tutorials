The probability scale

AC9M6P01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 6 Maths · Probability · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can place events on a 0–1 probability scale and connect impossible (0), certain (1), and unlikely/likely/even chance positions to their numerical values.

Success criteria

  • Student places at least three events at appropriate positions on a 0–1 probability scale with justification.
  • Student explains what 0, 1, and 0.5 represent on the scale.

Prior knowledge

Students can list outcomes of chance experiments and compare equally and unequally likely outcomes (AC9M5P01). They can estimate likelihoods from frequency (AC9M5P02). They have not yet connected likelihood to the 0–1 numerical scale or used fractions, decimals, and percentages as probability representations.

Width

Minimum
Place at least six events on a 0–1 probability number line; explain what 0, 1, and ½ represent; identify events at approximately ¼, ½, and ¾ probability.
Typical
Position events at a range of probability values on the 0–1 scale; connect the scale to 0–100% and 0–1 decimal forms; explain why probability cannot be less than 0 or greater than 1; compare qualitative probability language (unlikely, likely) to approximate numerical values.
Wider
  • Investigate sports or weather forecasting contexts that use probability — what does '70% chance of rain' mean on the 0–1 scale?
  • Compare different cultures' ways of expressing likelihood — how do different languages describe probability? Is there a universal understanding of 'likely'?

Hinge

The 0–1 scale is the representational foundation for Nodes 2 and 3. Students who cannot anchor impossible/certain/even chance to specific numerical values cannot place estimated probabilities meaningfully or verify that outcome probabilities sum to 1.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents who understand likelihood qualitatively often struggle to connect to the 0–1 numerical scale. The reasoning chain — impossible = nothing out of nothing = 0, certain = everything = 1, even chance = half = 0.5 = 50% — needs explicit scaffolding before the scale becomes a useful tool.
