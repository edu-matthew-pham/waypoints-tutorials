Negative integer exponents

AC9M9A01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 9 Maths · Algebra · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can evaluate expressions with negative integer exponents and explain why a negative exponent produces a reciprocal.

Lessons on this site

  • Negative powers

Success criteria

  • Correctly evaluates at least two expressions with negative integer exponents as fractions
  • Explains using a pattern or sequence why a negative exponent produces a reciprocal
  • Does not confuse a negative exponent with a negative result

Prior knowledge

Students can establish and apply exponent laws with positive integer exponents and the zero exponent using numbers (AC9M8N02). They have not yet applied exponent laws to negative integer exponents, or extended the laws systematically to algebraic expressions involving variables.

Width

Minimum
Evaluate at least two expressions with negative integer exponents (e.g. 5⁻³, 2⁻¹); explain using a sequence pattern (e.g. 5³, 5², 5¹, 5⁰, 5⁻¹…) why 5⁻³ = 1/5³.
Typical
Evaluate a range of expressions with positive and negative integer exponents; explain the pattern that connects positive and negative exponents using division-by-base sequences; connect decimal representations to negative powers of 10 (e.g. 0.01 = 10⁻²).
Wider
  • Represent decimals in expanded exponential form (e.g. 0.475 = 4×10⁻¹ + 7×10⁻² + 5×10⁻³) and connect to place value
  • Connect negative exponents to SI unit prefixes (e.g. milli- = 10⁻³, micro- = 10⁻⁶, nano- = 10⁻⁹) and explore scientific measurement contexts
  • Investigate how negative exponents appear in compound interest formulas and other financial mathematics contexts

Hinge

Students who read negative exponents as negative numbers produce systematically wrong results in every exponent law application in N2 and N3. This misconception is invisible to students — they produce confident wrong answers.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents commonly interpret a negative exponent as a negative number (reading 5⁻³ as −125 or −1/125). Seeing the pattern through multiple bases — with the sequence 5², 5¹, 5⁰, 5⁻¹, 5⁻² making the reciprocal structure visible — resolves the misconception more reliably than restating the rule.
