One-half as equal parts

AC9M2N03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 2 Maths · Number · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify and create one-half of a shape, object or collection, explaining that both parts must be equal.

Success criteria

  • Creates or identifies one-half of a shape, object or collection with equal parts
  • Explains that both halves must be equal — not just two parts

Prior knowledge

Students have informally explored equal sharing (AC9M1N06). They have not yet named halves, quarters or eighths as unit fractions, or connected them through the structure of repeated halving.

Width

Minimum
Share a collection or fold a shape into 2 equal parts; identify one part as one-half; confirm equality of the two parts.
Typical
Create halves of shapes, objects and collections using different methods (folding, measuring, sharing); explain 'a half is one part out of 2 equal parts of a whole'; compare halves of different-sized wholes.
Wider
  • Compare half of a set of 12 counters with half of a set of 8 counters — discuss how both are one-half even though they are different amounts
  • Create halves using measurement contexts: half of a strip of paper, half of a cup of water; explain what 'equal' means in each case

Hinge

Nodes 2 and 3 depend on halving — creating quarters by halving a half, and eighths by halving a quarter. Students who accept unequal parts as halves will produce incorrect quarters and eighths, and the repeated-halving structure breaks completely.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents commonly confuse half with 'one of two parts' without understanding the equal-parts constraint, and conflate the size of the half with the concept of one-half (e.g. thinking half of 12 is bigger than half of 8, so it's 'more than a half'). Multiple contexts — shapes, objects and collections — help students see that the relationship (1 of 2 equal parts) is what defines one-half, not the size.
