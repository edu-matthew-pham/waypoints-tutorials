← AC9M6N02 Prime, composite and square numbers

Factors and the prime/composite distinction

AC9M6N02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 6 Maths · Number · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can determine the number of factors a whole number has and classify it as prime (exactly two factors: 1 and itself) or composite (more than two factors).

Success criteria

  • Student correctly identifies all factors of a given number to 100 and classifies it as prime or composite.
  • Student explains why 1 is neither prime nor composite and why 2 is the only even prime.

Prior knowledge

Students can express natural numbers as products of their factors and determine if one number is divisible by another (AC9M5N02). They can recognise multiples. They have not yet classified numbers as prime or composite, nor explored square numbers as a distinct number class.

Width

Minimum
Find all factors of numbers to at least 30; classify each as prime or composite based on factor count; explain why 1 is neither prime nor composite.
Typical
Find all factors of numbers to 100; classify numbers as prime or composite with justification; identify all primes to 50 using factor analysis; explain why 2 is the only even prime.
Wider
  • Use the Sieve of Eratosthenes to systematically identify all primes to 100 and describe the pattern of what remains
  • Investigate the distribution of primes — why do primes thin out as numbers get larger? Is there a largest prime?
  • Explore twin primes (pairs differing by 2) and identify examples up to 100

Hinge

Correct classification of numbers as prime or composite is a prerequisite for Node 2 (prime factorisation). Students with misconceptions about primes — odd = prime, or 1 is prime — produce systematically incorrect factor trees that cannot be corrected without first fixing the classification.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents commonly confuse 'has no factors' with 'prime' or think odd numbers are automatically prime. Varied examples — including borderline cases (1, 2, 4, 9, 25) and testing larger numbers — expose and resolve these misconceptions more reliably than repeated practice on a narrow range.
