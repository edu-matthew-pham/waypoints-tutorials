← AC9M6N02 Prime, composite and square numbers

Square numbers and their properties

AC9M6N02 n3 · waypoint 3 of 4 · Year 6 Maths · Number

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify square numbers, recognise them as products of a number multiplied by itself, connect them to square arrays, and identify square roots of familiar square numbers.

Success criteria

  • Student correctly identifies whether a given number is a square number and explains why using an array or multiplication.
  • Student states the square root of familiar square numbers up to 144.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Prime factorisation.

Width

Minimum
List the first 12 square numbers; explain what makes a number 'square' using array or area models; identify the square root of familiar square numbers (1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, 121, 144).
Typical
Identify and generate square numbers; connect square numbers to square arrays and the area of squares; identify that square numbers have an odd number of factors (unique property); use square number knowledge to estimate and check calculations.
Wider
  • Investigate triangular numbers (1, 3, 6, 10...) as a parallel number class and compare their visual patterns with square numbers
  • Explore the sum of consecutive odd numbers pattern (1=1², 1+3=2², 1+3+5=3²...) and explain why it works using array diagrams

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents often treat square numbers as a list to memorise rather than a property to understand. Array and area model representations, alongside procedural practice, build both the conceptual understanding and the recall needed for problem-solving use.
