← AC9M10M02 Logarithmic scales

Order of magnitude and the logarithmic idea

AC9M10M02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 10 Maths · Measurement · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain that a logarithmic scale is calibrated in orders of magnitude and describe how equal intervals on a log scale represent multiplicative (not additive) steps.

Lessons on this site

  • How big is a step?

Success criteria

  • Student correctly explains what equal intervals represent on a logarithmic scale (multiplicative, not additive steps)
  • Student reads a value from a base-10 log scale and expresses it as a power of 10

Prior knowledge

Students can use mathematical modelling to solve proportion, rate, ratio and scale problems (AC9M9M05). They have not yet encountered logarithmic scales or the concept of order-of-magnitude calibration.

Width

Minimum
Explain that each step on a logarithmic scale represents a ×10 (or ×2, depending on base) change rather than a fixed additive amount; contrast this with a linear scale.
Typical
Explain the order-of-magnitude calibration of logarithmic scales; read values from a base-10 log scale; convert between linear and log representations of the same value; explain why 10² and 10³ are one 'step' apart on a log scale despite being 900 apart on a linear scale.
Wider
  • Investigate how the choice of base (base 2, base 10, natural log) changes the scale and appears in different scientific contexts
  • Explore how scientific notation and logarithmic scales express the same underlying idea — that very large or very small numbers are more usefully described by their order of magnitude

Hinge

The multiplicative (not additive) calibration of log scales is the foundational concept for all subsequent interpretation work — students who do not grasp this cannot meaningfully read log scale values (N2) or compare quantities across orders of magnitude (N3).

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe multiplicative nature of log scales is conceptually unfamiliar — students arrive expecting all scales to be linear. The reasoning chain (each step multiplies rather than adds; equal visual distance = equal ratio) needs explicit scaffolding.
