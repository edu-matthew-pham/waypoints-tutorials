Effect of approximation in repeated calculations

AC9M10N01 n3 · waypoint 3 of 3 · Year 10 Maths · Number

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can demonstrate, using a worked example, that repeated use of an approximate value produces a different final result than using the exact representation, and describe the nature of the accumulation.

Lessons on this site

  • Rounding over and over

Success criteria

  • Student produces a worked example showing the numerical difference between approximate and exact calculations across repeated steps
  • Student explains why the difference grows with repetition rather than staying constant

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Truncation vs rounding.

Width

Minimum
Calculate a result using an approximate value repeated across at least two steps and compare it to the result using the exact value; describe the difference.
Typical
Investigate the effect of approximation in at least two repeated-calculation contexts (e.g. repeated simple interest steps, perimeter of a compound shape involving π, volume of a sphere used in a multi-step problem); quantify the accumulated error; discuss whether the accumulated error matters for the application.
Wider
  • Investigate approximation error accumulation in contexts involving compound shapes with circles — e.g. using π ≈ 3.14 vs exact π across multiple component areas and comparing the cumulative difference
  • Limited width note: this descriptor has genuinely thin context breadth at Y10 — the core concept (approximation error accumulates in repeated calculation) does not vary dramatically across phenomena. Width directions above extend the contexts modestly; depth of reasoning about when error matters is where the real enrichment lies.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents who only see approximation error in one context (e.g. rounding π) may not recognise the same accumulation pattern in financial or measurement contexts. Two or three context types stabilise the general principle.
