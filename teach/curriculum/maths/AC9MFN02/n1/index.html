Instant recognition to 3

AC9MFN02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Foundation Maths · Number · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can recognise and name collections of 1, 2 and 3 objects instantly, without counting.

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Student names a collection of 1, 2 or 3 objects immediately (without counting) on at least three consecutive occasions
  • Student can distinguish a subitised response from a counted response ('I just knew' vs 'I counted')

Prior knowledge

Students can count small collections of objects using one-to-one correspondence. They have not yet recognised quantities instantly without counting — subitising as a deliberate strategy is new.

Width

Minimum
Recognise collections of 1, 2 and 3 objects instantly in standard arrangements (e.g. dot cards, dice) and name the quantity without counting.
Typical
Recognise and name collections of 1, 2 and 3 in a range of arrangements (different dot card layouts, small groups of objects); use subitising to identify quantities in quick-look games.
Wider
  • Recognise collections of 1, 2 and 3 in non-standard arrangements and in familiar objects (e.g. 3 buttons on a shirt, 2 eyes)
  • Compare two collections of 1–3 objects using subitising — stating which has more without counting

Hinge

Reliable instant recognition of 1–3 is the perceptual base for extending subitising to 4 and 5 in node 2. Students who count rather than subitise for 1–3 do not develop the perceptual number sense needed to see 4 as '3 and 1 more' or 5 as '3 and 2' — structured subitising at node 2 depends on fluency at this node.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Consolidation: more practise steps, short feedback loopsInstant recognition requires fluency built through repeated exposure to quick-look activities. Students need practice with many examples across arrangements to build reliable perceptual number sense for 1–3 before extending to 4 and 5.
