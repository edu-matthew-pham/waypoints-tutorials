← AC9M1N05 Model additive problems

Represent with diagrams and materials

AC9M1N05 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Year 1 Maths · Number · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can represent an additive situation using at least two different representations (e.g. diagram, materials, Think Board) and explain the connections between them.

Success criteria

  • Produces a diagram or materials representation that correctly captures the quantities in the story problem.
  • Explains the connection between the representation and at least one element of the story (e.g. 'these blocks are the passengers who got on').

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Interpret the additive situation.

Width

Minimum
Represent one additive problem using physical materials or a diagram; connect the representation to the numbers in the story.
Typical
Use Think Boards to represent additive problems with pictures, materials, diagrams and number sentences; explain the connections between each representation and the story; include simple money transactions using whole dollar amounts.
Wider
  • Represent the same problem using three different formats (materials, number line, part-part-whole diagram) and compare which representation makes the solution most visible.
  • Create a class display linking physical representations, diagrams and number sentences for a set of related additive problems.

Hinge

The ability to represent a problem situation is a prerequisite for Node 3 — students who cannot connect a story to a representation cannot verify whether their calculation answers the right question or interpret the answer back in context.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkMoving from a concrete material representation to a diagram to a number sentence is a three-step abstraction that requires explicit scaffolding. Students need the connections between each level made visible before they can coordinate them independently.
