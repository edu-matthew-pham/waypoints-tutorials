Scale factors and conversion

AC9M10M05 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 10 Maths · Measurement · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can determine the scale factor between a model and the actual object, and use it to convert measurements in both directions.

Success criteria

  • Student correctly calculates a scale factor from given measurements and applies it to find an unknown dimension
  • Student explains why area and volume scale differently from linear dimensions

Prior knowledge

Students can identify how measurement errors affect accuracy of results in practical contexts (AC9M10M04). They have not yet used mathematical modelling as a full cycle (formulate, solve, interpret, evaluate, modify) specifically for proportion and scaling problems.

Width

Minimum
Calculate the scale factor given one model measurement and one actual measurement; use the scale factor to find an unknown actual or model dimension.
Typical
Determine scale factors from plans, drawings or physical models; convert dimensions in both directions (model → actual, actual → model); apply scale factors to areas (scale factor squared) and volumes (scale factor cubed); check scale consistency across a drawing.
Wider
  • Estimate the scale of an unfamiliar object (e.g. a toy car, a model building) by measuring a recognisable linear dimension and comparing to a known typical value
  • Investigate how scale factors apply differently to lengths, areas and volumes — and why a model that is 1:10 in length has a volume ratio of 1:1000

Hinge

Scale factor errors cascade through N2 and N3 — students who cannot correctly derive and apply scale factors will misread plans (N2) and formulate incorrect proportion models (N3), invalidating all downstream work.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe scaling of areas and volumes (squared and cubed scale factors) is a non-obvious extension of linear scale that students frequently miss. Explicit reasoning about why area scales by k² and volume by k³ prevents rote misapplication.
