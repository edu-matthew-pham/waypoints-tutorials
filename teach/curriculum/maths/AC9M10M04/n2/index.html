Impact of individual measurement errors

AC9M10M04 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Year 10 Maths · Measurement · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can describe how a measurement error propagates into a calculated result and estimate the size of the impact.

Success criteria

  • Student correctly calculates the range of values for a derived quantity given a measurement error margin
  • Student explains why the impact of a measurement error is larger for some calculations than others

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Sources and types of measurement error.

Width

Minimum
Given a measurement with a stated error margin, calculate how that error affects a derived result (e.g. if length is off by 1 cm, how does area change).
Typical
Calculate the effect of measurement error on derived quantities (area, volume, interest, rate); describe how the impact grows as the formula becomes more complex; use bounds (max/min values from error margins) to state ranges for calculated results.
Wider
  • Investigate how truncation versus rounding errors affect the accuracy of financial calculations at scale — e.g. the impact of truncating cent values across millions of customer records
  • Explore how measurement accuracy in dating methods (e.g. radiocarbon dating) determines the precision of age estimates for historical artefacts and discuss what this means for archaeological claims

Hinge

Understanding how individual errors propagate into calculated results is the prerequisite for N3 — students who cannot trace a single error's effect cannot reason about how errors accumulate across iterations.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe propagation of error through a formula requires systematic reasoning — students need a process (calculate with maximum, minimum, and best-estimate values; compare results) rather than intuitive guessing. Explicit scaffolding prevents underestimation of impact.
