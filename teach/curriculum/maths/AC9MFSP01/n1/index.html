Naming and recognising familiar shapes

AC9MFSP01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Foundation Maths · Space · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Maths: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can name familiar shapes (circle, triangle, rectangle, square) and recognise them in different sizes and orientations.

Success criteria

  • Student correctly names at least three familiar shapes in varied sizes and orientations
  • Student identifies a named shape within at least two everyday objects in the environment

Prior knowledge

Students have informal experience with shapes in play and everyday objects. They may be able to identify some shapes by name (circle, square) in canonical orientations. They have not yet systematically sorted shapes by attributes or described shapes within objects in the environment.

Width

Minimum
Name at least three familiar shapes (circle, triangle, rectangle or square) and identify them in a collection that includes varied sizes and orientations.
Typical
Name circles, triangles, rectangles and squares in a range of sizes and orientations; recognise these shapes in everyday objects; connect shape names to their defining features (number of sides, curved vs straight).
Wider
  • Describe and name shapes observed on Country/Place — connecting shape recognition to the natural and built environment in First Nations contexts
  • Investigate shapes within familiar objects (bicycles, kitchen pantry items, toy vehicles) — identifying and naming the shapes they can see

Hinge

Node 3 requires students to identify shapes within environmental objects — which appear in arbitrary sizes and orientations, not canonical ones. Students whose recognition is prototype-based will fail to identify shapes embedded in objects or rotated in artworks, undermining the environmental description and reasoning the descriptor demands.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents commonly recognise shapes only in canonical orientations and sizes — a triangle must be upright and equilateral, a rectangle must be landscape. Encountering shapes in many sizes, orientations and colours (with colour, size and orientation explicitly varied) is what builds recognition based on defining features rather than prototype matching.
