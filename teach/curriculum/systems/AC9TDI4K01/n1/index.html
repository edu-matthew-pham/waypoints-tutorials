Peripherals as categories

AC9TDI4K01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 4 Digital Technologies (knowledge) · Digital systems · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Digital Technologies (knowledge): treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can sort peripherals into input, output and storage categories and give an example of each

Success criteria

  • Correctly places at least two peripherals in each of the three categories
  • Explains what input, output and storage each mean in plain language

Prior knowledge

Students can identify and explore digital systems and their components for a purpose (AC9TDI2K01). They can name hardware and software as components of digital systems. They have not yet explored the specific categories of peripherals (input, output, storage) or described how peripherals expand system functionality.

Width

Minimum
Name at least one example of an input peripheral, one output peripheral, and one storage peripheral; explain the job each category performs
Typical
Classify a range of familiar peripherals (keyboard, mouse, microphone, camera; monitor, printer, speaker; USB drive) into input, output and storage categories and explain the function of each
Wider
  • Investigate how peripherals used in First Nations community contexts (e.g. GPS devices used by rangers, shared devices in remote schools) are classified and what purposes they serve
  • Explore how a touchscreen device combines input and output in a single peripheral — discuss why this blurs the category boundaries

Hinge

Category confusion between input and output (treating the monitor as 'the computer') propagates into N2 and N3 — students who cannot classify peripherals by data-flow direction cannot reason about what adding a peripheral enables or which peripheral suits a purpose.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents commonly conflate input and output or treat the monitor as 'the computer'. More examples across varied peripheral types help students see what defines the category (direction of data flow) rather than memorising a fixed list.
