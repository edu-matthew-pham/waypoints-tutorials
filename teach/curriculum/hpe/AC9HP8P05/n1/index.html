Understanding diversity and its value

AC9HP8P05 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 7–8 Health and Physical Education · Personal, social and community health

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Health and Physical Education: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain what diversity means in community contexts, identify key dimensions of diversity relevant to their own community, and describe why valuing diversity matters for health and wellbeing.

Success criteria

  • Explains what diversity means in a community context
  • Identifies at least one dimension of diversity and explains why valuing it matters for health and wellbeing

Prior knowledge

Students have described and implemented strategies to value diversity in their communities (Y5-6). They can describe and implement strategies but have not yet been asked to investigate the broader mechanisms by which communities come to value diversity, or to propose community-level actions.

Width

Minimum
Explain what diversity means and describe at least one dimension of diversity in their community, giving one reason why valuing it matters for health and wellbeing.
Typical
Identify and describe diversity across multiple dimensions (gender, race, sexuality, ability, cultural background); explain how valuing diversity contributes to individual and community health and wellbeing; examine how stereotypes, prejudice and discrimination undermine community health.
Wider
  • Investigate how beliefs and cultural practices within, between and across cultural groups — including groups from the Asian region — have changed over time, and what has driven those changes
  • Examine how the concept of intersectionality (overlapping dimensions of diversity) affects how different individuals experience inclusion and exclusion

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepDiversity concepts become meaningful when grounded in multiple dimensions — students who only examine one type of diversity have a narrow basis for investigating community strategies.
