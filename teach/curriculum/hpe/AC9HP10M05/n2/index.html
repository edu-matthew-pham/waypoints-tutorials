Designing participation strategies for others

AC9HP10M05 n2 · waypoint 2 of 2 · Years 9–10 Health and Physical Education · Movement and physical activity

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Health and Physical Education: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can design and evaluate participation strategies for physical activities that promote health and social outcomes for themselves and others

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Designs a strategy for a named target group — not a generic strategy
  • Strategy promotes both health and social outcomes — addresses both, not only one
  • Evaluation addresses whether the strategy is realistic and accessible for the target group

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Physical activity for social outcomes.

Width

Minimum
Design one strategy for increasing participation in a physical activity with health and social benefits; explain who it is designed for and why it would be effective for that group
Typical
Create and evaluate proposals to promote use of natural settings for physical activity; design strategies involving family, friends and community in culturally significant physical activities that promote connection and belonging; evaluate participation strategies against health and social outcome goals
Wider
  • Design and evaluate a strategy to involve a specific community group (e.g. elderly residents, culturally and linguistically diverse community members) in a physical activity that promotes health and social connection — justify design choices for that specific group
  • Create a proposal for a community celebration based around culturally significant physical activities — evaluate how it would promote a sense of belonging for diverse community members
  • Investigate and evaluate a school-based physical activity participation strategy — identify what elements are working, what barriers exist, and propose specific improvements based on evidence

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly design participation strategies without evaluating whether they will work for the intended audience — designing a strategy for 'everyone' without considering what barriers different groups face. The structured reasoning investment is in making the design decisions explicit: who is this for, what barriers do they face, how does this strategy address those barriers, how will health and social outcomes be achieved.
