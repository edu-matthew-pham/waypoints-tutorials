The importance of asking permission

AC9HPFP04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Foundation Health and Physical Education · Personal, social and community health · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Health and Physical Education: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain why asking for permission matters when sharing possessions or personal space, and identify situations where permission is needed.

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least one situation where asking permission is required
  • Gives a simple reason why asking permission is important for respecting others

Prior knowledge

No prior HPE curriculum. Students have informal experience of asking and being asked to share. No prior structured curriculum exploration of consent, rights and respectful negotiation.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least one situation where they would need to ask permission before sharing or accessing something, and give a reason why asking is important.
Typical
Explore multiple situations requiring permission — sharing possessions, personal space, and activity roles; explain why asking matters for respect and rights; recognise the right to say no as part of respecting others.
Wider
  • Explore permission and consent in play contexts across different cultural settings, including First Nations Australian negotiation practices in games
  • Discuss digital contexts where permission is needed — e.g. taking or sharing a photo of someone
  • Connect body autonomy to permission — explaining that our bodies belong to us and others must ask before touching

Hinge

Without understanding why permission matters, practising how to ask becomes mechanical compliance. The Y1-2 equivalent (practising strategies) requires this conceptual foundation. Body autonomy connections are foundational to protective behaviours work in AC9HPFP05.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe concept of rights — including the right to say no — requires explicit, teacher-led discussion. Scenario-based exploration (what would you do if... ?) is more effective than rule-telling.
Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepPermission situations vary: possessions, personal space, digital contexts, bodily autonomy. Varied context discussion prevents students from associating permission only with object-sharing.
