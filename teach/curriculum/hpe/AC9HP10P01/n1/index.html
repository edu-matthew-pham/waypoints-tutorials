Societal and cultural factors on identity

AC9HP10P01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Years 9–10 Health and Physical Education · Personal, social and community health · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Health and Physical Education: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can analyse how societal norms, stereotypes and cultural practices shape how individuals see themselves

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two distinct factors (e.g. societal norms, cultural practices, stereotypes) that shape identity
  • Explains how each factor operates in a specific context with reference to evidence or examples
  • Does not conflate identity with personality — recognises identity as socially and culturally shaped

Prior knowledge

Students can examine how power, coercion and gender stereotypes influence roles and relationships (AC9HP8P03). They have not yet analysed the broader range of societal, cultural, and community factors that shape identity, nor evaluated how individuals actively influence each other's identities.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least two societal or cultural factors that influence identity and explain how each shapes self-perception in a specific context
Typical
Analyse how societal norms, stereotypes and cultural expectations influence how young people view themselves across multiple contexts (e.g. school, sport, online); evaluate which factors are most influential and why
Wider
  • Analyse how personal totems in First Nations Australian cultures contribute to individual identity, comparing this with how identity markers function in other cultural traditions
  • Investigate how cultural beliefs and practices surrounding transitions to adulthood differ across cultures, including cultures of Asia, and what this reveals about how identity is constructed differently across communities
  • Examine how societal norms and stereotypes specifically influence perceptions of movement competence, and how this shapes participation and identity in physical activity contexts

Hinge

Students who cannot analyse how external factors shape identity cannot meaningfully evaluate how individuals influence each other's identities in Node 2 — that evaluative move requires a working understanding of which forces are already at play before individual influence is layered on top.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepIdentity is shaped differently across contexts — what norms operate in sport differ from those online or within family. Students need multiple distinct contexts to develop the analytical skill rather than a single worked example. First Nations and Asian cultural contexts are not optional extras; they are substantive different frameworks for understanding how identity is constructed.
