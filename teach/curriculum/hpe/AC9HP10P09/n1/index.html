Criteria for critiquing health information

AC9HP10P09 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Years 9–10 Health and Physical Education · Personal, social and community health · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Health and Physical Education: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify criteria for evaluating the quality, bias and influence of health information and media messaging

Success criteria

  • Names at least two criteria for evaluating health information quality or bias
  • Applies criteria to a specific example — not just describes them in the abstract
  • Criteria include something beyond 'is from a reliable source' — e.g. considers whose interests are served, what is left out, who the audience is

Prior knowledge

Students can plan, rehearse and evaluate strategies for managing health and safety risk situations (AC9HP10P08), and have previously investigated how media and influential people impact health attitudes and behaviours (AC9HP8P09). At Y9-10 the focus shifts to critique — a higher-order evaluative move that assesses the validity, bias and influence of health information and media.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least two criteria for evaluating health information (e.g. source credibility, evidence base, whose interests are served) and apply them to one example
Typical
Develop criteria for critiquing health information and services; apply criteria to evaluate health information targeting specific groups of young people (substance use, healthy food choices, fitness plans, mental health support, sexual health, personal safety); evaluate strategies and actions to increase personal safety
Wider
  • Analyse the nutritional composition of foods eaten by First Peoples of Australia and evaluate how this information could be used to increase health benefits for everyone — assess how this knowledge has been presented in mainstream health media
  • Investigate health issues specific to First Nations Australian communities and critique mainstream health messaging for its relevance and accessibility — propose proactive community strategies for promoting better access and health outcomes
  • Critique health information targeting specific cultural or demographic groups (e.g. young men, LGBTIQ+ youth, culturally and linguistically diverse communities) — evaluate whether the information addresses actual barriers to health access

Hinge

Students who cannot identify criteria for evaluating health information cannot apply critique in Node 2 — critique requires a principled basis, not just personal opinion about whether information seems right.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly express opinions about health information without applying evaluative criteria — saying a source is unreliable without specifying why by what standard. The investment is in building the criteria explicitly first: what makes health information credible, what indicates bias, what does it mean to evaluate whose interests a message serves. These criteria then underpin the critique work in Node 2.
