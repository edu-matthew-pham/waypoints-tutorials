Understanding values and beliefs as identity influences

AC9HP8P01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 7–8 Health and Physical Education · Personal, social and community health · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Health and Physical Education: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain what values and beliefs are and describe how they can shape how a person sees themselves.

Success criteria

  • Explains what values and beliefs are
  • Gives at least one example of how a value or belief influences how a person sees themselves

Prior knowledge

Students have explained how identities can be influenced by people and places, and how positive self-identities can be created (Y5-6). They can identify external influences on identity but have not yet been expected to analyse how values and beliefs specifically operate as mechanisms of influence.

Width

Minimum
Define values and beliefs and give at least one example of how a personal or family value influences how a person sees themselves.
Typical
Explain how values and beliefs from different sources (personal, family, peer, cultural) operate as influences on identity; describe specific mechanisms by which values and beliefs shape self-perception.
Wider
  • Examine how cultural values and beliefs from diverse communities in Australia — including young Australians of Asian heritage — influence self-perception and identity in distinctive ways
  • Explore how digital culture and social media create new sites where values and beliefs about identity are negotiated and contested for young people

Hinge

Analysis requires a conceptual framework for what values and beliefs are and how they function — students who cannot distinguish values and beliefs from other identity influences cannot meaningfully analyse their specific role. Reflection on personal identity is equally dependent on this foundation.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkDistinguish between values (what one believes is important) and beliefs (what one holds to be true) and model how each type of influence operates through specific examples before students attempt their own analysis.
