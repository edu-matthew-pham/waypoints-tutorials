Identifying transferable skills and strategies

AC9HP10M07 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Years 9–10 Health and Physical Education · Movement and physical activity · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Health and Physical Education: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify which skills and strategies from previous movement experiences are likely to transfer to an unfamiliar situation

Success criteria

  • Names a specific skill or strategy from a familiar movement context
  • Identifies which elements transfer and explains the underlying principle that makes them transferable
  • Distinguishes between what transfers directly and what would need adaptation

Prior knowledge

Students can practise and apply leadership, collaboration and group decision-making in physical activities (AC9HP8M09). At Y9-10 the focus shifts to the metacognitive skill of deliberately transferring movement skills and strategies from known to unknown contexts.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least one skill or strategy from a familiar movement context and explain which elements are likely to transfer to a named unfamiliar situation
Typical
Draw parallels between successful movement strategies in one sporting situation and how similar strategies could be used in a different sport; speculate on possible outcomes of innovative solutions based on past experience in outdoor or nature-based activities; identify which prior movement experiences are most relevant to a new challenge
Wider
  • Systematically map transferable skills across two or more sport or movement contexts — identify what transfers directly, what needs adaptation, and what is genuinely new
  • Apply transfer thinking to an outdoor adventure activity (rope climbing, bushwalking, abseiling, kayaking) — identify which prior movement experiences are most relevant and why
  • Evaluate which aspects of a movement strategy are context-independent (transferable) versus context-specific (non-transferable) — build a framework for deciding what to transfer

Hinge

Students who cannot identify what is transferable and why cannot deliberately apply transfer in Node 2 — they will act intuitively rather than strategically, and the evaluation of transfer success in Node 2 requires having predicted what would transfer first.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkTransfer thinking requires explicit reasoning about what makes a skill or strategy portable — what are the underlying principles that apply across contexts, versus what is specific to the original context. Students who move to unfamiliar situations without this reasoning just try familiar things without knowing why. The structured reasoning investment is in building the transfer analysis: what is the skill, what principle underpins it, in what other contexts does that principle apply.
