Safe storage of medications and substances

AC9HP4P10 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Years 3–4 Health and Physical Education · Personal, social and community health

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Health and Physical Education: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify how medications and other substances can be stored safely in the home and at school.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two rules for safely storing medications or substances.
  • Explains the reason for at least one rule.
  • Applies the rules to identify a safe storage scenario.

Prior knowledge

Students have investigated health messages and practices in their community and discussed their purposes (AC9HP2P06) and identified and demonstrated protective behaviours (AC9HP2P05). They understand health messages and basic safety behaviours but have not yet investigated or applied the full range of health behaviours across nutrition, physical activity, mental health and safety.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least two safety rules for storing medications or substances safely and explain why each rule matters.
Typical
Investigate how different types of substances (medications, cleaning products, personal care items) require safe storage, and explain the safety rationale for each.
Wider
  • Examine how household product labelling conveys safety storage information (e.g. 'keep out of reach of children', 'store below 25°C') and investigate what happens when storage instructions are not followed.
  • Explore how schools manage medication storage for students and discuss the safety procedures involved.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkSafe storage rules are best understood when the risk-consequence logic is explicit: 'we store it this way because if we didn't, X could happen'. Connecting rules to consequences builds more durable health and safety understanding than rule-memorisation alone.
