Naming emotions

AC9HPFP03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Foundation Health and Physical Education · Personal, social and community health · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Health and Physical Education: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify and name a range of emotions — happy, sad, excited, tired, angry, scared, confused — and describe what they look like in others and themselves.

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Names at least four emotions correctly when shown or described a scenario
  • Describes at least one observable indicator (expression, posture, voice) for two different emotions

Prior knowledge

No prior HPE curriculum. Students have intuitive emotional experience but limited vocabulary and structured practice for identifying and expressing emotions.

Width

Minimum
Name at least four emotions and describe what they look like (facial expression, body language) in a character, image or peer.
Typical
Identify and describe a range of emotions (at least six) in stories, images and peers; connect emotion names with observable indicators (expression, posture, voice); recall emotional responses to their own experiences.
Wider
  • Explore how emotions are expressed differently across cultures — using stories or examples from First Nations Australian or Asian cultural contexts
  • Investigate emotion expression in physical activity contexts — e.g. how winning, losing or taking risks feels
  • Represent a range of emotions through movement, drawing or creative expression

Hinge

Emotion vocabulary is prerequisite for expressing and communicating feelings (next node). Students with limited emotion vocabulary cannot access appropriate language or actions for communicating their feelings in different situations.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Repertoire building: more cases to sort or classifyEmotion vocabulary is genuinely a repertoire — more named emotions means more precise communication. Accumulate the range systematically through stories, imagery and physical activity reflection.
Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepEmotions in varied contexts — stories, movement, cultural settings — broaden recognition beyond the classroom's dominant emotional register.
