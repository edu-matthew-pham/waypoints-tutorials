← AC9HP4M07 Creative movement design

Transferring movement skills to solve challenges

AC9HP4M07 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 3–4 Health and Physical Education · Movement and physical activity · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Health and Physical Education: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can transfer and apply fundamental movement skills to solve movement challenges in different contexts.

Success criteria

  • Identifies a movement skill they know and describes a new context where it could be applied.
  • Demonstrates the skill in the new context.
  • Explains what made the transfer work or what they needed to adjust.

Prior knowledge

Students have co-constructed and applied rules to promote fair play (AC9HP2M04) and applied strategies to work collaboratively in physical activities (AC9HP2M05). They have experience working with others in movement contexts but have not yet applied creative thinking explicitly to design and problem-solving in movement.

Width

Minimum
Apply at least one movement skill from a familiar context to solve a movement challenge in an unfamiliar context, and explain the transfer.
Typical
Transfer movement skills across a range of contexts (field, net/court, outdoor, gymnastics) to solve varied movement challenges, explaining the rationale for each transfer.
Wider
  • Design a movement challenge for peers that requires them to transfer a named skill to a new context, and evaluate whether the transfer was successful.
  • Investigate how athletes transfer movement skills across different sports (e.g. a gymnast's body control applied to water polo) and explore examples from their own experience.

Hinge

Skill transfer is the cognitive basis of creative movement thinking — students who can only apply skills in the context they learned them cannot engage in the design and problem-solving that the rest of this standard demands.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkTransfer requires students to see the structural similarity between contexts ('what's the same about this problem and one I've solved before?'). A think-aloud or 'what do I already know about this?' prompt scaffold helps surface the prior knowledge needed for transfer.
