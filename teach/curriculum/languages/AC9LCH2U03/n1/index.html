← AC9LCH2U03 Target language features similar to and different from English

Word order and structural similarities

AC9LCH2U03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 1–2 Languages · Background speakers, F–10: understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Languages: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify where word order and sentence structure in the target language is similar to or different from English, with examples.

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two examples of word order similarity between the target language and English
  • Identifies at least one example of word order difference with a correct explanation

Prior knowledge

Students have noticed sound patterns and tonal features of the target language (AC9LCH2U01) and recognised how language features construct meaning (AC9LCH2U02). They have compared some sound and writing features with English. They have not yet systematically compared structural features, word order, vocabulary borrowing, or grammatical categories across the two languages using beginning metalanguage.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least two examples where word order in the target language is the same as English and at least one where it differs, using a simple sentence example for each.
Typical
Compare word order in statements, questions and phrases across the target language and English; notice where modifiers, numbers, and descriptive words appear relative to nouns; discuss where the two languages follow similar logic and where they diverge.
Wider
  • Investigate a specific structural difference — for example, how dates, names or expressions of time are ordered differently — and explain the rule in simple terms
  • Compare equivalent phrases in bilingual texts and map the structural differences — annotate a short text to show where target language and English word order matches and where it diverges

Hinge

Recognising structural similarities and differences is the foundation for language comparison. Students who assume the target language works exactly like English will make systematic errors and cannot develop the comparative metalinguistic awareness that this descriptor and the Y3-4 strand require.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStructural comparison requires students to hold two language systems in mind simultaneously. Explicit comparison frames — 'in English this goes here, in the target language it goes there, because...' — scaffold the reasoning before students can conduct comparisons independently.
