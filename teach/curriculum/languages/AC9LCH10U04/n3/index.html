Evaluating own multilingual identity

AC9LCH10U04 n3 · waypoint 3 of 3 · Years 9–10 Languages · Background speakers, F–10: understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Languages: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can evaluate how their own multilingual and multicultural identity has been shaped by the languages, cultures, attitudes and values they have encountered, and how this shapes their ways of communicating.

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Produces a reflective evaluation examining how at least two languages or cultural contexts have shaped own identity
  • Connects identity reflection to specific communication style choices or adaptations

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Title conventions and non-verbal cultural behaviour.

Width

Minimum
Produce a reflective evaluation of at least ten sentences examining how at least two languages or cultural contexts have shaped their identity and communication style.
Typical
Reflect on how own identity is shaped by languages, cultures, attitudes and values encountered daily; evaluate how multilingual background shapes communication choices; reflect on the protocols required for culturally authentic communication with diverse communities including First Nations Australians.
Wider
  • Produce an extended reflective account of own language and cultural identity journey — tracing how it has developed, what has shaped it, and how it continues to evolve
  • Compare own multilingual identity experience with that of a peer, parent, or community member from a different generation — identify what has changed in how identity is navigated across language communities and discuss what has driven the change

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Integration: a combining task at the wider tierMultilingual identity evaluation integrates globalisation analysis (node 1) and cultural behaviour understanding (node 2) into a full, personally-grounded synthesis. The teaching investment is in creating a genuinely reflective, non-prescriptive environment where students can examine their own identity honestly — including tensions, contradictions and ongoing negotiations — rather than producing a tidy, resolved account.
