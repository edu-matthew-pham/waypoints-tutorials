← AC9LCH2U01 Sound patterns and tones

Representing sounds in written form

AC9LCH2U01 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Years 1–2 Languages · Background speakers, F–10: understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Languages: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can represent target language sounds in written form using the romanisation or phonetic system of the target language, including tone marks where applicable.

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Writes at least three syllables in the target language phonetic system with correct tone marks
  • Can match at least five written phonetic forms to their spoken equivalents

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Recognising and distinguishing tones.

Width

Minimum
Write at least three syllables in the target language romanisation or phonetic system with correct tone marks, demonstrating understanding that the written form represents spoken sound.
Typical
Write syllables and words in the target language phonetic or romanisation system with correct tone marks; match written phonetic forms to spoken sounds; understand that the romanisation system is a learning tool representing spoken language; play pair-matching games matching sound to written form.
Wider
  • Investigate how the romanisation or phonetic system of the target language handles sounds that are different from English — identify sounds that require attention because they look like English letters but sound different
  • Analyse how tone marks or diacritic marks on the same vowel produce different words — create examples showing the same syllable in all tones with meanings

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents need explicit instruction on how the phonetic or romanisation system works — it is not intuitive for English speakers or for students who know the language only orally. Explicit mapping between spoken sound and written symbol, with attention to deceptive similarities with English, prevents systematic errors.
