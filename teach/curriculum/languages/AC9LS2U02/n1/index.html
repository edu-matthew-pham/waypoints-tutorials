Grammatical markers and word class features

AC9LS2U02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 1–2 Languages · Second language, F–10: understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Languages: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can recognise how grammatical markers in the target language signal word class, agreement and relationships between words — and apply these in familiar word groups.

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Uses correct grammatical markers with at least four familiar words
  • Produces at least two word groups with correct agreement patterns

Prior knowledge

Students have noticed basic grammatical patterns with support (AC9LSF02). They are developing early literacy in English using the Roman alphabet. They have no prior systematic knowledge of target language grammatical structures.

Width

Minimum
Correctly use grammatical markers (e.g. articles, agreement markers) with at least four familiar words; produce at least two word groups showing correct agreement.
Typical
Recognise and apply grammatical agreement patterns across familiar vocabulary; observe word order patterns; notice how endings and markers signal relationships between words; use possessive forms; produce simple descriptive word groups with correct agreement.
Wider
  • Sort a set of unfamiliar target language words into grammatical categories using patterns and context clues — explain the rule applied
  • Identify every grammatical marker in a short familiar text and describe what each one signals

Hinge

Grammatical agreement and word class markers are the foundational system governing noun-phrase and sentence production in the target language. Students who do not internalise these patterns produce systematic errors that cascade throughout all sentence production at every subsequent band.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Consolidation: more practise steps, short feedback loopsGrammatical agreement requires high-frequency practice across a wide range of vocabulary before it becomes automatic. Students need to encounter and produce marked forms in varied contexts — not only in drills but in real text creation and comprehension.
