Comparing translations and culture-specific terms

AC9LCH10EC04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 9–10 Languages · Background speakers, from Year 7: communicating · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Languages: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can compare different translations of the same text or expression, evaluate translation choices, and explain strategies for handling culture-specific terms.

Success criteria

  • Compares at least two translations of the same expression or text and evaluates the difference
  • Explains the strategy used to handle at least one culture-specific term

Prior knowledge

Students can interpret and produce bilingual texts, manage register conversion, and handle culturally-embedded language (AC9LCH8EC04). They have interpreted for others in familiar contexts. They have not yet interpreted for high-stakes cross-cultural events, managed idiom and cultural term challenges at the extended text level, or produced subtitled multimodal translations.

Width

Minimum
Compare at least two translations of the same expression or short text, evaluate the difference, and explain the strategy used to handle at least one culture-specific term.
Typical
Compare and discuss various target language translations of English words and expressions; identify words used with specific cultural assumptions — gender, formality, significance; consider strategies for translating culture-specific terms; use resources including online tools to understand emerging new words.
Wider
  • Research the translation history of a specific culture-specific term — document different translation approaches across contexts and evaluate which works best for which audience
  • Compare how the same culture-specific concept is handled across multiple published bilingual texts — identify patterns in translation strategy and evaluate effectiveness

Hinge

Translation comparison and culture-specific term handling are the prerequisites for high-stakes interpretation. Students who cannot compare and evaluate translation choices cannot produce culturally accurate interpretations for complex or formal contexts.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkTranslation comparison requires systematic analysis — students need a framework for evaluating what is gained and lost in each translation choice. Explicit comparative analysis with documented reasoning builds the evaluative capacity needed.
