Following and giving classroom instructions

AC9LS8EC02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Years 7–8 Languages · Second language, from Year 7: communicating · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Languages: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can follow modelled instructions and commands in the target language and attempt to give simple instructions to peers.

Success criteria

  • Responds correctly to at least four common classroom commands in the target language without translation support
  • Can give at least one simple instruction to a peer in the target language

Prior knowledge

Students can exchange basic personal information in familiar contexts using modelled language (AC9LS8EC01). They have not yet developed the functional classroom language needed to participate in the routines, tasks and responsibilities of the target language learning environment.

Width

Minimum
Follow at least four common classroom commands in the target language reliably; attempt to give one or two simple instructions.
Typical
Follow and give a range of classroom instructions (open/close, listen, write, read, work with a partner); respond appropriately to teacher commands during regular classroom routines.
Wider
  • Lead a class activity segment using only target language instructions — managing transitions and turn-taking
  • Create a bilingual classroom reference resource (poster, card set) translating common commands between the target language and English

Hinge

Classroom instruction language is the operational medium of the language classroom. Students who cannot follow basic commands cannot access the task structures through which all subsequent language learning occurs. This is a functional floor, not an enrichment item.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Consolidation: more practise steps, short feedback loopsReliable response to instructions requires automaticity — students need repeated encounters with the same commands in varied classroom routines until response becomes habitual rather than effortful.
