Transactional role-play language

AC9LCH4C02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 3–4 Languages · Background speakers, F–10: communicating · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Languages: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can participate in transactional role-plays — buying, ordering, negotiating — using appropriate request forms, quantity and price language, and transaction-closing expressions.

Success criteria

  • Participates in a transactional role-play using at least three appropriate target language phrases
  • Successfully completes a transaction — stating a preference, negotiating or requesting, and closing the exchange

Prior knowledge

Students can initiate and sustain exchanges, collaborate in play-based activities, and use digital formats for interaction (AC9LCH4C01, AC9LCH2C02). They can take turns, give instructions and participate in role-play. They have not yet participated in transactional exchanges (buying, ordering, negotiating), planned and presented cultural items, or interacted with guest speakers in structured contexts.

Width

Minimum
Participate in at least one transactional role-play (e.g. buying food, ordering a meal) using at least three appropriate target language phrases for the transaction.
Typical
Participate in transactional scenarios — shops, restaurants, markets — making requests, stating preferences, negotiating quantity and price, and concluding transactions; use appropriate modelled language for different stages of a transaction; use real or simulated currency or props.
Wider
  • Compare how transactions are conducted in the target language community with Australian conventions — identify differences in etiquette, bargaining norms, or interaction patterns
  • Extend a transactional role-play beyond a scripted exchange — add complications (wrong item, price disagreement, special request) and negotiate a resolution

Hinge

Transactional language is the functional prerequisite for all planning and negotiation activity. Students who cannot manage a basic transaction cannot participate in the more complex planning and collaborative presentation tasks this descriptor requires.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepTransactional language transfers across different transaction types — food ordering, shopping, seeking services. Practising across varied scenarios (not only one scripted shop) builds the flexible transactional capacity that the descriptor requires.
