← AC9LCH4U03 Compare features of target language and English texts

Cross-linguistic influence and text features

AC9LCH4U03 n3 · waypoint 3 of 3 · Years 3–4 Languages · Background speakers, F–10: understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Languages: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify examples of cross-linguistic influence in everyday target language use and compare layout, script and textual features across target language and English texts.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two examples of English influence on everyday target language use
  • Identifies at least two textual feature differences between a target language and English text of the same type

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Metalanguage for grammar comparison.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least two examples of English influence on everyday target language use and at least two textual feature differences between a target language and English text.
Typical
Explore how English influences informal everyday target language use — borrowed words, code-switching, adapted expressions; compare layout, heading, script and organisational features of target language and English texts; identify similarities and differences in how the same information is organised across text types.
Wider
  • Research the history of a specific borrowed word or expression — trace where it came from and how it entered everyday target language use
  • Create a bilingual comparison display showing how the same text type is structured differently in target language and English — annotate the key differences

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Integration: a combining task at the wider tierCross-linguistic influence and textual feature comparison integrates genre awareness (node 1) and grammar metalanguage (node 2) into a full text-level analysis. The teaching investment is in making visible what students often see as normal — the way English has entered everyday target language, and the distinct textual conventions of each language.
