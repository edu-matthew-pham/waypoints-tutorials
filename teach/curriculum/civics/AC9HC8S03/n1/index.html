Identifying differences in perspective

AC9HC8S03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 8 Civics and Citizenship · Skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Civics and Citizenship: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify differences in perspective in information about a civic issue, explaining what the perspectives are and naming the values or interests that ground them.

Success criteria

  • Can identify at least two different perspectives on a civic issue
  • Can explain at least one value or interest that grounds each perspective, not just describe what each person believes

Prior knowledge

Students have analysed civic information at Year 7 level (AC9HC7S03) to identify differences in perspectives. The gap this descriptor fills is the move to analysis that also identifies and explains potential challenges — not just that perspectives differ, but why they differ and what consequences those differences have for civic and democratic life.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least two different perspectives on a civic issue from provided information and explain what grounds each perspective.
Typical
Examine information from multiple sources on a civic issue — such as letters to the editor about a current issue or perspectives on marriage equality — identifying the different perspectives represented, naming the values or interests that ground each perspective, and explaining the key differences between them.
Wider
  • Investigate how the same civic issue (e.g. immigration, climate policy, Indigenous rights) is framed differently across a range of source types — news, advocacy, government, academic — and explain how each framing reflects a distinct perspective
  • Examine speeches from prominent Australian politicians or jurists on the same issue and identify the values and assumptions that produce different positions
  • Practise perspective-mapping: systematically recording all identified perspectives on a civic issue with their grounding values, and then identifying patterns (e.g. which perspectives dominate, which are marginalised)

Hinge

If students can identify perspectives but cannot explain what grounds them (values, interests, experiences), their analysis remains superficial and they cannot reason about why potential challenges arise. The grounding of perspectives is the key analytical move that enables the rest of this descriptor.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents often describe perspectives as 'Person A thinks X, Person B thinks Y' without explaining why. Invest in the reasoning scaffold: what values, experiences or interests produce each position, and how do those differences explain the disagreement?
