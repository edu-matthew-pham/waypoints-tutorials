Federal responsibilities and policy scope

AC9HC9K02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 9 Civics and Citizenship · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Civics and Citizenship: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify the areas of federal government responsibility that define the scope of federal policy-making.

Success criteria

  • Names at least two policy areas that are federal government responsibility
  • Explains why those areas fall under Commonwealth rather than state responsibility

Prior knowledge

Students know the role of political parties and independent representatives in Australian democracy including elections and formation of governments (AC9HC8K02), and how statutory law is made through parliaments (AC9HC8K03). They understand the basic passage of a bill into law but have not examined the upstream policy process — how government decisions move from idea to legislation.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least two areas of federal government responsibility as outlined in the Constitution and explain why these areas fall under federal rather than state jurisdiction.
Typical
Locate areas of federal government responsibility as outlined in the Constitution; explain why federal policy operates in these areas; identify how conflicts over federal/state/territory responsibilities are resolved (e.g. inconsistency doctrine, referral of powers).
Wider
  • Investigate a contemporary area of federal-state responsibility tension (e.g. natural disaster funding, public hospital funding) and analyse how the Constitution shapes who can act
  • Examine cases where states have referred powers to the Commonwealth and evaluate the implications for federal policy scope

Hinge

Without understanding which level of government has responsibility for a policy area, students cannot meaningfully trace how federal policy is developed — they may apply federal policy processes to state policy areas or vice versa throughout the rest of the sequence.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents often default to thinking 'the government' makes all policy without distinguishing federal from state responsibility. A structured exercise mapping policy areas to jurisdictions anchors the subsequent process tracing.
