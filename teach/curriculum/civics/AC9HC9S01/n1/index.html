Developing focused civic inquiry questions

AC9HC9S01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 9 Civics and Citizenship · Skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Civics and Citizenship: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can develop focused, investigable questions about Australia's political and legal systems or a contemporary civic issue.

Success criteria

  • Develops at least one inquiry question that is focused on a political, legal or civic issue and requires analysis rather than a simple factual answer
  • Explains why the question is worth investigating

Prior knowledge

Students can develop questions to investigate Australia's political and legal systems and contemporary civic issues (AC9HC8S01). At Year 9 the expectation adds modification — students are expected to revisit and refine questions as their investigation develops, not just formulate them at the outset.

Width

Minimum
Develop at least one question that is focused enough to investigate (not too broad, not a yes/no question) about a political, legal or civic topic.
Typical
Develop focused inquiry questions about Australia's political and legal systems or civic issues; distinguish between questions that can be answered with facts and questions that require analysis and evaluation; practise constructing questions using the elaboration examples (e.g. 'How democratic is Australia's system of government?' or 'Does Australia's legal system deliver justice?').
Wider
  • Develop question sets at different levels of abstraction (factual, analytical, evaluative) for the same civic topic and assess how each type drives different kinds of investigation
  • Practise developing inquiry questions from news events or policy changes, evaluating what makes a civic question worth investigating
  • Compare student-generated questions with those from published civic education inquiries and evaluate the differences in focus and scope

Hinge

A poorly scoped initial question prevents meaningful modification — students cannot improve a question that was never adequately formulated. If the initial question is too vague or simply factual, the modification work in Node 2 has nothing to build on and the investigation cannot proceed purposefully.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents need explicit practice distinguishing researchable analytical questions from vague or simply factual ones. Working through several examples — identifying what makes each stronger or weaker — builds the judgment needed before they formulate their own inquiry questions.
