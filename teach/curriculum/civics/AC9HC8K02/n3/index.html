Formation and loss of government

AC9HC8K02 n3 · waypoint 3 of 4 · Year 8 Civics and Citizenship · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Civics and Citizenship: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain how government is formed and how it may be lost in Australia, using concepts including parliamentary majority, the opposition, hung parliament, minority government, party discipline and balance of power.

Success criteria

  • Can explain what a party needs to form majority government and what happens if no single party achieves a majority
  • Can use at least two of the following concepts correctly in explaining how government may be lost or constrained: party discipline, balance of power, hung parliament, minority government, the opposition

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Voting systems.

Width

Minimum
Explain how a government is formed after a federal election — what a party needs to form government and what happens if no party has a majority.
Typical
Explain how government is formed and may be lost in Australia, applying the key concepts: parliamentary majority, the opposition, hung parliament, minority government, party discipline and balance of power. Explain what a confidence vote is and how a government can fall.
Wider
  • Analyse a real example of a hung parliament or minority government in Australia (federal or state), examining what negotiations took place and what the balance of power looked like
  • Investigate the role of party discipline in maintaining government stability — how party discipline operates and what happens when it breaks down (e.g. conscience votes, party defections)
  • Examine the role of the crossbench in the current Senate and how balance of power operates in practice

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepAbstract concepts like hung parliament and balance of power become meaningful when grounded in real examples. Widening across actual Australian cases prevents students from holding purely theoretical understandings that don't survive contact with real political events.
