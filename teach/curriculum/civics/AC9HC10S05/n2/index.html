Constructing an evidence-based argument

AC9HC10S05 n2 · waypoint 2 of 2 · Year 10 Civics and Citizenship · Skills

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Civics and Citizenship: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can construct a structured argument about a civics and citizenship issue that incorporates evidence, draws conclusions, and uses civics knowledge and terminology throughout.

Success criteria

  • Constructs an argument with a clear claim supported by at least two pieces of evidence
  • Uses civics concepts and terminology accurately throughout
  • Draws a conclusion that follows from the evidence presented

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Using civics knowledge and terminology accurately.

Width

Minimum
Construct a structured argument about a civics issue that includes a clear claim, at least two pieces of supporting evidence, and a conclusion.
Typical
Develop an evidence-based argument about a civics and citizenship issue — such as Australia's role in Asia-Pacific security, or trading relationships with nations with human rights issues — that draws on relevant knowledge, uses civics concepts and terms accurately, and draws conclusions about future impacts.
Wider
  • Develop an evidence-based argument that acknowledges and responds to the strongest counter-argument, demonstrating understanding of competing civic perspectives
  • Construct an argument about a live Australian civic debate (e.g. the Uluru Statement, Australia Day date, treaty with First Nations Australians) that draws on multiple sources and takes a defensible position
  • Write for a specific audience and purpose — e.g. a submission to a parliamentary inquiry, a letter to a minister, a public opinion piece — evaluating how the argument should be adapted for the context

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkArgument construction requires students to distinguish claim from evidence, and evidence from assertion. Scaffold the structural logic of argument explicitly — model how evidence connects to claims rather than sitting beside them.
Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepWriting for a specific audience and purpose (parliamentary submission, ministerial letter) gives students a real-world context that sharpens argument construction — purpose shapes what counts as relevant evidence.
