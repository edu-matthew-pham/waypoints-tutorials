Australia's key international treaty commitments

AC9HC10K04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 10 Civics and Citizenship · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Civics and Citizenship: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify the key international treaties Australia has signed and ratified, and explain what signing and ratifying a treaty requires of the Australian Government.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two international treaties Australia has signed and ratified
  • Explains the difference between signing and ratifying a treaty
  • Describes at least one obligation that ratification creates for the Australian Government

Prior knowledge

Students know how individuals and groups participate in civic life and contribute to global citizenship (AC9HC9K05). They understand the court system's role in applying law (AC9HC9K03, AC9HC9K04).

Width

Minimum
Identify at least two international treaties Australia has signed and ratified, and explain the difference between signing and ratifying a treaty.
Typical
Identify the main categories of international human rights and other treaties Australia has signed and ratified, explain the signing-to-ratification-to-implementation pathway, and describe what these obligations require of the Australian Government.
Wider
  • Research how many of the 9 key UN human rights treaties Australia has signed and ratified, and identify any that Australia has signed but not ratified
  • Investigate the reservations Australia has entered on certain treaty obligations and what this means for compliance
  • Examine the role of the UN treaty monitoring bodies and how Australia has engaged with them

Hinge

Students who don't understand the signing-ratification-implementation distinction will not be able to analyse the gap between Australia's stated obligations and its actual policies — they lack the conceptual framework to identify issues in application.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe treaty pathway (signed → ratified → implemented) is not self-evident and students frequently conflate stages. Invest in this foundational concept before examining specific treaty effects on domestic law.
