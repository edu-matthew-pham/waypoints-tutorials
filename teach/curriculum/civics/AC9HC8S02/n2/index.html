Selecting sources: accuracy and reliability

AC9HC8S02 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Year 8 Civics and Citizenship · Skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Civics and Citizenship: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can select information from sources by applying criteria of accuracy and reliability, distinguishing between more and less reliable sources on a civic issue.

Lessons on this site

Success criteria

  • Can identify at least two criteria for evaluating the reliability of a source
  • Can apply those criteria to at least one source on a civic issue and justify whether it is reliable

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Locating civic information using targeted search.

Width

Minimum
Evaluate one source for accuracy and reliability, identifying at least two criteria used and explaining whether the source meets them.
Typical
Select information from a range of sources by applying understandings of accuracy (whether claims are factually correct) and reliability (whether the source can be trusted to report faithfully), distinguishing between government reports, peer-reviewed content, advocacy materials and social media on a civics issue.
Wider
  • Compare the reliability of a government report and an advocacy organisation's report on the same civic issue, identifying what each source includes, excludes and emphasises
  • Investigate how fact-checking organisations evaluate political claims — applying similar criteria to evaluate sources used in a civic investigation
  • Examine how the funding, audience and purpose of a source affects its reliability as a source of civic information

Hinge

If students cannot apply reliability criteria to sources, they will build their analysis and arguments in S03 and S05 on uncritically accepted material. A student who accepts all sources equally cannot identify perspective differences or evaluate the strength of civic arguments.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkReliability evaluation requires explicit criteria and reasoning — not just intuition about whether a source 'seems credible'. Invest in the evaluative framework: authority, accuracy, currency, purpose, coverage.
