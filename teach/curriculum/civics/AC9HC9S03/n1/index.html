Identifying values and motivations underlying perspectives

AC9HC9S03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 9 Civics and Citizenship · Skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Civics and Citizenship: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify the values, motivations and contexts that underpin different perspectives on a civic or political issue.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two perspectives on a civic issue
  • Explains the value or motivation underlying each perspective, not just the position held

Prior knowledge

Students can analyse information about civic issues to identify and explain differences in perspectives and potential challenges (AC9HC8S03). At Year 9 the expectation advances from explaining differences to evaluating them — students must assess the values, motivations and contexts that underpin perspectives, not just identify that perspectives differ.

Width

Minimum
Identify the values or motivations underlying at least two different perspectives on a civic or political issue.
Typical
Identify the values, motivations and contexts that underpin different perspectives about political and legal systems and processes; explain why the same information can be interpreted differently depending on perspective; apply this to at least one substantive civic issue (e.g. constitutional recognition for First Nations Australians, impacts of mining activity on communities).
Wider
  • Investigate a civic issue where perspectives divide along lines of lived experience (e.g. First Nations Australians' perspectives on constitutional recognition versus non-Indigenous perspectives) and analyse how context shapes interpretation
  • Examine a historical civic issue and a contemporary one to compare whether the values underpinning key perspectives have changed over time
  • Map perspectives on a policy debate using a values framework (e.g. liberty, equality, community, authority) to evaluate what is really at stake in the disagreement

Hinge

If students can only identify that perspectives differ without understanding why, the evaluation in Node 2 is superficial — they end up comparing conclusions rather than reasoning. Evaluation requires understanding that perspectives are grounded in values and contexts, not just positions.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe move from 'these perspectives differ' to 'these perspectives differ because they prioritise different values' is the key analytical step. Students benefit from an explicit scaffold that asks: what does this perspective assume? What does it prioritise? What would challenge it?
