Characteristics of a resilient democracy and cohesive society

AC9HC10K05 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 10 Civics and Citizenship · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Civics and Citizenship: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify the key characteristics of cohesive, democratic societies and explain why an active and informed citizenry is important for sustaining them.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least three characteristics of cohesive, democratic societies
  • Explains why an active and informed citizenry is important for sustaining democracy
  • Uses accurate civics terminology (e.g. civic participation, social cohesion)

Prior knowledge

Students know how individuals and groups participate in and contribute to civic life in Australia and to global citizenship (AC9HC9K05). They understand the influence of media, including social media, in shaping identity and attitudes to diversity (AC9HC9K06).

Width

Minimum
Identify at least three key characteristics of cohesive, democratic societies and explain why each matters.
Typical
Explain the key characteristics of cohesive, democratic societies — cooperation, inclusion, respect, peaceful resolution of conflicts, and an active and informed citizenry — and examine how these characteristics reinforce each other.
Wider
  • Investigate how First Nations Australians have articulated the conditions needed for a cohesive and democratic Australia (e.g. through the Uluru Statement from the Heart) and compare with mainstream democratic theory
  • Explore what 'active citizenship' requires in practice and examine examples of citizens who have strengthened democratic resilience
  • Examine international research on what makes democracies resilient over time and evaluate how Australia compares

Hinge

Students who don't have a clear model of what a resilient democracy looks like cannot identify what constitutes a threat to it — the threat analysis in the next node requires a baseline understanding of what is being threatened.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents often have intuitive but vague notions of democracy. Invest in building a precise, multi-dimensional model before examining threats — the quality of threat analysis depends on how clearly the baseline is defined.
Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepThe Uluru Statement direction connects the characteristics of a cohesive democracy to an active civic document produced by First Nations Australians — this is a richer starting point than abstract theory alone.
