Court hierarchy and jurisdiction

AC9HC9K03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 9 Civics and Citizenship · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Civics and Citizenship: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can describe the structure of Australia's court hierarchy and explain what jurisdiction means in this context.

Success criteria

  • Names at least three levels of the court hierarchy in order
  • Explains what jurisdiction means and gives an example of a matter each court level can hear

Prior knowledge

Students know the characteristics of laws and how they are made through parliaments and courts (AC9HC8K03), and the types of law in Australia including criminal and civil law and First Nations customary law (AC9HC8K04). They understand law is made through two mechanisms but have not yet examined how the court hierarchy is structured or how different courts exercise their jurisdiction.

Width

Minimum
Identify the main levels of the Australian court hierarchy (Magistrates/Local Court, District/County Court, Supreme Court, High Court) and explain that each court's jurisdiction defines the matters it can hear.
Typical
Create or interpret a visual representation of the Australian court hierarchy; explain what each court's jurisdiction covers (original and appellate); describe where most criminal and civil matters begin and how matters move up the hierarchy on appeal.
Wider
  • Compare the structure and jurisdiction of courts in two Australian states and identify how federal and state court systems interact
  • Investigate the role of specialist courts and tribunals (e.g. Family Court, Administrative Tribunals, NCAT, VCAT) and explain why these exist alongside the general court hierarchy
  • Examine how the High Court's jurisdiction to hear constitutional challenges distinguishes it from all other courts in the hierarchy

Hinge

If students do not understand the concept of jurisdiction — that different courts have authority over different types and scales of matters — they cannot make sense of Node 2's civil/criminal distinction or Node 3's explanation of how judgements create precedent. Students who treat all courts as interchangeable cannot trace how matters progress through the system.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe concept of jurisdiction is abstract; students benefit from a structured exercise mapping specific types of cases (minor traffic infringement, serious criminal trial, constitutional challenge) to the court that would hear them. This makes jurisdiction operational rather than definitional.
