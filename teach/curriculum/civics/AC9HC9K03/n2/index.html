← AC9HC9K03 Court jurisdictions and tribunals

Civil and criminal law in practice

AC9HC9K03 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Year 9 Civics and Citizenship · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Civics and Citizenship: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can use sample cases to explain the differences between civil and criminal law and how courts apply each.

Success criteria

  • Correctly classifies at least one sample case as civil or criminal and explains the reasoning
  • Identifies a difference in who initiates a civil case versus a criminal case

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Court hierarchy and jurisdiction.

Width

Minimum
Use at least one sample case to explain the difference between a civil dispute and a criminal matter, including who brings the case and what the court can order.
Typical
Use sample cases to investigate the differences between civil and criminal law — including who initiates action, burden of proof, and available remedies or penalties; explain how courts apply and interpret criminal laws and resolve civil disputes; distinguish between the roles of courts and tribunals.
Wider
  • Investigate a specific civil case (e.g. a tort or contract dispute) and a specific criminal case and compare the process, parties, standards of proof and outcomes
  • Examine how the same event (e.g. assault, negligence) can give rise to both civil and criminal proceedings simultaneously, and evaluate the implications
  • Explore how tribunals differ from courts in resolving civil disputes and assess advantages and limitations of each pathway

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepThe civil/criminal distinction is easier to apply than to define in the abstract. Using two or three contrasting sample cases (one clearly civil, one clearly criminal, one ambiguous) helps students build flexible understanding rather than pattern-matching on surface features.
