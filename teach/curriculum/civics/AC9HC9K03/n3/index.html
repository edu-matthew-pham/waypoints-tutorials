Precedent and the development of law

AC9HC9K03 n3 · waypoint 3 of 3 · Year 9 Civics and Citizenship · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Civics and Citizenship: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain how court judgements impact on the development of law through statutory interpretation and the creation of precedent.

Success criteria

  • Explains what a legal precedent is and why courts follow them
  • Uses the Mabo Case (or equivalent) to illustrate how a court judgement can develop the law beyond statute

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Civil and criminal law in practice.

Width

Minimum
Explain that courts interpret laws when applying them and that significant judgements can set precedents that bind future courts.
Typical
Explain the roles of statutory interpretation and precedent in the development of law; describe how a significant judgement (e.g. the Mabo Case 1992) overturned an established legal concept and created new law; discuss how this illustrates the law-making function of courts alongside parliament.
Wider
  • Trace the legal legacy of the Mabo Case through subsequent native title legislation and cases, assessing how one precedent reshaped an entire area of law
  • Investigate a more recent High Court decision (e.g. LGBTIQ+ rights, immigration, corporate liability) and explain how it changed or clarified the law
  • Compare the doctrine of precedent in Australia's common law system with the approach in a civil law country and evaluate the advantages of each

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkPrecedent is conceptually demanding — students need to understand that a judgement in one case constrains decisions in future unrelated cases. Unpacking the logic of why judges follow precedent (legal certainty, equality) is worth explicit investment before students engage with specific cases.
