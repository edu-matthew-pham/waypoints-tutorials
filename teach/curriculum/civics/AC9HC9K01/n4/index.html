Referendum process and constitutional change

AC9HC9K01 n4 · waypoint 4 of 4 · Year 9 Civics and Citizenship · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Civics and Citizenship: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can describe the process for changing the Australian Constitution through referendum and assess why it is difficult.

Success criteria

  • Describes the double majority requirement for a successful referendum
  • Explains at least one advantage and one disadvantage of requiring a referendum to change the Constitution
  • Names at least one successful or unsuccessful referendum and states its outcome

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 3: Constitutional protection of democratic processes.

Width

Minimum
Describe the double majority requirement for a successful referendum and explain why the Constitution can only be changed this way.
Typical
Describe the full referendum process including how it is initiated and decided, the role of the Australian Electoral Commission, and the double majority requirement; explain the advantages and disadvantages of requiring a referendum for constitutional change; discuss examples of successful and unsuccessful referenda.
Wider
  • Assess the effectiveness of constitutional change through referendum by examining both the successful 1967 referendum (Aboriginals) and the unsuccessful 1999 republic referendum, comparing the conditions that led to each outcome
  • Investigate why no referendum has succeeded since 1977 and evaluate whether the referendum threshold is an appropriate safeguard or barrier to democratic reform
  • Examine current debate about an Indigenous Voice to Parliament and consider what constitutional change would require

Hinge

Understanding the referendum mechanism is the integration point for the whole standard — students need to connect the Constitution's foundational status (Nodes 1–3) with the logic of why such a document is deliberately hard to change. Thin treatment leaves the referendum as a procedural trivia item rather than a meaningful democratic mechanism.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepWorking through specific referendum cases (successful and unsuccessful) makes the double majority requirement concrete and allows students to analyse what conditions lead to constitutional change. This is where the procedural knowledge becomes evaluative reasoning.
