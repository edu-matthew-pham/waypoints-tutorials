Identifying perspectives in civic sources

AC9HC7S03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 7 Civics and Citizenship · Skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Civics and Citizenship: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify the perspective expressed in a civic source — who produced it, what position it represents, and how this shapes the information it contains.

Success criteria

  • Identify the perspective represented by a civic source and explain how that perspective might influence the information it contains
  • Identify at least one perspective that might be absent from a given source

Prior knowledge

Students can evaluate information to identify patterns, trends, and relationships (AC9HS6S03) and evaluate sources and perspectives (AC9HS6S04). The Y7 shift is to explicitly civic analytical work — identifying how perspectives on political and legal issues differ and why, and what challenges arise from this.

Width

Minimum
Identify the source of a piece of civic information (e.g. government website, newspaper editorial, advocacy group report), describe whose perspective it represents, and explain how that perspective might shape the information.
Typical
Examine online publications or media reports and identify the perspective each represents; explain how a government source, a civil society source, and a media source on the same issue might present the information differently; identify whose perspective might be absent from each source.
Wider
  • Compare multiple sources on the same civic issue (e.g. court system reform) from different institutional perspectives — government, legal profession, advocacy group, media — to map the range of viewpoints
  • Investigate how the framing of a civic issue (e.g. which facts are emphasised, what language is used) reflects the perspective of the source
  • Explore how First Nations Australians' perspectives on civic issues (e.g. land rights, constitutional recognition) may differ from mainstream government or media coverage of those issues

Hinge

Identifying perspective is the prerequisite for explaining differences between perspectives — students who cannot see a source as a positioned view rather than neutral information cannot meaningfully compare perspectives or identify challenges.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe move from 'what does this source say' to 'whose perspective does this source represent and how does that shape it' is a genuine conceptual step — invest in this distinction explicitly before moving to comparison.
