Sources of rights protection in Australia

AC9HC10K03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 10 Civics and Citizenship · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Civics and Citizenship: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify the three main sources of rights protection in Australia — the Constitution, common law, and statute law — and explain how each operates.

Success criteria

  • Correctly identifies all three sources of rights protection in Australia
  • Explains how at least two of the three sources operate to protect rights
  • Distinguishes between constitutional rights and legislated rights

Prior knowledge

Students know the key features and jurisdictions of Australia's court system and the operations of courts and tribunals (AC9HC9K03). They understand the role of courts, judges, lawyers and juries in trials, and the rights of the accused and victims (AC9HC9K04).

Width

Minimum
Identify the three sources of rights protection (Constitution, common law, statute law) and explain in basic terms how each provides protection.
Typical
Explain how the Constitution, common law, and federal and state statute law each protect rights, including the nature of express versus implied constitutional rights, judge-made common law rights, and legislated rights.
Wider
  • Compare Australia's approach to rights protection (without a bill of rights) with a constitutional bill of rights (e.g. USA) and a statutory bill of rights (e.g. New Zealand), evaluating the strengths and limitations of each
  • Investigate what 'express rights' and 'implied rights' mean in the Australian constitutional context using specific historical examples
  • Explore why Australia does not have a national bill of rights and analyse the arguments for and against introducing one

Hinge

Students who conflate the three sources — or who don't understand the distinction between constitutional, common law and statutory rights — will misattribute the role of parliament vs High Court in subsequent nodes, undermining the whole comparison.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe three-source framework requires clear categorical distinctions. Students commonly conflate common law and statute law, or assume the Constitution is the only source. Scaffolded comparison of all three before examining specific cases reduces confusion.
Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepComparing with the US and NZ models gives students a clear external reference point that makes Australia's unusual rights framework more legible.
