Rights of the accused and victims

AC9HC9K04 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Year 9 Civics and Citizenship · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Civics and Citizenship: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can compare the rights of the accused with the rights of victims in criminal trials, and identify the rights of parties in civil cases.

Success criteria

  • Names at least two rights of the accused in a criminal trial and explains their purpose
  • Identifies at least one right or protection available to victims and compares it with a corresponding right of the accused

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Roles within trials.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least two rights of the accused in a criminal trial (e.g. right to silence, presumption of innocence) and state one right afforded to victims.
Typical
Compare the rights of the accused with the rights of victims in criminal trials, including presumption of innocence, right to silence, right to legal representation, and the role of victim impact statements; compare the rights of plaintiff and defendant in a civil case; explain the role of the police in enabling equal treatment under the law.
Wider
  • Examine cases where the rights of victims and rights of the accused have been argued to conflict, and evaluate how the legal system attempts to balance them
  • Investigate how victim support services and restorative justice approaches seek to address limitations in formal victim rights
  • Compare rights of the accused in Australia with those in another jurisdiction (e.g. the USA) and evaluate differences

Hinge

The tension between rights of the accused and rights of victims is the conceptual core of this standard, and thin understanding here means students cannot engage with the access-to-justice analysis in Node 3. Students who reduce rights to 'accused has all the rights' or 'victims are left out' cannot evaluate the system's fairness.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents often assume the presumption of innocence privileges the accused at the expense of victims. Unpacking why these procedural protections exist (preventing wrongful conviction) before comparing them with victim entitlements produces more nuanced analysis.
Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepWorking through specific case types — a domestic violence case, a property crime case — that make rights concrete helps students move past the abstract definitions and into evaluation.
