← AC9HC8S05 Create civic arguments

Evidence-referenced civic descriptions and explanations

AC9HC8S05 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Year 8 Civics and Citizenship · Skills

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Civics and Citizenship: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can create civic descriptions and explanations that reference evidence — connecting claims to sources or examples rather than making unsupported assertions.

Success criteria

  • Can write a civic description or explanation that includes at least one explicitly referenced piece of evidence
  • Can explain why the evidence they have chosen supports the claim it is linked to

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Civic concepts and terminology.

Width

Minimum
Write a civic description or explanation that references at least one piece of evidence (fact, example or source) to support a key claim.
Typical
Create civic descriptions and explanations that use subject-specific knowledge and terminology and that reference evidence — connecting claims to specific examples, case studies, statistics or cited sources — demonstrating that assertions about political and legal systems are grounded rather than opinion-only.
Wider
  • Practise creating descriptions and explanations for different civic audiences — comparing how evidence is referenced differently for a formal report versus a public information piece versus a student essay
  • Examine how different types of evidence (statistical, anecdotal, expert opinion, legal precedent) function differently in civic descriptions and explanations, and practise using each type
  • Analyse exemplar civic descriptions and explanations — identifying where evidence is used effectively, where it is missing and what stronger evidence use would look like

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepEvidence-referenced writing is a transferable skill. Widening across different civic topics and different output types — descriptions, explanations, reports — builds the habit of evidence-grounding across contexts, not just in one assessed task.
