Examining civic action plans

AC9HC9S04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 9 Civics and Citizenship · Skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Civics and Citizenship: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can examine a government or community action plan, identifying its actions, measures, and intended and unintended effects.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two specific actions in a civic action plan
  • Identifies at least one intended effect and one measure used to track it

Prior knowledge

Students can explain the methods or strategies related to making decisions about civic participation (AC9HC8S04). At Year 9 the expectation advances from explaining to evaluating — students must assess whether methods are effective and what their intended and unintended effects are.

Width

Minimum
Examine one government or community action plan and identify its intended goals and at least one measure used to achieve them.
Typical
Examine a government action plan and identify the actions, the measures, and the intended and unintended effects of the plan; use examples such as a local recycling program, a national literacy campaign, or a waste reduction strategy; explain how intended and unintended effects are discovered and how they should inform future planning.
Wider
  • Compare two civic action plans on the same issue (e.g. two different government approaches to reducing plastic waste) and evaluate which methods were more effective and why
  • Investigate how a government action plan has been evaluated over time and assess whether evaluation data led to changes in method
  • Examine an action plan that produced significant unintended negative effects and analyse what evaluation processes could have identified the risk earlier

Hinge

The ability to identify actions, measures, and effects is prerequisite to developing an evaluated plan in Node 2. Students who cannot distinguish a measure (how progress is tracked) from an action (what is done) cannot build evaluation criteria into their own planning.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents frequently conflate 'what we will do' with 'how we will know it worked'. An explicit scaffold that separates action → measure → intended effect → evaluation builds the analytical vocabulary needed for Node 2's planning work.
