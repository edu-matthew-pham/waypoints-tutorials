What makes a conclusion evidence-based?

AC9HS5S05 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 5 HASS (Foundation to Year 6) · Skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in HASS (Foundation to Year 6): treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain the difference between a conclusion based on evidence and a conclusion based on personal preference, limited sources, or unsupported opinion.

Success criteria

  • Students explain what makes a conclusion 'evidence-based'.
  • Students identify the difference between at least two contrasting conclusions (one evidence-based, one not).

Prior knowledge

From AC9HS4S05 (Conclude from analysis): Students draw basic conclusions from their analysis of information. Students can state what information shows but may not yet explicitly ground conclusions in cited evidence.

Width

Minimum
Explain what 'evidence-based' means and give an example of an evidence-based conclusion versus one that is not.
Typical
Explain the distinction between conclusions based on identified evidence (from valid primary and secondary sources), conclusions based on personal preference, and conclusions based on limited or unreliable sources. Apply these criteria to evaluate examples of student conclusions.
Wider
  • Explore how historians and geographers judge the quality of conclusions in academic writing, and apply these criteria to evaluate the conclusions in a secondary source
  • Investigate cases where initial evidence suggested one conclusion but additional evidence changed it — discussing what this means for inquiry practice

Hinge

Students who don't distinguish evidence-based from opinion-based conclusions will conflate personal judgment with analytical finding throughout their HASS work. This conceptual distinction is the prerequisite for all evidence-based reasoning in Year 5 and beyond.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkUse concrete examples — two conclusions on the same topic, one evidence-based and one opinion-based — and use explicit criteria to identify the difference. The contrast makes the distinction tangible.
