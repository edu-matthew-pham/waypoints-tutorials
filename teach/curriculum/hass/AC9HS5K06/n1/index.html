What is democracy?

AC9HS5K06 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 5 HASS (Foundation to Year 6) · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in HASS (Foundation to Year 6): treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain the meaning of democracy and identify the key values underpinning Australian democracy, including freedom of election, assembly, speech and religion, the rule of law, and human rights.

Success criteria

  • Students explain what democracy means and name at least three key values of Australian democracy.
  • Students give an example of how at least one democratic value applies in a context they are familiar with.

Prior knowledge

From AC9HS4K07 (Rules and laws) and AC9HS4K08 (Local government and services): Students explain why rules and laws exist and how local government provides services to communities.

Width

Minimum
Define democracy and name two key values of Australian democracy.
Typical
Explain the meaning of democracy and describe the range of key values: freedom of election and being elected, freedom of assembly and political participation, freedom of speech and religious belief, rule of law, and basic human rights. Illustrate how these values apply in familiar contexts.
Wider
  • Compare Australian democratic values with those of a non-democratic system of government to highlight what distinguishes democracy
  • Explore the Australian citizenship pledge and the federal government's statement of Australian values, evaluating what these documents reveal about the ideals of Australian democracy

Hinge

Students who don't understand the core values of democracy will treat elections as a procedural mechanism without understanding what they protect or enable. The values framework is the conceptual foundation for understanding why elections, representatives and citizenship responsibilities exist.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkUse concrete, familiar examples (class vote, school council, local election) to illustrate each value. Abstract democratic values become accessible when applied to contexts students already understand.
