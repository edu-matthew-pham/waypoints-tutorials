Push and pull factors for migration

AC9HS6K03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 6 HASS (Foundation to Year 6) · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in HASS (Foundation to Year 6): treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain the push and pull factors that have motivated people to migrate to Australia since Federation, including economic factors, conflict and political refuge.

Success criteria

  • Students identify at least two push factors and two pull factors for migration to Australia.
  • Students apply push/pull factors to at least one specific historical migrant group.

Prior knowledge

From AC9HS5K03 (Individuals and groups in colonial development): Students understand the contributions and experiences of different groups in colonial Australia, including some migrant groups. Students understand historical significance and multi-perspective analysis.

Width

Minimum
Identify two push factors and two pull factors that have driven migration to Australia and give one historical example of each.
Typical
Explain the push and pull factors that have driven migration to Australia since Federation: economic push factors (poverty, unemployment), conflict and political instability as push factors, and Australia's economic opportunities, political stability and immigration programs as pull factors. Apply to specific 20th century migrant groups (e.g. post-WWII Europeans, South-East Asian refugees, Assisted Passage).
Wider
  • Compare the push and pull factors for two different migrant groups from different eras (e.g. post-WWII European migrants and South-East Asian refugees) and explain why their motivations differed
  • Investigate a specific immigration program (e.g. the Assisted Passage Migration scheme, Refugee and Humanitarian Program) and assess how it shaped who migrated and why

Hinge

Students who cannot distinguish push from pull factors will describe migration as a single undifferentiated movement. The push/pull distinction is the analytical foundation for understanding why specific groups migrated when they did.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkUse a push/pull T-chart with specific examples for at least two different migrant groups before moving to analysis. Visual scaffolding makes the distinction clear and avoids conflation.
