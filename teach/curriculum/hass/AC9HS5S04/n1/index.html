Origin and purpose of sources

AC9HS5S04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 5 HASS (Foundation to Year 6) · Skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in HASS (Foundation to Year 6): treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify the origin of a source (who created it, when, where) and explain its likely purpose (to inform, persuade, entertain, record), and explain how origin and purpose might affect the information a source contains.

Success criteria

  • Students identify the origin (creator, date) and purpose of at least one source.
  • Students explain how the source's origin and purpose might affect what it shows or omits.

Prior knowledge

From AC9HS4S04 (Analyse data and perspectives): Students analyse data and identify different perspectives in sources. Students can identify viewpoints in sources, but at Year 4 level do not yet evaluate origin and purpose systematically.

Width

Minimum
Identify the origin and purpose of one provided source and explain how these might affect what the source shows.
Typical
For a range of primary and secondary sources, identify origin (creator, date, context) and purpose (informative, persuasive, record-keeping), and explain how origin and purpose affect the reliability, completeness and perspective presented in each source.
Wider
  • Compare a primary source (e.g. a colonial-era newspaper article) and a secondary source (e.g. a modern history book) on the same topic, and evaluate how origin and purpose result in different accounts
  • Investigate how to verify source details — checking publication dates, authorship and institutional affiliation — and evaluate what this reveals about the reliability of different source types

Hinge

Students who cannot identify origin and purpose cannot evaluate perspective or bias — the evaluative skills in the rest of this descriptor and in AC9HS5S05 depend on understanding that sources are created by someone for a reason that shapes their content.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkUse a source analysis frame (e.g. Who? When? Why? What does this mean for the information?) to scaffold the move from describing what a source says to analysing what it is and why it was created.
