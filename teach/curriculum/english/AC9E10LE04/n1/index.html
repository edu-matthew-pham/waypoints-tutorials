Identifying ethical positions and world views

AC9E10LE04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 10 English · Literature · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify the ethical positions and world views present in a literary text, distinguishing between those challenged, presented, reinforced or privileged.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least one ethical position or world view in a literary text
  • Describes whether the text challenges, reinforces or privileges that position

Prior knowledge

Students analyse texts and evaluate the aesthetic qualities and appeal of an author's literary style (AC9E9LE04). They have not yet evaluated the explicit ethical positions or world views that may be challenged or reinforced by literary texts from a range of contexts.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least one ethical position or world view in a provided literary text and describe whether it is challenged, reinforced or privileged.
Typical
Identify and analyse the ethical positions and world views across a literary text, explaining how the text positions each — what is challenged, what is reinforced, what is taken for granted.
Wider
  • Compare the ethical positions presented in texts from different cultural contexts about the same issue (e.g. justice, loyalty, belonging)
  • Examine how First Nations Australian literary texts challenge or reframe ethical positions that are privileged in mainstream Australian literature
  • Analyse how a text's genre or form shapes which ethical positions it is capable of challenging or reinforcing

Hinge

Node 2 requires students to evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of ethical positions — a move that presupposes students have identified and distinguished the text's relationship to those positions. Without this, evaluation has no object.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkDistinguishing between positions that are challenged versus reinforced versus merely presented requires explicit reasoning scaffolding — students tend to identify positions but struggle to analyse the text's relationship to each position.
