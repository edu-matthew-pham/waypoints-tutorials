Nominalisation and the construction of events

AC9E10LA06 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 10 English · Language · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify nominalisation in texts and analyse how it affects the way events are constructed and what information is made explicit or implicit.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least one nominalisation in a text
  • Explains how the nominalisation affects what information about the event or agent is made explicit or hidden

Prior knowledge

Students understand how abstract nouns and nominalisation summarise ideas in texts (AC9E9LA06). They have not yet analysed how meaning and style are achieved through syntax including nominalisations, clause combinations and noun group abstraction.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least one nominalisation in a provided text and explain how it affects what information about the event is made explicit or hidden.
Typical
Analyse how nominalisation across a range of academic, journalistic or political texts constructs events, making some actors and causes explicit and others implicit.
Wider
  • Compare how nominalisation is used in scientific texts versus political texts, analysing how each uses nominalisation to construct authority and objectivity
  • Examine how nominalisation in news reports can obscure agency and responsibility for events
  • Analyse how nominalisation functions in First Nations Australian legal or advocacy documents to establish formal register and conceptual authority

Hinge

Node 2 (clause combinations) and Node 3 (noun group abstraction) both depend on students understanding how nominalisation packages information — without this, analysis of complex clause and noun group structures has no grounding in how syntactic choice constructs meaning.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepNominalisation’s effect on meaning is highly context-dependent — its function in political language (obscuring agents) differs from its function in science (creating technical vocabulary); students need contrasting contexts to see the range.
