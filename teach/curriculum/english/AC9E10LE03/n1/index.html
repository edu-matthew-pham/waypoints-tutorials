Identifying aesthetic qualities and their sources

AC9E10LE03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 10 English · Literature · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify aesthetic qualities in a literary text and trace them to specific text structures, language features, literary devices or visual features.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least one aesthetic quality in a text
  • Traces that quality to a specific text feature (structural, linguistic, literary or visual)

Prior knowledge

Students analyse how features of literary texts influence reader preferences (AC9E9LE03). They have not yet analysed how aesthetic qualities, context and text features combine to influence audience response.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least one aesthetic quality in a provided text and trace it to the specific feature (language, structural or visual) that creates it.
Typical
Identify a range of aesthetic qualities in a literary text and analyse how different features — structural, linguistic, literary and visual — combine to produce those qualities.
Wider
  • Compare the aesthetic qualities of texts experienced in different modes (e.g. a poem read silently versus read aloud versus set to music) and analyse how mode of experience affects aesthetic response
  • Examine how the aesthetic qualities of First Nations Australian texts may be constructed through features not typically foregrounded in Western literary analysis (e.g. oral performance conventions)
  • Analyse how the aesthetic qualities of a text change when adapted from one medium to another

Hinge

Node 2 requires students to analyse how context of experience shapes response — a move that depends on being able to connect features to effects. Students who cannot trace aesthetic qualities to their sources will analyse context as separate from the text rather than interacting with it.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents tend to describe aesthetic responses ('I found it powerful') without identifying the specific features producing those responses — the reasoning from effect back to cause needs explicit scaffolding.
