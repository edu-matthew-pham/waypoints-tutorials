Experimenting with figurative language

AC9E5LE05 n2 · waypoint 2 of 4 · Year 5 English · Literature · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can deliberately experiment with figurative language — simile, metaphor, personification — in their literary writing, making choices about which type of imagery to use and where to use it.

Success criteria

  • Includes at least two examples of figurative language (using different types) in their literary text.
  • Can explain the intended effect of each figurative language choice — the choice is deliberate, not accidental.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Reading as a writer: noticing craft choices.

Width

Minimum
In a draft literary text, include at least two examples of deliberately chosen figurative language (at least two different types) and explain the effect intended with each choice.
Typical
Draft a literary text that demonstrates deliberate use of figurative language — simile, metaphor and/or personification — at key points. Explain the craft choices made: why this image here, what comparison is intended, what effect is sought. Draw on imagery from model texts as inspiration or starting points.
Wider
  • Experiment with the same scene or description using different types of figurative language and compare the effects — developing a feel for when simile vs. metaphor vs. personification serves the moment better
  • Experiment with extended metaphor across a short piece, developing a central comparison and sustaining it throughout

Hinge

Node 3 requires students to draw on figurative language experimentation as source material for extended narrative creation, and Node 4 requires them to edit figurative language for effect. Students who have not yet made deliberate figurative language choices cannot meaningfully attempt either — their drafts will lack the intentional imagery that editing and narrative integration depends on.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents often produce figurative language that is generic ('the sun was like a ball') rather than purposeful. Encountering a range of model images — including unexpected comparisons, culturally specific imagery, and imagery from texts that have been studied — helps students develop a broader sense of what is possible and what makes an image work.
