Planning with purpose in mind

AC9E7LY06 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 7 English · Literacy · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can plan a text by identifying its purpose, audience and the structural and language choices that will serve them

Success criteria

  • Identifies the purpose and audience for a given writing task
  • Names at least one structural and one language feature choice that serves the purpose and explains why

Prior knowledge

Students can plan, create, edit and publish written and multimodal texts using paragraphs, complex sentences, expanded verb groups, tense, topic-specific vocabulary, punctuation and visual features (AC9E6LY06). They have not yet worked at the level of deliberately selecting text structures, language features and literary devices to convey specific purposes — the move from using these elements to selecting them purposefully.

Width

Minimum
Given a writing task, identify the purpose and audience; name at least one text structure and one language feature that would be appropriate and explain why
Typical
Plan a text by mapping purpose and audience to structural choices and language features — what text type, what stages, what vocabulary register, what sentence complexity is appropriate, and why each choice serves the purpose
Wider
  • Compare how the same subject matter would be planned differently for different purposes — an imaginative recount vs a persuasive argument vs an analytical response — and discuss how purpose drives every planning decision
  • Examine how compiling a portfolio of texts for a common concept or audience requires consistent structural and language choices across different text types

Hinge

If students cannot connect purpose and audience to structural and language choices at the planning stage, Nodes 2 and 3 lack the deliberate decision-making frame — creation and editing both depend on having an articulated intention to evaluate against.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly plan what to write (content) without planning how to write it (structure and language). The reasoning scaffold — purpose → audience → appropriate structure → appropriate language features — establishes the decision chain that makes planning purposeful rather than content-listing.
