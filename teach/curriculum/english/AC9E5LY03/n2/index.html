Characters in persuasive texts

AC9E5LY03 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Year 5 English · Literacy · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain how characters are used in persuasive texts to deliver a message, and analyse why real people might be replaced by constructed characters.

Success criteria

  • Explains why characters are used in a persuasive text and connects that choice to the persuasive purpose.
  • Analyses what the character choice communicates to the audience beyond the explicit message.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Features serve purpose.

Width

Minimum
Identify a text where characters are used to deliver a persuasive message and explain how the character choice serves the persuasive purpose.
Typical
Explain how characters function as persuasive vehicles in texts such as advertising or advocacy, discussing why characters (rather than factual claims) are used and what the character choice communicates to the audience. Apply this to at least one health or community advocacy example.
Wider
  • Compare persuasive texts that use real people versus constructed characters to make the same argument — discussing what each choice does for the persuasive strategy and audience response
  • Examine how First Nations Australian community advocacy texts use character and voice, and discuss how the choice of spokesperson reflects community values and communication conventions

Hinge

Node 3 requires students to explain how multiple characteristic features of a text type work together. Students who have not yet understood why particular choices are made — not just that characters appear in persuasive texts, but why this is more effective than other options — cannot produce the analytical accounts of feature function that Node 3 demands.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe reasoning chain — 'characters are used instead of real people because... and this achieves... for the audience' — requires explicit scaffolding. Students often accept that characters are used without reasoning about why that choice is more persuasively effective than other options.
