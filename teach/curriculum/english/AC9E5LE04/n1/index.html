Imagery: comparison and new ways of seeing

AC9E5LE04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 5 English · Literature · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify simile, metaphor and personification in texts and explain how each works through comparison to create a new way of seeing something.

Success criteria

  • Identifies an example of simile, metaphor and personification and explains the comparison each makes.
  • Explains what new understanding or experience the comparison creates for the reader — not just names the device.

Prior knowledge

Students can examine the use of literary devices and deliberate word play including imagery and rhythm in literary texts (AC9E4LE04). They have not yet examined how specific imagery types — simile, metaphor, personification — create meaning through comparison, or how sound devices create effects in poetry and song.

Width

Minimum
Identify examples of simile, metaphor and personification in a text and explain how each creates a comparison and what that comparison helps the reader understand or experience.
Typical
Identify and explain the working mechanism of simile (explicit comparison using 'like' or 'as'), metaphor (implicit comparison asserting identity), and personification (attributing human qualities to non-human things) across narrative, poetry and song examples. Explain how each device creates a new way of looking at the world.
Wider
  • Examine how simile and metaphor can be used to explore the same concept in different ways — comparing examples and discussing what each comparison makes visible that others do not
  • Investigate extended metaphors in texts and discuss how sustaining a comparison across a passage or poem creates particular effects

Hinge

Understanding how each imagery type works through comparison is prerequisite for Node 2's analysis of effect. Students who cannot explain the comparison mechanism — what is being compared to what, and why — cannot reason about the specific emotional, sensory, or conceptual effect the comparison creates for the reader.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents often identify imagery types as labels without understanding the comparison mechanism — 'that's a simile' without explaining what is being compared to what and what insight the comparison creates. Encountering diverse examples across narrative, poetry and song helps students develop the analytical move from identification to explanation of effect.
