Comprehension strategies for evaluation

AC9E8LY05 n2 · waypoint 2 of 2 · Year 8 English · Literacy

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can use comprehension strategies to evaluate ideas in texts — assessing the credibility, effectiveness and significance of content, including evaluating the selection and composition of images for their effect on credibility.

Success criteria

  • Applies at least one comprehension strategy to evaluate an idea or image in a text.
  • States a clear evaluative judgement (credible/not credible, effective/not effective) with reasoning that references specific textual or visual evidence.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Comprehension strategies for deeper reading.

Width

Minimum
Apply at least one comprehension strategy to evaluate an idea in a text — assessing whether it is credible, well-supported or effectively communicated.
Typical
Use comprehension strategies to evaluate the content of texts, including determining criteria for evaluating website content, assessing the credibility of information and evaluating the selection and composition of images for their effect on the credibility or meaning of a text.
Wider
  • Develop and apply explicit evaluation criteria for a class of texts (e.g. websites on a health topic) and use comprehension strategies to systematically evaluate multiple texts against those criteria
  • Investigate how the selection and framing of images in a news website affects reader credibility judgements — examining which images are selected, how they are cropped and what associations they trigger
  • Evaluate how cross-referencing information across texts (connecting strategy) can reveal inconsistencies or contradictions between sources — and what to do when reliable sources disagree

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkEvaluation requires applying criteria, not just responding with a gut reaction. The structured reasoning scaffold — what criteria am I using? what does the evidence show against those criteria? what is my evaluation? — needs to be made explicit before students can evaluate credibly rather than impressionistically.
