Direct vs indirect evaluation

AC9E9LA02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 9 English · Language · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can distinguish between evaluations expressed directly (explicit appraisal language) and those embedded indirectly through word choice and framing.

Success criteria

  • Student identifies at least one direct and one indirect evaluation in a text
  • Student explains how indirect evaluation positions the reader without using explicit appraisal terms

Prior knowledge

Students recognise evaluative language and understand how evaluations are substantiated by textual evidence (AC9E7LA02). They have not yet examined how evaluation can be embedded indirectly through literary devices that position readers without explicit appraisal language.

Width

Minimum
Identify one example of direct evaluation and one of indirect evaluation in a text, and explain how they differ in the way they communicate the author's position.
Typical
Compare direct and indirect evaluation across two text types (e.g. editorial and advertisement, podcast and poem), explaining how each mode positions the reader differently.
Wider
  • Analyse how advertising deliberately avoids direct evaluative claims while using indirect devices to position consumers
  • Compare the evaluative intensity of direct versus indirect appraisal in political speech, examining which is more persuasive and why

Hinge

The direct/indirect distinction is a prerequisite for N2 and N3 — students who cannot identify indirect evaluation cannot analyse evocative vocabulary or metaphor as evaluative devices at those nodes.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents default to identifying explicit evaluative language ('great', 'terrible') and miss indirect embedding. Comparing diverse text types — especially non-literary ones like advertisements — reveals the fuller range of how evaluation works.
