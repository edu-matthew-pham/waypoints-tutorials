← AC9E8LA03 Text structure, purpose and hybrid genres

Structure and language features serve purpose

AC9E8LA03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 8 English · Language · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain how the structural choices and language features of a specific text type are designed to achieve its communicative purpose.

Success criteria

  • Names the purpose of a given text and identifies at least two structural or language features that serve that purpose.
  • Explains why those features are suited to the text's purpose rather than simply labelling them.

Prior knowledge

Students can understand how different text types are structured to achieve different purposes and identify how language features vary across text types (AC9E7LA03). They have not yet examined how texts combine multiple genre structures or analysed hybrid texts that deliberately blend conventions.

Width

Minimum
Explain how the structure and at least two language features of one text type (e.g. critical response, exposition) work together to achieve its purpose.
Typical
Explain how texts such as critical responses, expositions, interpretations, discussions, literary recounts and memoirs are structured and how their language features serve their communicative purposes, with attention to how placement of images and written text may vary in print vs online formats.
Wider
  • Compare two texts with similar purposes but different structures (e.g. a written exposition vs a formal speech) and explain how structural variation affects meaning
  • Examine how formal speeches use structure and language features differently from written argument to achieve persuasive effect with a live audience
  • Analyse how the placement of images and written text in non-linear online texts (e.g. news websites) creates different reading pathways and affects the text's purpose

Hinge

Node 2 requires students to recognise that two genre conventions are being combined in a hybrid text — which requires knowing what those conventions are in the first place. Students who have not built a working repertoire of distinct text-type conventions cannot identify what is being hybridised.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Repertoire building: more cases to sort or classifyStudents need to accumulate a working repertoire of text types and their conventions. Encountering a range of text structures — not just one — builds the comparative lens needed for the hybrid genre concept at node 2.
