Types of logical relationship

AC9E5LA05 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 5 English · Language · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify the type of logical relationship a dependent clause creates (reason, purpose, condition, concession, time) and match subordinating conjunctions to the relationships they signal.

Success criteria

  • Correctly identifies the relationship type (reason, purpose, condition, concession, or time) for at least four examples of complex sentences.
  • Names the subordinating conjunction and explains what logical work it performs in at least two examples.

Prior knowledge

Students can understand that complex sentences contain one independent clause and at least one dependent clause, joined by a subordinating conjunction (AC9E4LA06). They have not yet examined how the specific type of dependent clause — reason, purpose, condition, concession, time — creates a particular logical relationship, or how the ordering of clauses affects emphasis and effect.

Width

Minimum
Given examples of complex sentences, identify the type of logical relationship the dependent clause establishes and name the subordinating conjunction that creates it.
Typical
Identify and explain the five types of logical relationship (reason, purpose, condition, concession, time) across a range of complex sentence examples. Match subordinating conjunctions to relationship types and explain what logical work each conjunction performs.
Wider
  • Collect examples of each relationship type from texts across different genres — narrative, argument, explanation — and discuss how the distribution of relationship types reflects the text's purpose
  • Examine how multiple complex sentence types appear in a single paragraph and discuss how the pattern of logical relationships builds the paragraph's argument or narrative

Hinge

Node 2's clause-ordering analysis and Node 3's effect explanation both require students to know which type of logical relationship they are working with. Students who conflate condition and concession, or purpose and reason, will make errors in Node 2 when comparing clause orderings and will be unable to explain the specific effect a complex sentence creates in Node 3.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Consolidation: more practise steps, short feedback loopsStudents commonly confuse the five relationship types, particularly condition vs. concession and purpose vs. reason. Consolidation through varied examples — focusing on what question the dependent clause answers — builds the reliable identification needed before examining clause ordering and effect.
