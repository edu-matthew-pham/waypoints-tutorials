Paragraph internal structure

AC9E8LA04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 8 English · Language · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify the structural components of a well-formed paragraph (claim/topic sentence, example, evidence/quotation, substantiation/explanation) and explain how each component contributes to the paragraph's coherence.

Success criteria

  • Identifies the claim, at least one piece of evidence (example or quotation) and the substantiation step in a given paragraph.
  • Explains how the substantiation connects the evidence back to the paragraph's claim.

Prior knowledge

Students can identify how cohesive devices (e.g. connectives, reference chains) create flow within and between paragraphs (AC9E7LA04). They have not yet examined how the internal structure of paragraphs — claim, example, quotation, substantiation — creates the logical coherence that cohesion depends on.

Width

Minimum
Identify the claim, example and substantiation in one provided paragraph and explain how they work together to develop a point.
Typical
Identify and explain how claims, examples, quotations and substantiation function together in extended analytical paragraphs, including understanding how the substantiation step connects evidence back to the claim.
Wider
  • Compare a well-substantiated paragraph with one that makes a claim but lacks explanation, and analyse what is lost without the substantiation step
  • Examine how academic writing in different disciplines (e.g. science report vs literary analysis) uses different conventions for substantiating claims
  • Annotate a peer's or published paragraph, identifying each structural component and evaluating the effectiveness of the substantiation

Hinge

Node 2 requires students to write substantiated paragraphs and explain how they improve textual cohesion — a consolidation task that depends entirely on understanding the claim-example-substantiation chain at Node 1. Students who have not grasped the structure have nothing to consolidate.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly write claim + quote with no explanation, treating quotation as self-evidently supporting the claim. Explicit scaffolding of the claim-example-substantiation chain — and why each step matters — is needed before students can execute it reliably.
