← AC9E6LE01 Responses to characters and events across contexts

Context shapes response

AC9E6LE01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 6 English · Literature · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain how the historical, social or cultural context of a text may influence how readers respond to characters, actions and events.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least one way that a text's context shapes a reader's response to a character or event
  • Explains how a reader from a different time or context might respond differently to the same character or event

Prior knowledge

Students can identify aspects of literary texts that represent details or information about historical, social and cultural experiences (AC9E5LE01). They have not yet examined how the historical, social or cultural context of a text shapes responses to its characters and events, nor read widely across First Nations Australian, Australian and world authors.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least one way that the historical, social or cultural context of a provided text shapes how readers respond to a character or event; explain why a response might differ from a reader of a different time or context.
Typical
Discuss how historical, social and cultural experiences embedded in a text may produce different responses to characters and events; explain how a reader's own context also shapes their response.
Wider
  • Compare responses to the same character from a contemporary reader and a reader from the historical context of the text
  • Examine how responses to heroes in texts from different cultural contexts (e.g. First Nations Australian texts vs. Western adventure narratives) reflect different values

Hinge

Students who treat their own response as universal cannot engage meaningfully with diverse authors in Node 2 or evaluate others' responses in Node 3 — both require the foundational understanding that response is contextually situated.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkMoving from 'I respond to this character in this way' to 'I respond this way because of my context, and someone from a different context might respond differently' requires explicit scaffolding. Students often treat their response as universal without prompting to consider its situatedness.
