← AC9E7LA05 Complex and compound-complex sentences

Sentence structure as a deliberate choice

AC9E7LA05 n4 · waypoint 4 of 4 · Year 7 English · Language

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can understand how writers choose between simple, complex and compound-complex sentences to serve different expressive purposes

Lessons on this site

  • Choose the shape for the job

Success criteria

  • Identifies where a writer has used a complex or compound-complex sentence and gives a reason for the choice
  • Explains why varying sentence structure serves the text's purpose better than using one sentence type throughout

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 3: Elaborating, extending and explaining.

Width

Minimum
Given a short passage, identify where the writer has used a complex or compound-complex sentence and explain why a simpler sentence would have been less effective at that point
Typical
Examine how a writer varies sentence structure across a passage — when they choose simple sentences for impact and when they choose complex or compound-complex sentences to carry more meaning; discuss the cumulative effect
Wider
  • Explore the ACARA elaboration insight that a simple sentence can express sophisticated ideas and a complex sentence need not express 'complex' ideas — find examples in published texts that challenge the assumption that longer = more sophisticated
  • Examine how writers use sentence structure variation for rhythm and reader engagement, not just logical development of ideas

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepThe misconception that sentence sophistication equals sentence length is persistent and cuts across the whole descriptor. Students need to see enough counterexamples — powerful simple sentences alongside unwieldy complex ones — across varied text types before this understanding sticks. A single example is not enough to dislodge the length-equals-sophistication assumption.
