Representation as construction

AC9E9LE01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 9 English · Literature · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify that representations of people and places in literary texts are constructed — the product of authorial choices — not neutral or natural.

Success criteria

  • Student identifies authorial choices (not just character attributes) in the representation
  • Student connects at least one choice to a likely contextual influence (historical, cultural, social)

Prior knowledge

Students identify and explore ideas, viewpoints and issues in literary texts across historical, social and cultural contexts (AC9E7LE01). They have not yet analysed how representations of people and places are constructed — examining the choices authors make and what those choices reveal about context.

Width

Minimum
Identify two choices an author has made in representing a character or place and explain what those choices suggest about the author's perspective or context.
Typical
Analyse how an author's choices construct a representation of a person or place, examining vocabulary, characterisation and narrative framing as constructed rather than transparent.
Wider
  • Compare two representations of the same type of person or place (e.g. rural Australia, an immigrant community) from different time periods, examining how the construction differs
  • Examine how First Nations Australian authors construct representations that reflect unique ways of being, knowing, thinking and doing, and contrast with representations in mainstream Australian literature

Hinge

The shift from 'the character is like this' to 'the author has constructed this character this way' is prerequisite for N2 — analysing how context shapes representation only makes sense if representations are already understood as constructed choices.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepThe conceptual shift from 'the character is like this' to 'the author has constructed the character this way, and here's why' is a genuine hinge. Students resist this shift because it feels like it undermines the reality of the story. Wider cultural and historical contexts — particularly First Nations Australian authorship — help students see that representations are perspectives, not mirrors.
