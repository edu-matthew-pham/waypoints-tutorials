Symbolism and metonymy

AC9E9LE05 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Year 9 English · Literature

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can analyse how symbols and metonymy create meaning in literary texts by substituting part for whole, or associating object with concept.

Success criteria

  • Student identifies a symbol or metonymic usage and names the concept or whole it represents
  • Student explains the specific meaning-effect the device creates — not just labels it

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Extended metaphor and allegory.

Width

Minimum
Identify a symbol or metonymy in a literary text and explain the meaning it creates through association rather than direct statement.
Typical
Analyse how symbolism and metonymy operate in a range of literary texts — identifying imagery, examining examples of authors' use of devices to create meaning, and explaining the effect on audiences.
Wider
  • Compare how the same symbol carries different meanings across cultural contexts — examining where symbolic meanings are shared and where they diverge
  • Analyse how a poet's use of metonymy creates compressed meaning by asking what the part stands for and what it omits

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepSymbols and metonymy are easiest to recognise in explicit literary examples but harder to spot in subtler texts. Encountering them across poetry, prose and visual contexts builds pattern recognition that prevents students from treating each instance as isolated rather than as a device type.
