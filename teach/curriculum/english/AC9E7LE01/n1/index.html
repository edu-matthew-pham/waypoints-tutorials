Identifying ideas and issues in context

AC9E7LE01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 7 English · Literature · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify the ideas, issues or points of view present in a literary text and connect them to the text's historical, social or cultural context

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least one significant idea, issue or point of view in a literary text
  • Connects the idea to the historical, social or cultural context of the text

Prior knowledge

Students can identify responses to characters and events in literary texts drawn from historical, social or cultural contexts (AC9E6LE01). They have not yet moved from identifying responses to exploring ideas, points of view and issues — the shift from response to analysis of the ideas themselves.

Width

Minimum
In a given literary text, identify at least one significant idea, issue or point of view; explain how it connects to the context in which the text was created
Typical
Identify multiple ideas, issues and points of view in a literary text and examine how each is shaped by the historical, social or cultural context — what does the text reveal about the world it came from?
Wider
  • Explore ideas about heroism in literary texts by wide-ranging world authors — how does context shape what counts as heroic?
  • Explore depictions of the city or the bush in Australian poems and short stories from different eras — how has the representation of these settings changed across historical contexts?

Hinge

If students cannot connect ideas to context, Node 2's comparative work collapses — comparison across contexts requires first being able to situate ideas within a single context.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents commonly read literary texts as universal without recognising how context shapes what ideas are foregrounded, what counts as a problem, and whose perspective is centred. Reading across genuinely different historical, social and cultural contexts — including First Nations Australian texts and wide-ranging world authors — is what builds the understanding that all texts are contextually situated.
