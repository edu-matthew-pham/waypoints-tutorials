← AC9E5LY08 Shared patterns, different sounds

Recognising pattern–pronunciation divergence

AC9E5LY08 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 5 English · Literacy · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify words that share a common letter pattern but have different pronunciations, and explain that multiple pronunciation options are possible for the same pattern.

Success criteria

  • Identifies a letter pattern that can be pronounced in at least two ways and provides correct examples of each pronunciation.
  • Explains that phonic knowledge alone is not sufficient for these words — vocabulary or morphemic knowledge is also needed.

Prior knowledge

Students can use and apply phonological and morphological knowledge to read and spell complex letter combinations (AC9E4LY09). They have not yet focused on the specific challenge of words that share written patterns but diverge in pronunciation — cross-pattern reading requiring multiple knowledge sources.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least one letter pattern that can be pronounced in more than one way and provide word examples of each pronunciation.
Typical
Identify and group words with common letter patterns that have different pronunciations (e.g. 'journey', 'your', 'tour', 'sour'). Explain that the same written pattern can map to different spoken sounds, and that phonic knowledge alone is insufficient for accurate reading of these words.
Wider
  • Investigate other letter patterns with multiple pronunciations across English — examining how the range of pattern–sound mappings reflects the diverse origins of English vocabulary
  • Compare how pattern–pronunciation relationships differ between words derived from different language origins (e.g. French-derived vs Anglo-Saxon-derived words) and discuss what this reveals about English spelling history

Hinge

Node 2 requires students to apply phonic, morphemic and vocabulary knowledge together specifically because phonic knowledge alone is insufficient for pattern-divergent words. Students who have not grasped that the same pattern can map to multiple pronunciations will default to a single-source phonic strategy in Node 2 rather than engaging the multi-source reasoning the node demands.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents may apply a single pronunciation rule to a pattern and not monitor when the result does not match a known word. Encountering diverse examples of pattern–pronunciation divergence helps students develop monitoring awareness and builds their lexical bank of known exceptions.
