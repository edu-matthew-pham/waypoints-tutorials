← AC9E5LY08 Shared patterns, different sounds

Using multiple knowledge sources

AC9E5LY08 n2 · waypoint 2 of 2 · Year 5 English · Literacy

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can draw on phonic, morphemic and vocabulary knowledge together to determine the correct pronunciation or spelling of words with ambiguous letter patterns.

Success criteria

  • Can explain which knowledge source was decisive in determining the pronunciation or spelling of a specific word with an ambiguous pattern.
  • Demonstrates monitoring — recognises when a phonically-derived pronunciation does not match a known word and applies an alternative strategy.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Recognising pattern–pronunciation divergence.

Width

Minimum
For at least two examples of words with ambiguous letter patterns, explain which knowledge source (phonic, morphemic, vocabulary) provides the key information for determining correct pronunciation or spelling.
Typical
Apply phonic, morphemic and vocabulary knowledge in combination when reading or spelling words with ambiguous patterns. Explain the decision-making process: what the phonic pattern suggests, what morphemic knowledge adds, and how vocabulary knowledge (knowing the word) confirms or overrides.
Wider
  • Develop a personal 'tricky pattern' vocabulary — collecting words that required multiple knowledge sources and annotating what each knowledge source contributed to reading and spelling them accurately
  • Examine how encountering a new word with an ambiguous pattern in a reading context provides enough vocabulary knowledge to add the word to the known-word base

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe three-knowledge-source reasoning process — phonic, morphemic, vocabulary — needs to be made explicit. Students who rely on one source in isolation will fail at pattern-divergent words. A structured reasoning scaffold ('what does the pattern suggest? what does the word part/meaning suggest? what does knowing this word suggest?') builds reliable multi-source decoding.
