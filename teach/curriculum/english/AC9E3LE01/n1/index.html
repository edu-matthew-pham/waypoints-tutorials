← AC9E3LE01 Characters and events across contexts

Same story, different tellings

AC9E3LE01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 3 English · Literature

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can discuss how the same story or character type is told differently by different authors, identifying variations in characters, events and settings.

Success criteria

  • Student identifies at least two specific differences in character, event or setting across two versions of a story
  • Student discusses one reason why the versions might differ — not just listing differences

Prior knowledge

Students discuss how characters and settings are connected in literature from diverse contexts (AC9E2LE01). They have not yet compared how the same character type (e.g. the wolf) or story element is portrayed differently across texts from different cultural contexts.

Width

Minimum
Compare two versions of the same story or story type and identify at least two differences in how characters, events or settings are presented.
Typical
Explore how different wide-ranging authors tell the same story — identifying variations in character portrayal, setting and events; discuss how the same character type (e.g. the wolf) is portrayed differently in different versions.
Wider
  • Compare a traditional story told by a First Nations Australian author with a mainstream version, discussing what is similar and what reflects different relationships with Country, family and community
  • Discuss why different authors might portray the same character type differently — connecting authorial choice to cultural perspective and purpose

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents easily accept a single version of a story as 'the real one'. Seeing multiple tellings — especially cross-cultural ones — reveals that character, setting and event are constructed choices, not fixed facts. This is a conceptual shift that multiple examples are needed to secure.
