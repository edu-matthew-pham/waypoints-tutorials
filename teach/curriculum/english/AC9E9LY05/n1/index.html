Comprehension strategies for comparison

AC9E9LY05 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 9 English · Literacy · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can select and apply comprehension strategies specifically for the purpose of comparing ideas and opinions within and across texts.

Success criteria

  • Student applies at least two named comprehension strategies and explains how each supported comparison
  • Student identifies at least one similarity and one difference between ideas or opinions in the texts compared

Prior knowledge

Students use comprehension strategies including inferring to analyse and summarise information and ideas (AC9E7LY05). They have not yet deployed strategies specifically to compare and contrast ideas and opinions across multiple texts simultaneously.

Width

Minimum
Apply at least two comprehension strategies (e.g. summarising, questioning, inferring) to compare ideas or opinions in two texts, explaining how each strategy supported the comparison.
Typical
Apply a range of comprehension strategies — visualising, predicting, connecting, summarising, monitoring, questioning and inferring — to compare and contrast visual representations, print and digital sources, and current event representations.
Wider
  • Develop a comparison protocol: which strategies are most useful for comparing visual versus verbal texts, and why?
  • Apply comprehension strategies to compare texts with conflicting interpretations of the same event, evaluating the quality of the evidence each presents

Hinge

Cross-text comparison at N2 depends on being able to deploy multiple strategies flexibly — students defaulting to one strategy produce shallow comparisons that miss the multiple dimensions needed for the descriptor's comparison task.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Repertoire building: more cases to sort or classifyStudents often default to one or two favoured comprehension strategies. Building a full repertoire of all seven named strategies — and matching strategy to comparison purpose — expands the analytical toolkit for cross-text comparison.
