Suffixes change grammatical form

AC9E5LY10 n2 · waypoint 2 of 2 · Year 5 English · Literacy

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain how specific suffixes change the grammatical form of a word and use this knowledge to build and recognise different word classes.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two suffixes that change grammatical form and gives correct examples of each.
  • Explains what grammatical change each suffix creates — not just names the suffix and a word.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Less common plurals and word origins.

Width

Minimum
Identify how at least two suffixes change the grammatical form of a word (e.g. '-tion' changing a verb to a noun) and provide two correct examples for each suffix.
Typical
Explain how suffixes such as '-tion', '-ment', '-ness', '-er/-or', and '-ful/-less' change the grammatical class of words — converting verbs to nouns (nominalisation), adjectives to nouns, or words to other classes. Use this knowledge to recognise and build word forms in reading and writing contexts.
Wider
  • Examine how nominalisation (verb-to-noun conversion via suffixes) is used in informational and academic texts to build compact, precise noun phrases — and discuss what nominalisations do to text register
  • Compare how the same base word generates different forms through different suffixes and explore what each form does grammatically (e.g. 'protect' → 'protection', 'protective', 'protectively', 'protector')

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe connection between suffix and grammatical class change — and why that matters for reading and writing — requires explicit reasoning. Students who learn suffix patterns as lists without understanding the grammatical function miss the utility of the knowledge. A structured reasoning scaffold ('this suffix changes X class to Y class, which means in a sentence it can...') develops functional understanding.
