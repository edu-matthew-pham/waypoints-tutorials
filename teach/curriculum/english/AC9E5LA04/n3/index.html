Giving prominence through starting position

AC9E5LA04 n3 · waypoint 3 of 3 · Year 5 English · Language

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain how placing particular information at the start of a sentence or paragraph gives that information prominence, and identify why a writer has made that choice.

Lessons on this site

  • Where a sentence starts

Success criteria

  • Identifies an element in starting position in a text and explains what prominence it receives as a result.
  • Explains why the writer chose to position that element first, connecting the choice to the text's purpose or the reader's expected path through the text.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Theme position creates cohesion.

Width

Minimum
Identify in at least two examples how moving an element to the starting position of a sentence changes what receives emphasis, and explain what the writer achieves by that positioning.
Typical
Analyse and explain how writers use starting position to give prominence to time, concession, reason, contrast or topic across a range of text types. Apply this understanding to explain specific writer choices in a text, connecting the positioning decision to the text's communicative purpose.
Wider
  • Experiment with repositioning elements in sentences from a given text and discuss how each repositioning changes the emphasis and the reader's experience of the text
  • Examine how different text types use different types of elements in the prominent starting position (e.g. time in recount, concession in argument, topic in report) and explain what this reflects about each text type's purpose

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents often understand that the start of a sentence is 'what it's about' but do not see prominence as an active writer choice. Encountering multiple examples where writers have moved different elements — concession, reason, time, contrast — to starting position helps students recognise prominence as deliberate and purposeful, not accidental.
Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkExplaining why a writer chose to give prominence to a particular element requires reasoning from writer intent through structural choice to reader effect. This chain — 'the writer moved X to the front because... and the reader experiences...' — needs to be made explicit.
