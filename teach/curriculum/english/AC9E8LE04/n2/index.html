Intertextuality and aesthetic quality

AC9E8LE04 n2 · waypoint 2 of 2 · Year 8 English · Literature

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain how an intertextual reference in a literary text enables new understanding of the text's aesthetic quality — how the reference enriches the reader's appreciation of the text's meaning, craft or effect.

Success criteria

  • Explains how a specific intertextual reference enriches the reader's understanding or appreciation of a literary text.
  • Uses appropriate aesthetic vocabulary (e.g. 'layers of meaning', 'resonance', 'allusion', 'aesthetic effect') in the explanation.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Identifying literary intertextuality.

Width

Minimum
Explain how one intertextual reference in a literary text adds to the reader's understanding or appreciation of the text's aesthetic quality — what the reference enables that a reading without knowledge of the source would miss.
Typical
Identify intertextual references through allusion or quotation in literary texts and explain how knowledge of other texts influences the reader's understanding and appreciation of the aesthetic quality of the text being read.
Wider
  • Analyse how sustained intertextual allusiveness (multiple references to a single source tradition) shapes the aesthetic identity of a literary text as a whole
  • Investigate how a parody or creative response to a canonical text generates aesthetic quality through the gap between the original and the imitation
  • Examine how a First Nations author's use of oral storytelling traditions as intertextual material creates aesthetic qualities not available within Western literary conventions alone

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe connection between intertextual reference and aesthetic quality is abstract — students may understand that a reference exists without being able to articulate what it adds aesthetically. Explicit scaffolding of the 'what does this add that the text alone cannot provide?' question is needed to move from identification to aesthetic explanation.
