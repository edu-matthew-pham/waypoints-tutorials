Language signals roles and relationships

AC9E5LA01 n2 · waypoint 2 of 4 · Year 5 English · Language · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain how particular language choices signal roles (e.g. chairperson, speaker, expert) and the relationships between participants in an interaction.

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Identifies a specific language choice and explains which role it signals and for whom.
  • Explains how the relationship between participants shapes the language choice made (not just identifies the relationship).

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Register and social context.

Width

Minimum
Identify how language choices in a structured context (e.g. a classroom debate or formal discussion) signal who holds which role, and explain what those choices communicate about the relationship between participants.
Typical
Analyse examples from debate, formal discussion, and workplace contexts to explain how language signals authority, deference, peer equality, or expertise. Explain the consequences of role-mismatched language choices.
Wider
  • Compare how language signals roles in formal Australian institutional contexts (parliament, courtroom, formal ceremony) versus informal peer contexts, discussing what makes the role-signalling legible
  • Examine how roles and relationships are signalled in texts written by First Nations Australian authors, identifying language choices that reflect cultural protocols around seniority, kinship or ceremony

Hinge

Node 4's integration task requires students to apply criteria for language selection that explicitly include roles and relationships. Students who cannot explain how language choices signal roles — not just identify that formality shifts — will be unable to account for the roles-and-relationships criterion in Node 4's multi-criteria analysis.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe connection between a specific language choice and the role it signals is not always transparent to students. A reasoning scaffold — 'what role does this language position the speaker in, and how does the listener know?' — helps students develop the analytical move needed to explain rather than just identify.
