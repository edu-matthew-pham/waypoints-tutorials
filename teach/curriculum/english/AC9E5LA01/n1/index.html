Register and social context

AC9E5LA01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 5 English · Language · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify that language varies in formality according to the social context, and name examples of formal and informal language in familiar situations.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two ways language choices (vocabulary, sentence structure, tone) differ between formal and informal contexts.
  • Explains why a specific language choice is appropriate or inappropriate for a given social situation.

Prior knowledge

Students can explore language used to develop relationships in formal and informal situations (AC9E4LA01). They have not yet examined how language choices actively signal social roles and relationships, or considered cultural protocols and how silence/formality norms vary across communities.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least two examples of language choices that shift between a formal and informal version of the same communication (e.g. 'Could you please pass the report?' vs 'Can you grab that?').
Typical
Identify and explain how language formality signals context across a range of situations: classroom, playground, formal presentation, adult conversation. Match language choices to context purpose and relationships involved.
Wider
  • Explore how digital communication contexts (texting, email, social media) generate their own register norms, and how these sit on a formal–informal continuum
  • Examine transcripts or scripts from different social situations and sort language choices onto a formality spectrum, justifying placements

Hinge

Register as a context-sensitive continuum is foundational to Node 2's analysis of role-signalling and Node 4's multi-criteria language selection framework. Students who treat formality as binary (on/off) rather than as a contextually calibrated spectrum will be unable to reason about how fine-grained language choices signal specific roles and relationships in Node 2.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents often treat formality as binary (formal/informal) rather than as a spectrum that shifts continuously with context. Encountering diverse situations — school, sport, family, online — helps students see that register is contextually calibrated, not switched on or off.
