Language and group identity

AC9E8LA01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 8 English · Language · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify language features — such as specialist terminology, acronyms and terms of address — that signal group membership and create solidarity.

Lessons on this site

  • Words that build roles

Success criteria

  • Identifies specific language features (specialist terms, acronyms, terms of address) in a given text and names the group they are associated with.
  • Explains how at least one identified language feature creates a sense of belonging or shared identity among group members.

Prior knowledge

Students can understand how language is used to build relationships and how word choices affect how ideas are perceived (AC9E7LA01). They have not yet examined how language actively constructs group identity, solidarity, and role differentiation through shared terminology and address forms.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least two language features (e.g. specialist terms, forms of address) in one text or social context that signal group membership.
Typical
Identify and explain how language features including specialist terminology, acronyms and terms of address create solidarity and define group identity across several social and cultural contexts, including examples from youth culture, sport or professional communities.
Wider
  • Investigate how First Nations language names (such as those on the AIATSIS Map of Indigenous Australia) reflect relationships to Country/Place and construct group and community identity
  • Compare how two contrasting groups (e.g. a sporting team and a professional workplace) use language differently to signal membership and manage relationships
  • Examine how language associated with a particular online community or subculture both includes insiders and excludes outsiders

Hinge

Students who over-generalise from a single group context arrive at Node 2 without the pluralised understanding that language functions this way across all social groups. Node 2's conceptual shift — from language reflecting roles to language constructing them — cannot be made without encountering how this mechanism operates in varied group contexts.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents may over-generalise from a single example (e.g. only sports contexts). Encountering diverse groups — youth culture, workplace, First Nations communities — builds a more robust understanding that this is a universal language function, not context-specific.
