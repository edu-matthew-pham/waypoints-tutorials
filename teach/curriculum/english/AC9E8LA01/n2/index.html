Language constructing roles and relationships

AC9E8LA01 n2 · waypoint 2 of 2 · Year 8 English · Language

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in English: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can recognise how language choices position people in roles (e.g. leader, peer, outsider) and shape the nature of relationships between individuals and groups.

Lessons on this site

  • Words that build roles

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least one specific language choice in a text and explains how it positions a person in a particular role or relationship.
  • Uses appropriate metalanguage (e.g. 'forms of address', 'inclusive pronoun', 'tone') when discussing how language shapes relationships.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Language and group identity.

Width

Minimum
Identify how one text uses language (e.g. forms of address, inclusive/exclusive pronouns, tone) to define a role or relationship between at least two parties.
Typical
Explain how language choices — including word selection, tone, inclusive/exclusive language and forms of address — construct roles and power relationships across a range of text types including informal conversation, formal speech and social media.
Wider
  • Analyse how language use in a text positions one group as dominant or authoritative relative to another, considering implicit and explicit role markers
  • Investigate how the same individual uses different language choices to construct different roles in different contexts (e.g. a public figure speaking formally vs informally)
  • Explore how language choices in First Nations contexts reflect distinct relational and role structures that differ from mainstream Australian English conventions

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe shift from 'language reflects roles' to 'language constructs roles' is a subtle conceptual move students often miss. Scaffolding the reasoning — showing how the same event can be described in ways that create very different power dynamics — helps students see language as constitutive, not merely reflective.
