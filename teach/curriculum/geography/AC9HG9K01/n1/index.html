Biome concept and classification

AC9HG9K01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 9 Geography · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Geography: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can define biomes as regions with distinctive climates, soils, vegetation and productivity, and distinguish major terrestrial biome types

Success criteria

  • Defines biome as a region characterised by distinctive climate, soil, vegetation and productivity
  • Identifies at least five major terrestrial biome types with distinguishing features
  • Distinguishes biomes from each other using climate and vegetation criteria

Prior knowledge

Students can describe the characteristics of environments and explain how physical and human factors influence the characteristics of places (Y7-8). They have not formally studied biomes as regions defined by distinctive climate-soil-vegetation interconnections, nor analysed patterns of biome productivity across countries.

Width

Minimum
Define biome; identify at least five major terrestrial biome types (e.g. tropical rainforest, grassland, desert, temperate forest, tundra) and their key distinguishing features
Typical
Classify a set of biomes by climate, dominant vegetation and soil type; explain what makes each biome distinctive as a region
Wider
  • Compare aquatic biomes (freshwater, marine, estuarine) alongside terrestrial biomes — identify what makes 'biome' a useful classification for both
  • Investigate how biome classification systems differ (e.g. Whittaker's biome diagram uses temperature-precipitation axes) and why scientists use different classification schemes
  • Examine how soil formation processes link to climate and vegetation type in different biomes

Hinge

Without a working biome concept — that biomes are defined by the interaction of climate, soil and vegetation, not just one factor — students cannot explain why biomes have distinctive productivity in Node 2 or interconnections in Node 3. Conflating biomes with climatic zones or vegetation types alone blocks both downstream nodes.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe biome concept bundles four interdependent factors (climate, soil, vegetation, productivity). Students who list features without grasping the interconnective logic produce fragmented descriptions. Scaffolding the reasoning — why does tropical climate produce this soil type, which supports this vegetation? — builds the relational concept that underpins later nodes.
