Resource classification framework

AC9HG7K01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 5 · Year 7 Geography · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Geography: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can classify environmental resources as renewable, non-renewable or continuous and give examples of each type.

Lessons on this site

  • Renewable, non-renewable or continuous?

Success criteria

  • Classifies water in at least three forms (e.g. surface water, groundwater, water vapour, salt water, ice) using the renewable/non-renewable/continuous framework and justifies each classification.
  • Explains the defining characteristic that distinguishes renewable resources from continuous resources.

Prior knowledge

Students enter Year 7 Geography from the F–6 HASS curriculum. They have a working understanding of basic geographical concepts (place, space, environment, interconnections, scale, sustainability, change), can identify natural and managed environments, and have located Australia within the Asia-Pacific region. They have met renewable and non-renewable resources and their sustainable use (AC9HS4K06) and the types of resources that satisfy needs and wants: natural, human and capital (AC9HS5K08). They have not previously met a formal classification that adds continuous resources and asks whether use can run a resource down, or the concept of the hydrological cycle as an agent of landscape change.

Width

Minimum
Students classify at least two examples of each resource type and explain the defining characteristic that places each in its category.
Typical
Students use the three-category framework to classify a range of environmental resources including water in different forms, fossil fuels, solar energy and timber, explaining the basis for each classification.
Wider
  • Examine contested classifications — e.g. is groundwater renewable or non-renewable given recharge timescales? Where does 'fossil water' sit in the framework?
  • Compare this classification to alternative frameworks (e.g. natural capital, ecosystem services) and evaluate which is more useful for decision-making
  • Investigate how resource classification has changed over time as technology has enabled previously inaccessible resources to be used

Hinge

If students cannot accurately apply the three-category framework, they will misclassify water in its various forms throughout the rest of the unit, undermining analysis of sustainability, distribution and management strategies in K01–K03.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Consolidation: more practise steps, short feedback loopsStudents frequently confuse 'continuous' (always available, e.g. solar, wind) with 'renewable' (regenerates within a human timeframe, e.g. timber, fish). A consolidation pass using targeted sorting exercises with explicit feedback on the continuous/renewable distinction resolves this before it propagates.
Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepWidening to contested and edge cases (fossil water, geothermal, unconventional gas) builds genuine understanding of the classification logic rather than memorised category membership.
