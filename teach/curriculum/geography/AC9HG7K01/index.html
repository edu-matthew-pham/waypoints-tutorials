Water as an environmental resource

AC9HG7K01 · Year 7 Geography · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Geography: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Descriptor

classification of environmental resources and the way that water connects and changes places as it moves through environments

Prior knowledge

Students enter Year 7 Geography from the F–6 HASS curriculum. They have a working understanding of basic geographical concepts (place, space, environment, interconnections, scale, sustainability, change), can identify natural and managed environments, and have located Australia within the Asia-Pacific region. They have met renewable and non-renewable resources and their sustainable use (AC9HS4K06) and the types of resources that satisfy needs and wants: natural, human and capital (AC9HS5K08). They have not previously met a formal classification that adds continuous resources and asks whether use can run a resource down, or the concept of the hydrological cycle as an agent of landscape change.

By the end

Students explain how water is classified as an environmental resource and how its movement through environments connects and changes places.

ACARA elaborations

Examples from ACARA that illustrate this standard. They are examples, not a checklist, and belong to the standard as a whole.

  • classifying resources into renewable, non-renewable and continuous resources, and identifying examples of each type
  • explaining how the movement of water, such as groundwater, soil moisture (green water), surface water in dams, rivers and lakes (blue water), through the environment connects places and how water is a potential resource when it exists as salt water, ice or water vapour
  • explaining the environmental, economic or social effects of water as it connects places; for example, the environmental effects of water diversion in the Snowy Mountains, the economic effects of irrigation in the Ord River or the social effects of the Mutitjulu Waterhole connecting Australian First Nations Peoples in Central Australia
  • explaining how moving water changes places; for example, moving water causes soil and rock erosion or cuts valleys into mountains

Waypoints

  • 1. Resource classification frameworkHingeStudents can classify environmental resources as renewable, non-renewable or continuous and give examples of each type.
  • 2. Water movement and place connectionHingeStudents can explain how water moving through environments connects places, using geographical terminology for different water forms and pathways.
  • 3. Environmental, economic and social effects of water movementStudents can explain environmental, economic or social effects of water as it connects places, using a specific Australian example.
  • 4. Moving water as an agent of landscape changeStudents can explain how moving water changes places through erosion, deposition and related geomorphological processes.
  • 5. Integration: water as classifier, connector and change agentStudents can explain how water is classified as an environmental resource and how its movement through environments connects and changes places — applying the classification framework, connectivity concept and change processes to a specific geographical context.
