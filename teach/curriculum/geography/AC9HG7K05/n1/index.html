Factors influencing residential location decisions

AC9HG7K05 n1 · waypoint 1 of 5 · Year 7 Geography · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Geography: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify and explain multiple types of factors (economic, cultural, spiritual, aesthetic) that influence decisions about where to live.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least three different factor types (economic, cultural, spiritual, aesthetic) influencing residential location decisions, with an example for each.
  • Explains how one non-economic factor can be the primary reason for a residential location decision.

Prior knowledge

Students enter the liveability unit having completed the water unit (K01–K04). They understand that places have environmental, economic, cultural, spiritual and aesthetic dimensions of value, and that water distribution creates differential access for populations in different settlement types. They have not previously encountered the concept of 'liveability' or the objective/subjective measurement distinction.

Width

Minimum
Students identify at least two different types of factor and explain how each influences a residential location decision.
Typical
Students explain economic factors (employment, industries in remote places), cultural factors (connection for cultural groups), spiritual factors (meanings attributed to places) and aesthetic factors (urban amenity, 'tree change') that influence where people choose to live.
Wider
  • Analyse a migration case study — e.g. sea change/tree change movements in Australia — mapping which factor types predominate and which populations make these moves
  • Examine forced vs voluntary movement and how the weight of different factors changes when choice is constrained (e.g. economic refugees, natural disaster displacement)
  • Interview or survey community members about their residential location decisions and categorise the factors they name

Hinge

Students who cannot distinguish multiple factor types will default to economic explanations for all residential decisions. This blocks understanding of cultural and spiritual factors in K07 (First Nations connectedness) and prevents meaningful liveability measurement analysis in node 2.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepUsing diverse case studies — remote Indigenous community, rural tree change, urban professional, remote mining town — makes visible the range of factors that can be primary in different residential decisions, dismantling the economic default.
