← AC9HG7K06 Services, facilities and liveability

Comparing services and facilities across settlement types

AC9HG7K06 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 7 Geography · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Geography: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can compare the distribution and location of services and facilities between different settlement types — urban, rural, remote and very remote.

Success criteria

  • Describes the distributional pattern of at least two services or facilities across different settlement types, identifying where provision is greatest and where it is absent.
  • Uses appropriate geographical language (distribution, concentration, access, provision) to describe service patterns.

Prior knowledge

Students have completed K05 — they understand liveability as a concept, can distinguish objective and subjective measures, and know that factors including environmental quality influence residential decisions. They can represent distribution of resources from K02 work. This standard applies distribution analysis specifically to services and facilities and connects it to liveability outcomes.

Width

Minimum
Students compare access to at least two types of service or facility between two different settlement types.
Typical
Students compare the distribution and location of a range of services and facilities — including clean water, sanitation, education and health services — between urban, rural, remote and very remote settlements, identifying patterns of provision and under-provision.
Wider
  • Map the distribution of a specific service (e.g. hospitals, schools, internet access) across Australia at a national scale and identify patterns of concentration and absence
  • Compare service distribution in an Australian remote context with a rural context in a country in Asia — identify common patterns and differences in what drives under-provision
  • Examine the concept of a 'service desert' (an area with very limited access to multiple services simultaneously) and identify examples in Australia

Hinge

Students who cannot read or interpret a distribution pattern cannot engage meaningfully with the implication analysis in node 2. The distributional analysis is the spatial foundation on which liveability implications are built.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Consolidation: more practise steps, short feedback loopsAccurately describing distributional patterns from maps and data requires practice. Students need repeated exposure to service distribution maps with structured observation tasks before they can generate independent pattern descriptions.
Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepComparing multiple settlement types (not just urban vs rural) and multiple services reveals the cumulative disadvantage of compound under-provision — a place can lack one service or many simultaneously.
