Concepts of human wellbeing and development

AC9HG10K05 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 10 Geography · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Geography: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain what human wellbeing and development mean as geographical concepts, identifying the UN Sustainable Development Goals relevant to human wellbeing.

Success criteria

  • Students define human wellbeing as encompassing more than economic conditions, identifying at least two non-economic dimensions
  • Students identify at least three SDGs relevant to human wellbeing and explain what each targets

Prior knowledge

Students have examined causes of urbanisation and its impacts on places (AC9HG8K06), reasons for and effects of migration (AC9HG8K08), and interconnections between places through trade and consumption (AC9HG9K07-K08). They understand that places differ in economic and social conditions but have not yet used formal measurement indices.

Width

Minimum
Define human wellbeing and development as distinct concepts and identify at least three SDGs relevant to wellbeing.
Typical
Explain human wellbeing as a multi-dimensional concept encompassing economic, social, environmental and political dimensions; identify the SDGs most relevant to wellbeing (health, education, poverty, inequality, clean water) and explain what each targets.
Wider
  • Investigate the Capability Approach (Amartya Sen, Martha Nussbaum) as an alternative conceptual framework for human wellbeing and evaluate how it differs from GDP-based development thinking
  • Examine critiques of 'development' as a concept — including post-development and degrowth perspectives — and assess their implications for how wellbeing is measured
  • Explore how the SDG framework was developed and which countries or stakeholders had the greatest influence on the goals selected, examining whose definition of wellbeing it reflects

Hinge

If students understand human wellbeing only as economic prosperity, they will misinterpret multi-dimensional indices in Node 2 and cannot explain why different measures produce different country rankings.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents often reduce wellbeing to wealth. Explicit conceptual work establishing wellbeing as multi-dimensional (economic, social, environmental, political) before introducing measurement indices prevents persistent conflation of wealth with wellbeing.
