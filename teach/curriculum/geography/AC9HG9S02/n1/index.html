Primary research methods including fieldwork

AC9HG9S02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 9 Geography · Skills

Checkpoint

Students can identify appropriate primary research methods including fieldwork to collect original geographical materials

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two primary research methods and the data each produces
  • Explains when each method is appropriate for a geographical inquiry
  • Justifies method selection for a specific inquiry topic

Prior knowledge

Students can collect, represent and compare geographical data using a range of methods and tools (Y7-8). At Y9, the moves are: evaluating sources for relevance and reliability, creating multi-variable visual representations, and constructing cartographically-compliant special purpose maps using geospatial technologies.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least two primary research methods (e.g. fieldwork observation, photography, field sketches, surveys, interviews); explain what geographical data each produces and when each is appropriate
Typical
Identify and use primary research methods including fieldwork to collect original materials relevant to a geographical inquiry (e.g. aerial photograph comparison, field sketches documenting biome alteration, surveys on technology use); justify method selection for the inquiry
Wider
  • Compare what different primary methods reveal about the same phenomenon — explain what fieldwork observation captures that a survey cannot, and vice versa
  • Evaluate the feasibility of different primary methods for Y9 inquiry topics — which are accessible for students and which require modification?
  • Design a fieldwork protocol for documenting biome alteration over time using repeated photography and field sketches — specify what would be compared and how

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Repertoire building: more cases to sort or classifyPrimary method selection requires students to build a working repertoire of method types and their appropriate uses. Students who only know one method cannot make justified choices. The elaboration's range of examples (aerial photographs, field sketches, surveys) signals the need for repertoire across method types.
