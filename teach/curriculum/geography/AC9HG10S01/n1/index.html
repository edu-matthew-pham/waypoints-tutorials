Generating relevant questions for a geographical phenomenon

AC9HG10S01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 10 Geography · Skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Geography: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can develop a range of relevant questions to investigate why a geographical phenomenon has changed or a challenge may arise.

Success criteria

  • Students generate at least three questions about a phenomenon that are geographically framed, not just generally curious
  • Students' questions together address multiple aspects of the phenomenon (nature, distribution, causes, consequences, responses)

Prior knowledge

At Y9, students developed a range of questions for geographical inquiry (AC9HG9S01). At Y10 the added complexity is working with multi-scale phenomena and linking questions across scales (local, national, global) using geographical concepts (place, space, environment, interconnection, sustainability, scale, change) to sharpen focus.

Width

Minimum
Generate at least three questions about one geographical phenomenon or challenge, ensuring the questions are geographically relevant (not generic).
Typical
Develop a range of relevant questions that together address multiple dimensions of a geographical phenomenon or challenge — covering what, why, where, how and with what consequences; ensuring questions are grounded in geographical concepts.
Wider
  • Develop question sets for two contrasting phenomena (e.g. environmental sustainability and human wellbeing) and compare the inquiry structures, evaluating which questions are most productive for geographical investigation
  • Investigate how professional geographers frame inquiry questions and compare professional research questions with student-generated questions for the same topic
  • Generate questions that deliberately cross scales (local, national, global) for the same phenomenon and evaluate how scale change reshapes the inquiry

Hinge

If students cannot generate questions that are geographically framed rather than generically curious, the entire inquiry they conduct will lack geographical direction — data collection (S02), analysis (S03) and conclusion (S04) all flow from the question quality.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents often generate questions that could apply to any discipline ('What is the problem? How bad is it? What should we do?'). Scaffold geographical question framing explicitly — what makes a question geographical is that it involves place, space, scale, environment or interconnection.
