Evaluating sources for relevance, reliability and bias

AC9HG10S02 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Year 10 Geography · Skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Geography: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can evaluate primary and secondary research materials for relevance, reliability and bias, including identifying different types of bias.

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Students evaluate a source for relevance, reliability and bias using evidence from the source
  • Students explain how a specific type of bias (information or selection) affects what a source tells them and what it omits

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Primary research methods including fieldwork.

Width

Minimum
Evaluate one source using at least two criteria (relevance, reliability or bias) and explain the evaluation with evidence from the source.
Typical
Evaluate primary and secondary sources for relevance (currency, fit to inquiry), reliability (authorship, referencing, expertise) and bias (information bias, selection bias); compare findings from primary research with secondary research for relevance and reliability.
Wider
  • Analyse how bias operates differently in quantitative data (selection bias in sampling) vs qualitative sources (framing and perspective bias) — developing a multi-type bias evaluation framework
  • Evaluate sources specifically related to First Nations communities or aid programs where power dynamics shape what is published and from whose perspective
  • Investigate how media sources on environmental or wellbeing issues reflect particular world views, applying the bias evaluation framework to identify not just error but perspective

Hinge

If students cannot evaluate sources, they will use unreliable or biased data in their analysis (S03) and draw conclusions (S04) built on a weak evidentiary foundation — the entire subsequent inquiry chain is compromised by weak source evaluation.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents often treat relevance and reliability as the same thing, or conflate 'I agree with this' with 'this is reliable'. An explicit framework distinguishing the three criteria — with a worked example applying all three to the same source — prevents undifferentiated source assessment.
Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepPractising source evaluation across different source types (news, academic, NGO reports, government data, community-produced) builds a more flexible and accurate evaluation toolkit.
