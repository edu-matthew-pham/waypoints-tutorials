Waste management strategies and sustainability

AC9HG9K08 n4 · waypoint 4 of 4 · Year 9 Geography · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Geography: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can examine waste management strategies used by governments and identify implications for sustainability

Success criteria

  • Identifies a specific waste management strategy used by an Australian government
  • Explains what sustainability dimension the strategy addresses
  • Identifies at least one sustainability implication (environmental, economic or social) of the strategy

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 3: Global oil supply chain: extraction to waste.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least one waste management strategy used by a local, state or national government in Australia; explain what sustainability dimension (environmental, economic, social) it addresses
Typical
Examine a waste management strategy used in an Australian city or regional centre (e.g. waste reduction, recycling programs, circular economy approaches); identify the environmental, economic and social sustainability implications; connect to supply chain logic from Node 2
Wider
  • Evaluate a specific waste management strategy against all three sustainability criteria — environmental, economic and social — and identify trade-offs
  • Compare waste management approaches in different Australian cities or between Australia and an Asian country — explain why approaches differ
  • Investigate extended producer responsibility policies — explain how shifting waste management costs back to producers changes the supply chain logic

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Integration: a combining task at the wider tierThis node integrates production effects (Node 1), supply chain logic (Node 2) and oil supply chain evaluation (Node 3) into a management analysis. Waste management strategies only make sense as responses to specific impacts at specific supply chain stages. Teaching investment should connect the strategy directly back to the impact it addresses in the supply chain.
