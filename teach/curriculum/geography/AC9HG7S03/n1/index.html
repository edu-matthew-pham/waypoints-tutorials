Identifying similarities and differences in geographical data

AC9HG7S03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 7 Geography · Skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Geography: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify similarities and differences in geographical data and information, using specific evidence from maps, images or data.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two similarities and two differences between geographical datasets or places, citing specific evidence for each.
  • Avoids vague generalisations — each comparison is grounded in a specific data point, map feature or image detail.

Prior knowledge

Students have prior skills identifying patterns and trends from F–6 HASS (AC9HS6S03: evaluate information and data in a range of formats to identify and describe patterns and trends, or to infer relationships). This standard extends that to geographical-specific analytical moves: reading spatial data, explaining geographical patterns and trends with reference to geographical processes, and inferring place-based relationships from data.

Width

Minimum
Students identify at least two similarities and two differences in a geographical dataset or between two places, with specific evidence.
Typical
Students identify similarities and differences in geographical data — e.g. comparing housing density from aerial images of inner vs outer suburbs, or rural vs remote places — using specific evidence from the data and avoiding vague generalisations.
Wider
  • Compare data at multiple scales for the same phenomenon — identify similarities and differences at local, state and national scales
  • Compare similarities and differences using both quantitative data (statistics) and qualitative data (photographs, field sketches) for the same location pair
  • Examine a case where aggregate data obscures important local similarities and differences — practise identifying what fine-grained analysis reveals that aggregate analysis hides

Hinge

Identifying similarities and differences with specific evidence is the foundation for pattern explanation in node 2. Students who make vague comparisons ('one place has more services') without specific evidence cannot transition to explaining spatial patterns — they have no data points to connect into a pattern.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Consolidation: more practise steps, short feedback loopsStudents frequently describe differences without citing specific evidence. Consolidation requires repeated practice extracting specific data points from maps and images as the basis for comparison — not describing general impressions.
