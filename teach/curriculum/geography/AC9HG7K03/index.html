Values of water

AC9HG7K03 · Year 7 Geography · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Geography: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Descriptor

the economic, cultural, spiritual and aesthetic value of water for people, including First Nations Australians

Prior knowledge

Students have completed K01 and K02 — they can classify water resources, trace water movement connecting places, and explain distribution and sustainability. Those nodes treat water primarily as a physical and economic resource. This standard introduces the cultural, spiritual and aesthetic dimensions of water value, which require a different conceptual lens.

By the end

Students explain the economic, cultural, spiritual and aesthetic value of water for people, including First Nations Australians.

ACARA elaborations

Examples from ACARA that illustrate this standard. They are examples, not a checklist, and belong to the standard as a whole.

  • examining and comparing places in Australia and a country in Asia that have economies and communities based on irrigation; for example, rice production in the Murrumbidgee Irrigation Area in New South Wales and the Mekong Delta in Vietnam
  • discussing the multilayered meanings (material, cultural and spiritual wellbeing) associated with rivers, waterways, waterholes, human-made wells, seas, lakes, soaks and springs for First Nations Australians
  • examining bays, rivers, waterfalls or lakes in Australia and in countries of Asia that have been listed as either World Heritage sites or national parks for their aesthetic and cultural value
  • investigating the spiritual significance of water in an Asian culture

Waypoints

  • 1. Framework of water valuesHingeStudents can distinguish between economic, cultural, spiritual and aesthetic values of water and give an example of each.
  • 2. First Nations Australians and multilayered water valuesHingeStudents can explain the multilayered meanings of water for First Nations Australians — material, cultural and spiritual — using specific examples of rivers, waterways, waterholes or other water features.
  • 3. Economic value of water: irrigation and food productionStudents can explain the economic value of water for irrigation-based communities and industries, using a comparison between an Australian and an Asian example.
  • 4. Aesthetic and cultural value of water: heritage and tourismStudents can explain the aesthetic and cultural value of water features through examples of World Heritage or national park listings in Australia and Asia.
  • 5. Integration: multiple values in tension and contextStudents explain the economic, cultural, spiritual and aesthetic value of water for people — applying the four-value framework to a specific case and recognising where values coexist or conflict.
