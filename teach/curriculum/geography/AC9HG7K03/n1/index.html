Framework of water values

AC9HG7K03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 5 · Year 7 Geography · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Geography: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can distinguish between economic, cultural, spiritual and aesthetic values of water and give an example of each.

Success criteria

  • Names and distinguishes economic, cultural, spiritual and aesthetic values of water, providing a concrete example for each.
  • Identifies a water body or waterway that holds more than one type of value and explains how each applies.

Prior knowledge

Students have completed K01 and K02 — they can classify water resources, trace water movement connecting places, and explain distribution and sustainability. Those nodes treat water primarily as a physical and economic resource. This standard introduces the cultural, spiritual and aesthetic dimensions of water value, which require a different conceptual lens.

Width

Minimum
Students define each value type and provide at least one concrete example.
Typical
Students explain each of the four value types (economic, cultural, spiritual, aesthetic) and provide examples drawn from Australian and Asian contexts, recognising that a single water body may hold multiple value types simultaneously.
Wider
  • Examine how the four value types can conflict — e.g. a river's economic value (irrigation) may conflict with its spiritual value for First Nations communities — and consider how such conflicts are mediated
  • Compare how values attributed to water differ across cultures — e.g. the aesthetic value of a waterfall in Japanese culture vs an Australian National Park listing for the same feature type
  • Explore how a value type can be 'discovered' or recognised — e.g. aesthetic value formalised through World Heritage listing

Hinge

Students who cannot distinguish the four value types — particularly conflating cultural and spiritual, or treating economic value as the only 'real' value — will struggle to explain First Nations relationships to Country/water in node 2, which requires understanding non-economic values as primary rather than supplementary.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe economic/non-economic distinction needs explicit scaffolding. Many students default to economic explanations. A structured reasoning approach that requires students to explain each value type on its own terms — not as a secondary consideration after economics — addresses this.
Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepWidening to cases where non-economic values are primary (spiritual significance, aesthetic listing) gives students examples where the framework applies without defaulting to economic framing.
