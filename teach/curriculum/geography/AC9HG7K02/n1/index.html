Locating and representing Australia's water resources

AC9HG7K02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 5 · Year 7 Geography · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Geography: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can represent the location of Australia's major water resources (surface water and groundwater) and describe the pattern of their distribution.

Success criteria

  • Represents the location of at least two types of Australian water resource on a map, applying appropriate cartographic conventions.
  • Describes the distributional pattern of water resources across Australia, identifying which regions have greater or lesser access.

Prior knowledge

Students have completed AC9HG7K01 — they can classify water as a renewable/continuous/non-renewable resource, trace water movement connecting places, and explain environmental, economic and social effects. They can locate Australia broadly but have not previously examined the spatial distribution of Australia's water resources or the concept of sustainability applied specifically to water.

Width

Minimum
Students identify at least two types of water resource location in Australia (e.g. surface water in a river system, a major groundwater basin) and describe a basic distributional pattern.
Typical
Students represent the location of Australia's surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) and groundwater (aquifers, the Great Artesian Basin) on a map and describe the uneven distribution of these resources across the continent.
Wider
  • Analyse temporal variation — represent not just where water is located but how availability changes seasonally and across drought/flood cycles using historical data
  • Compare Australia's water resource distribution with a country in Asia with contrasting distribution (e.g. Japan, India) to identify what makes Australia's distribution distinctive
  • Examine the difference between 'water exists here' and 'water is accessible here' — mapping accessible versus total water resources

Hinge

Students who cannot accurately read or produce a distribution map will be unable to engage with the implications analysis in nodes 2–3. Spatial literacy with distribution maps is the foundation for the rest of this standard.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Consolidation: more practise steps, short feedback loopsConstructing and reading distribution maps requires repeated practice with cartographic conventions. Students who have only seen maps need guided practice constructing one before interpreting distribution patterns.
