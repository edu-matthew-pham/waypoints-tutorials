Making generalisations from data

AC9HG9S03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 9 Geography · Skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Geography: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can make generalisations about trends from geographical data

Success criteria

  • Makes at least one generalisation that goes beyond restating individual data points
  • Identifies specific evidence from the data that supports the generalisation
  • Qualifies the generalisation appropriately (e.g. 'in most cases', 'with some exceptions')

Prior knowledge

Students can identify patterns and trends in geographical data (Y7-8). At Y9, the moves are: making and justifying generalisations from data, making predictions from trends, and inferring causal relationships between variables.

Width

Minimum
Make at least one generalisation from a dataset or survey; explain what evidence from the data supports the generalisation
Typical
Make and justify generalisations about geographical trends from data sources such as questionnaires, interviews or quantitative datasets; identify evidence that supports and evidence that qualifies the generalisation
Wider
  • Compare generalisations made from a qualitative source (e.g. interviews about food access) with generalisations from a quantitative source (e.g. food production statistics) on the same topic — explain what each type of data reveals
  • Identify when a generalisation is over-broad — find a data point that doesn't fit and explain what it reveals about the limitations of the generalisation
  • Evaluate two generalisations about the same trend (e.g. from different data sources) — explain which is better supported and why

Hinge

Generalisation is the bridge between raw data and pattern explanation. Students who cannot make a justified generalisation produce data summaries rather than analysis, and cannot make predictions or infer relationships in Nodes 2-3. If students cannot move from 'the data shows X' to 'we can therefore say Y generally', the subsequent analytical moves in this standard are inaccessible.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkGeneralisation requires students to synthesise across data points rather than read off individual values. The reasoning scaffold — 'what pattern appears across multiple data points?' then 'what does that allow us to say generally?' — needs explicit modelling. Students who describe individual data points rather than synthesising them have not crossed this node's threshold.
