Transport and information networks: supply chains

AC9HG9K05 n2 · waypoint 2 of 4 · Year 9 Geography · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Geography: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify and describe how transport and information networks function to connect people to goods and services, including how supply-chain logistics influence these connections

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two types of transport and information networks
  • Traces at least three steps in a product supply chain from production to consumer
  • Explains how logistics decisions at one step affect connectivity at another

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Transport access and perceptions of place.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least two types of transport or information networks; trace how a product moves through a supply chain from production to consumer
Typical
Explain how transport networks (road, rail, air, sea) and information networks connect people to goods and services; trace a specific product supply chain (e.g. cotton crop to t-shirt, farm to table) and identify where logistics decisions affect connectivity
Wider
  • Map the supply chain of a specific food product from production to consumption — identify every transport and information network involved
  • Examine how supply chain disruptions (e.g. COVID-19, natural disasters) affect people's connections to goods and services
  • Investigate how e-commerce platforms change the logistics of connecting consumers to goods — compare the supply chain of a locally purchased item versus an internationally purchased one

Hinge

The supply chain concept bridges transport/information networks as abstract systems and their real effect on where and how people access goods and services. Students who cannot trace how logistics decisions shape connectivity cannot explain the digital divide or global business services in Nodes 3-4, which both depend on understanding that network access is not uniform.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkTracing a supply chain requires students to sequence network-dependent steps and identify where breakdowns would affect connectivity. Students who skip the logistics step treat supply chains as frictionless. Scaffolding the step-by-step trace — from source to consumer, identifying transport and information network at each stage — builds the systemic understanding needed for Nodes 3-4.
