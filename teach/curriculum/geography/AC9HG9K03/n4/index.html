Interconnections between factors and crop yield

AC9HG9K03 n4 · waypoint 4 of 4 · Year 9 Geography · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Geography: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain the impact of interconnections between environmental, economic and technological factors on the yield of a particular crop in Australia

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two interconnections between different factor types
  • Explains the combined impact of interconnected factors on yield of one named crop
  • Uses geographical terminology (interconnection, yield, productivity) correctly

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 3: Agricultural innovations reducing environmental limitations.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least two interconnections between factors (e.g. water availability affects which technology is viable; technology adoption requires finance); explain their combined impact on yield of one crop
Typical
Explain the interconnected impacts of environmental, economic and technological factors on the yield of a specific crop (e.g. wheat or rice) in Australia; show how a change in one factor cascades through others
Wider
  • Construct a systems diagram showing the interconnections between at least three factors affecting wheat or rice yield in Australia — explain at least two feedback loops
  • Analyse a case where changing one factor (e.g. drought reducing water availability) cascades through economic and technological factors to affect yield
  • Compare the factor interconnections for the same crop in Australia and an Asian country — explain why the same crop faces different interconnection patterns

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Integration: a combining task at the wider tierThis node integrates all three factor types into an interconnected explanation for a specific crop. The teaching investment is on coordination — students need to show how factors interact, not treat them as independent variables. Using a systems diagram or cascading-change scenario forces this coordination.
