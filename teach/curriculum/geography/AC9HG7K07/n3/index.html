First Nations Australians: connectedness and belonging to Country/Place

AC9HG7K07 n3 · waypoint 3 of 4 · Year 7 Geography · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Geography: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can discuss the cultural connectedness and belonging that First Nations Australians have to a range of places through family, Country/Place, dispossession, relocation and employment.

Success criteria

  • Explains the cultural connectedness and belonging of First Nations Australians to Country/Place, identifying at least two dimensions of that connection.
  • Explains how dispossession or relocation has affected First Nations relationships to Country, using specific reference to historical or contemporary experience.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Social connection and liveability.

Width

Minimum
Students explain the cultural connectedness and belonging that First Nations Australians have to Country/Place, using at least one dimension (family, Country, dispossession or relocation).
Typical
Students discuss the cultural connectedness and belonging that First Nations Australians have to Country/Place through family ties, Country/Place identity, and the ongoing effects of dispossession, relocation and employment on relationships to place — recognising that connections persist across multiple places and are affected but not erased by displacement.
Wider
  • Examine the historical geography of dispossession and relocation for a specific community — trace how policy (missions, reserves, forced removal) altered relationships to Country and the ongoing consequences for place connectedness today
  • Research how Native Title and land rights frameworks recognise and partly restore First Nations connections to Country — what does legal recognition mean for cultural connectedness?
  • Examine contemporary First Nations urban identity — how do Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people maintain connection to Country while living far from ancestral lands?

Hinge

Students who do not grasp the depth and specificity of First Nations connectedness to Country — particularly the way dispossession constitutes a liveability harm beyond physical displacement — will be unable to properly evaluate liveability strategies for First Nations communities in K08.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents may initially understand First Nations connection to Country as analogous to any person's attachment to a home place. The reasoning scaffold needed is to explain how Country relationship is ontological (defines identity and belonging in a fundamental way) rather than merely sentimental — and how dispossession therefore causes a distinctive form of harm.
Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepExamining multiple dimensions of connectedness (family, Country, spiritual, employment) and how dispossession has affected each dimension prevents a one-dimensional understanding of a complex, layered relationship.
