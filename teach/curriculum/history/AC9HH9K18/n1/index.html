Historical agency — how individuals and groups promote ideas

AC9HH9K18 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 9 History · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain what it means for an individual or group to promote an idea — the distinction between holding an idea and actively working to advance it — and describe the range of strategies available (writing, organising, campaigning, legislating).

Success criteria

  • Explains the distinction between holding an idea and actively promoting it
  • Identifies at least two strategies for promoting ideas and describes how each works

Prior knowledge

Students can describe the role of significant individuals and groups in historical change (AC9HH8K05, AC9HH8K10, AC9HH8K15) and understand the ideas that emerged during the Industrial Revolution (AC9HH9K17). They have not yet applied the concept of historical agency — the role of individuals in promoting and enacting ideas — to the Industrial Revolution context, or examined how responses to ideas shaped the trajectory of industrial society.

Width

Minimum
Students explain that promoting an idea involves active effort to persuade others and enact change, identify at least two strategies for promoting ideas (e.g. writing, public speaking, organising, lobbying), and apply this to one example.
Typical
Students describe the range of strategies available — publishing (pamphlets, books, newspapers), public speaking and oration, political organisation and campaigning, religious networks, direct action and industrial organisation; explain that the same idea can be promoted by different actors using different strategies; explain that responses to ideas — opposition, revision, partial adoption — are also historically significant forms of agency; connect to the ideas framework from K17.
Wider
  • Compare the effectiveness of different strategies for promoting ideas — when was publishing more effective than organising? When did religious networks reach audiences that secular advocates could not?
  • Examine how ideas that were initially suppressed or marginalised eventually gained acceptance — what changed to make them viable?

Hinge

The agency framework is the prerequisite for the individual/group case studies. Students who cannot distinguish promoting an idea from simply holding it cannot evaluate why specific individuals were significant — what specifically they did that made a difference.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly describe historical figures as important without articulating what specifically they did to promote an idea. Making the action-mechanism explicit — what did they do, how did they do it, what changed as a result — is the scaffold for meaningful individual investigations.
