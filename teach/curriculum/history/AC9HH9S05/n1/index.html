Analysing cause-and-effect relationships

AC9HH9S05 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 9 History · Historical skills

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can analyse cause-and-effect relationships in historical events, distinguishing between long-term, medium-term and immediate causes, and explaining how causes are interconnected.

Success criteria

  • Identifies and analyses at least three causes of one historical event, distinguishing between long-term and immediate causes
  • Explains how at least two causes are connected — demonstrating causal complexity rather than parallel listing

Prior knowledge

In Year 7 students described causes and effects and explained continuities and changes (AC9HH7S05). In Year 8 they continued with description and explanation (AC9HH8S05). Y9 lifts the demand — from describe/explain to analyse cause-and-effect and evaluate continuity/change patterns. The addition of 'evaluate' signals that students must now make judgements about significance, not only describe relationships.

Width

Minimum
Identify and describe at least two causes of one historical event, explaining the relationship between them.
Typical
Create a timeline identifying significant events or individuals across a period; analyse cause-and-effect patterns including distinguishing between long-term structural causes and immediate triggers; explain how multiple causes interact (e.g. MAIN causes of WWI plus the assassination as trigger); organise primary sources and perspectives chronologically to support historical argument.
Wider
  • Construct a multi-causal analysis of a complex historical event (e.g. the outbreak of WWI or the Industrial Revolution) using a structured framework distinguishing long-term, medium-term and immediate causes — applying the skill at its most demanding level
  • Apply cause-and-effect analysis to two contrasting historical events in the same unit to demonstrate the skill's transferability
  • Use quantitative data sources (e.g. population records, casualty figures) alongside qualitative sources to build a cause-and-effect argument — practising the integration of different evidence types

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe distinction between long-term structural causes and immediate triggers is the key analytical move at Year 9. Students who only identify immediate causes cannot analyse historical causation at the expected depth — explicit scaffolding of the long/short-term distinction is necessary.
