Australia's changing relationship with Britain

AC9HH9K11 n4 · waypoint 4 of 5 · Year 9 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can examine the continuities and changes in Australia's relationship with the British Empire as a result of the war, including how the war both affirmed and strained the imperial relationship.

Success criteria

  • Describes at least one continuity and one change in Australia's relationship with Britain as a result of the war
  • Explains how the shared sacrifice at Gallipoli contributed to both a sense of imperial belonging and a distinct Australian national identity

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 3: The conscription debates — political crisis and social division.

Width

Minimum
Students describe at least one way the war reinforced Australia's relationship with Britain (fighting together, shared sacrifice) and at least one way it created new tensions or a new sense of distinct Australian identity (Gallipoli as an Australian story, resentment of British command decisions).
Typical
Students examine the affirmation of Empire loyalty through shared sacrifice; the simultaneous development of a distinctly Australian national identity through the Gallipoli experience (ANZAC as a specifically Australian story); the resentment of British command decisions (Fromelles, Gallipoli) and what this meant for how Australians saw British leadership; the new sense of Australia as a distinct nation that had 'earned its place' on the world stage through sacrifice; the seeds of eventual greater independence in foreign policy.
Wider
  • Examine how Australian and British official histories of the war — Bean's Official History vs British accounts — differ in emphasis and what this reveals about the distinct national narratives being constructed
  • Trace the trajectory from 1914 Empire loyalty to the growing independence visible in the 1930s and 1940s — connecting this descriptor to longer-term changes in Australian sovereignty

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly frame the relationship with Britain as either straightforwardly loyal or straightforwardly resentful. The content requires holding both — the war both affirmed and complicated the relationship — which requires explicit modelling of how continuity and change can coexist in the same relationship.
