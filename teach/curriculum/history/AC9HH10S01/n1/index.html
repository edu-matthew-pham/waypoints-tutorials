Developing questions that require historical argument

AC9HH10S01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 10 History · Historical skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can develop a range of historical questions — a key question and related sub-questions — about an aspect of the past that require historical argument rather than factual retrieval.

Success criteria

  • Develop a key question and at least two sub-questions for an inquiry topic
  • Explain why each question requires historical argument rather than factual recall
  • Identify the type of historical thinking each question demands (causation, significance, continuity/change, perspective, interpretation)

Prior knowledge

Students can develop and modify historical questions to inform inquiry at Year 9 level — scoping questions for a specific investigation and adjusting them based on source availability.

Width

Minimum
Develop a key question and at least two related sub-questions for an inquiry topic; explain why each requires historical argument rather than simple factual recall.
Typical
Develop a structured range of questions including a key question and related sub-questions for a chosen inquiry topic; identify, plan and begin investigating a specific historical question or issue; distinguish questions that require historical argument (cause, significance, change, perspective, interpretation) from those that require only factual recall.
Wider
  • Develop inquiry questions for a topic with contested or incomplete sources — practising how to formulate researchable questions when the evidentiary record is uneven or politically sensitive
  • Compare the question structures used by professional historians for different purposes (academic monograph, public history exhibition, documentary), examining how purpose and audience shape what questions historians ask

Hinge

Students who cannot distinguish argument-requiring questions from factual questions will develop research plans that cannot produce historical argument — the work of the entire inquiry collapses if the question doesn't require interpretation. This is the entry condition for meaningful historical inquiry.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkExplicitly teach the distinction between questions that require argument ('Why did...', 'To what extent...', 'How significant was...') and those that require only retrieval ('When did...', 'Who was...'). Have students categorise example questions before attempting to write their own.
