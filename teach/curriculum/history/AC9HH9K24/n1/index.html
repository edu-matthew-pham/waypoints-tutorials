Nationalism and self-determination as ideas in the Asian context

AC9HH9K24 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 9 History · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain what nationalism and self-determination meant in the context of the chosen Asian society, and describe how these ideas were taking hold by the early 20th century.

Success criteria

  • Explains nationalism and self-determination as ideas and describes how they applied in the chosen Asian context
  • Identifies at least one movement or event expressing nationalist aspiration in the chosen society

Prior knowledge

Students can describe the development of the chosen Asian society, the effects of European contact, and the role of significant individuals and movements (AC9HH9K19–K23). They have not yet synthesised this knowledge into an account of where the chosen society stood in the world by 1914, or examined how nationalism and self-determination as ideas were reshaping Asian societies' relationships with European powers.

Width

Minimum
Students explain nationalism as the idea that a people sharing culture, language or history should govern themselves, describe how this idea was being applied in the chosen Asian society, and identify at least one movement or event that expressed it.
Typical
Students describe how Western ideas of nationalism and self-determination — spread partly through Western education systems established under colonialism — were adopted and adapted by Asian intellectuals and movements; examine specific nationalist movements (Indian National Congress, Chinese nationalism, Indonesian nationalist organisations); explain how nationalism provided a framework for challenging colonial rule on the colonisers' own terms — using their proclaimed values against them; examine tensions within nationalist movements between moderate and radical approaches, and between different social groups' visions of the nation.
Wider
  • Examine the paradox that Western colonialism both suppressed and enabled nationalism — by spreading ideas of rights and self-governance while denying them — and what this reveals about the internal contradictions of colonial ideology
  • Investigate how women participated in nationalist movements — examining the intersection of nationalism and gender politics in the chosen society

Hinge

Understanding nationalism as an idea with a specific trajectory in the Asian context is prerequisite for understanding the confrontations and resistance movements in N2. Students who see nationalism as simply 'wanting independence' cannot explain why it emerged when it did, why it took different forms in different societies, or why it was so powerful.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly treat nationalism as self-evident — of course people want to govern themselves — without understanding it as a historically specific idea that emerged in a particular context and that required active intellectual development and political organisation to become a force. Making the idea's historical emergence explicit is the scaffold for understanding the movements of the period.
