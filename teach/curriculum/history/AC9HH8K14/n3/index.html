Interactions and responses to authority

AC9HH8K14 n3 · waypoint 3 of 3 · Year 8 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can describe how subject peoples responded to authority in the chosen Asia-Pacific society — including accommodation, resistance or cultural negotiation — and explain how these interactions shaped the society.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least one example of how subject peoples responded to authority in the chosen society
  • Explains how that response shaped the society — whether maintaining, modifying or challenging existing power structures

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Experiences of subject peoples.

Width

Minimum
At least one example of how subject peoples responded to authority, with an explanation of its effect on the society.
Typical
Responses across accommodation, resistance and exchange. Examples: how the daimyo contributed to the end of the Ashikaga Shogunate as subject-rulers challenging authority from within; the role of merchants and artisans in feudal Japan navigating formal status constraints; Polynesian peoples' practical sovereignty over resources through Rahui as a community-led response to environmental pressure; the role of councils and collective decision-making in chiefly societies.
Wider
  • For feudal Japan: examine the role of the daimyo in contributing to the end of the Ashikaga Shogunate as a case study in how powerful subject-rulers could challenge and ultimately transform the authority structure from within
  • For the Khmer: examine how the god-king's authority was both sustained and undermined by his obligation to deliver prosperity — when environmental failure (drought, flooding) disrupted the water management system, the basis of royal authority was challenged

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Integration: a combining task at the wider tierThis node integrates understanding of how authority was exercised (Node 1) and experienced (Node 2) into an analysis of how the relationship between rulers and subjects was dynamic rather than fixed. Invest in helping students see authority as a relationship that required ongoing maintenance and negotiation.
