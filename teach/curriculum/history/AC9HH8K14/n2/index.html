Experiences of subject peoples

AC9HH8K14 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Year 8 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can describe the experiences and perspectives of subject peoples in the chosen Asia-Pacific society — including how social position, class and cultural context shaped their daily life under authority.

Success criteria

  • Describes how authority shaped the daily life of at least one subject group in the chosen Asia-Pacific society
  • Identifies at least one way in which different groups within the same society experienced authority differently

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: How rulers exercised authority.

Width

Minimum
A description of how authority affected the daily life of at least one subject group.
Typical
The varied experiences of subject peoples. Examples: the impact of the Tokugawa Shogunate reimposing a feudal system on different classes (daimyo, samurai, farmers, merchants); the use of artefacts such as Lapita pottery from Vanuatu, tapa cloth and ship-building techniques to provide insight into the lives of ordinary Polynesian peoples; how daily life in the Khmer Empire was shaped by the obligations of subject peoples to the god-king.
Wider
  • For feudal Japan: examine the impact of the Tokugawa Shogunate's reimposition of the feudal system on different classes — particularly the impact on merchants and artisans whose wealth grew but whose status remained formally low
  • For Polynesia: examine artefacts such as Lapita pottery, tapa cloth and ship-building techniques as evidence of the daily life and capabilities of ordinary Polynesian peoples, not just their rulers

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepThe experience of subject peoples under authority varies dramatically across Asia-Pacific societies. Using multiple contexts (Khmer peasants vs Polynesian commoners vs Japanese samurai) prevents students from generalising from a single subordinate experience.
