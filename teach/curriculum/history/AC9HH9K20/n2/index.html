Effects of European contact — change and continuity in the chosen society

AC9HH9K20 n2 · waypoint 2 of 2 · Year 9 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify aspects of the chosen Asian society that changed and those that remained the same as a result of European contact.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two changes resulting from European contact with the chosen society
  • Identifies at least one significant continuity and explains why it persisted

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Nature and causes of European contact — trade, competition and control.

Width

Minimum
Students identify at least two changes and at least one continuity in the chosen society resulting from European contact.
Typical
Students describe changes — political (loss of sovereignty, new legal systems), economic (integration into European trade systems, deindustrialisation, cash crop monocultures), social (new class structures, missionary influence), cultural (spread of European languages, education systems, religion); and continuities — traditional practices, family structures, religious beliefs, cultural arts that persisted under or alongside colonial rule; apply the continuity-change framework from K05; examine how different social groups experienced contact differently — elites who collaborated, peasants who resisted, artisans displaced by imports.
Wider
  • Examine the British Raj's infrastructure legacy (railways, telegraph, schools, law) and evaluate whether its long-term effects were primarily beneficial or harmful — examining the debate from Indian and British perspectives
  • Investigate how colonial contact changed gender relations in the chosen society — examining what changed for women specifically and what forces drove those changes

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents commonly focus on dramatic political changes (loss of sovereignty) without examining the economic, social and cultural changes that often had greater effects on everyday life. Multiple domains of change — and attention to continuities — build the fuller picture.
