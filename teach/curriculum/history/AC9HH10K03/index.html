Turning points of the Second World War

AC9HH10K03 · Year 10 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Descriptor

the significant events and turning points of the Second World War, including the Holocaust and use of the atomic bomb

Prior knowledge

Students have examined turning points of WW1 (AC9HH9K10) and can apply the concept of historical significance. From AC9HH10K01 they understand the course of the war at a structural level. From AC9HH10K02 they have examined specific Australian campaigns. They have studied the rise of fascism and Nazi ideology (AC9HH10K01, N2), giving them the ideological context for the Holocaust. They have not yet examined the Holocaust as a conceptually distinct historical phenomenon requiring its own analytical framework, nor analysed the atomic bomb decision as an ethical and strategic problem.

By the end

Students explain why particular events were significant turning points in both the European and Asia-Pacific theatres, examine the historical context and scale of the Holocaust, and analyse the decision to use atomic weapons including their short- and long-term effects.

ACARA elaborations

Examples from ACARA that illustrate this standard. They are examples, not a checklist, and belong to the standard as a whole.

  • explaining why particular events were significant turning points in the European theatre of war, such as Germany’s invasion of Poland in 1939, the Blitzkrieg, the Battle of Britain, Operation Overlord (the invasion of Normandy), the Battle of the Bulge, and the Soviets’ capture of Berlin
  • explaining why particular events were significant turning points in the Asia-Pacific theatre of war, such as the Japanese occupation of China, the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor in 1941, the fall of Singapore in 1942, the bombings of northern Australia, the American victory at the Battle of Midway in 1942, the Battle for Kokoda and the Battle of Iwo Jima
  • examining the historical context in which the Holocaust occurred, including anti-Semitism and Nazi race theory
  • investigating the scale and significance of the Holocaust using primary and secondary sources, such as survivor testimonies
  • discussing the short- and long-term impacts of the Holocaust on the Jewish community post–Second World War, such as coming to terms with the scale of loss, migration to Israel, Australia and the United States, and the creation of Yad Vashem and other Holocaust centres, museums and memorials
  • identifying the race to build the atomic bomb by Germany, Japan and the USA, and analysing why the atomic bombs were dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki
  • investigating the effects of the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, including short- and long-term effects on human health, and short- and long-term environmental effects on the cities and surrounding areas

Waypoints

  • 1. Turning point as a historical conceptHingeStudents can explain what distinguishes a turning point from a significant event and apply explicit criteria to candidate events.
  • 2. Turning points — European theatreStudents can explain why particular events were significant turning points in the European theatre, applying turning-point criteria to a range of named events.
  • 3. Turning points — Asia-Pacific theatreStudents can explain why particular events were significant turning points in the Asia-Pacific theatre and explain how the two theatres relate to each other.
  • 4. Holocaust — ideology, context, and escalationHingeStudents can explain the historical context in which the Holocaust occurred — including anti-Semitism as a longstanding European phenomenon, Nazi race theory as its political weaponisation, and the escalation from persecution to genocide.
  • 5. Holocaust — scale, significance, and long-term impactsStudents can describe the scale of the Holocaust using evidence, evaluate its historical significance, and explain its long-term impacts on the Jewish community and on international human rights frameworks.
  • 6. The atomic bomb — decision, effects, and significanceStudents can analyse why atomic bombs were dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, describe their short- and long-term effects, and evaluate the significance of this decision.
