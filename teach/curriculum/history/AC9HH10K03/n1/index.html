Turning point as a historical concept

AC9HH10K03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 6 · Year 10 History · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain what distinguishes a turning point from a significant event and apply explicit criteria to candidate events.

Success criteria

  • Explains what distinguishes a turning point from a significant event using at least two explicit criteria
  • Correctly classifies at least one event as a turning point and at least one as significant but not a turning point, justifying each

Prior knowledge

Students have examined turning points of WW1 (AC9HH9K10) and can apply the concept of historical significance. From AC9HH10K01 they understand the course of the war at a structural level. From AC9HH10K02 they have examined specific Australian campaigns. They have studied the rise of fascism and Nazi ideology (AC9HH10K01, N2), giving them the ideological context for the Holocaust. They have not yet examined the Holocaust as a conceptually distinct historical phenomenon requiring its own analytical framework, nor analysed the atomic bomb decision as an ethical and strategic problem.

Width

Minimum
Students explain the turning-point concept using explicit criteria and apply it to at least two events — correctly classifying one as a turning point and one as significant but not a turning point.
Typical
Students establish the distinction — a turning point shifts momentum or outcome decisively; a significant event matters but does not necessarily change trajectory; apply criteria (shift in balance of power, change in strategic situation, irreversibility of consequence) to a range of WW2 events; practise the distinction on clear cases before applying it to contested ones.
Wider
  • Examine whether the Eastern Front (Operation Barbarossa, Stalingrad) or the Western Front narrative better explains where and how the war was decided — applying turning-point criteria to compare theatres
  • Investigate the concept of contingency in history — using a turning-point event to ask 'what if the outcome had been different?' as a way of testing the significance claim

Hinge

The turning-point concept with explicit criteria is the analytical scaffold N2 (European turning points) and N3 (Asia-Pacific turning points) apply to specific events. Without it, students list events rather than evaluate them. It also underpins the Holocaust analysis in N4 — students need to ask 'was the Holocaust a turning point?' in what sense? — and the atomic bomb analysis in N6.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly treat 'turning point' and 'significant event' as synonyms. Making the distinction explicit and practising it on clear cases — Midway clearly qualifies; the bombing of Darwin is significant but not a turning point — builds the concept before it is applied to harder cases in N2–N3 and contested cases in N4–N6.
