Collaboration and institutional roles in preservation

AC9HH7K07 n3 · waypoint 3 of 3 · Year 7 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain the roles of multiple stakeholders — Traditional Owners, researchers, government bodies, and institutions — in cultural preservation, and describe how collaboration and world heritage frameworks support the protection of significant sites.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two stakeholder groups and explains the role each plays in cultural preservation.
  • Describes at least one institutional mechanism (heritage listing, museum/archive role) and explains how it contributes to preservation.
  • Explains that effective preservation requires collaboration — not sole control by any single stakeholder.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Lake Mungo — ownership, repatriation, and collaboration.

Width

Minimum
Describe the roles of at least two stakeholders (e.g. Traditional Owners and research institutions, or government bodies and community groups) in cultural preservation, and identify one institutional mechanism (e.g. world heritage listing, national galleries and archives) that supports preservation.
Typical
Describe the roles of Traditional Owners, researchers, national and state institutions (galleries, libraries, archives, museums, historical societies), and government bodies in cultural preservation. Explain how collaboration between these stakeholders — rather than any single authority — is required for effective preservation. Describe how world heritage listing (e.g. Budj Bim, Willandra Lakes, Uluru-Kata Tjuta, Kakadu) functions as a mechanism for international recognition and protection.
Wider
  • Examine the world heritage criteria in detail — examining which criteria Budj Bim, Willandra Lakes, and Kakadu satisfy, and what this reveals about how international frameworks assess the significance of First Nations cultural landscapes.
  • Investigate the roles and responsibilities of specific institutions — such as the Australian Institute of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Studies (AIATSIS) — in curating, repatriating, and making accessible First Nations cultural heritage.
  • Explore the tensions that can arise in collaborative heritage management — when Traditional Owner priorities, research interests, and tourism/access demands conflict — and how these are navigated through negotiated frameworks.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Integration: a combining task at the wider tierThis node integrates the cultural obligations (Node 1) and the specific repatriation case (Node 2) into the descriptor's full demand: an understanding of how collaboration between First Nations Australians and other individuals and groups — including institutions and international bodies — functions to ensure cultural preservation. Students must coordinate the relational obligations, the specific example, and the institutional mechanisms into a coherent account.
