Nuclear energy and climate change — evolving debates

AC9HH10K20 n4 · waypoint 4 of 4 · Year 10 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can discuss the global debate over nuclear energy and the evolution of the climate change debate, examining how each debate has changed over time from the 1960s to the present.

Success criteria

  • Explains how the nuclear energy debate has changed since the 1960s — identifying at least two stages
  • Explains how the climate change debate has evolved — identifying at least two stages and what drove each change
  • Discusses how scientific evidence interacts with political interests in at least one of the two debates

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 3: The rise of extremism and the post-9/11 world.

Width

Minimum
Students explain how the nuclear energy debate has changed since the 1960s; explain how the climate change debate has evolved — identifying at least two stages of change.
Typical
Students trace the nuclear energy debate — initial post-war enthusiasm for civilian nuclear power, the anti-nuclear movement of the 1970s–80s, the effects of Three Mile Island (1979), Chernobyl (1986), and Fukushima (2011) on public opinion, the contemporary revival of nuclear energy debate in the context of climate change; trace the climate change debate — early scientific consensus building (1960s–80s), IPCC establishment (1988), Kyoto Protocol (1997), growing public concern (2000s), the debate over denial and vested interests; examine how each debate illustrates how scientific evidence interacts with political interests and public values.
Wider
  • Examine how the nuclear energy debate has returned to relevance in the context of climate change — analysing the arguments for nuclear as low-carbon energy and the arguments against based on waste, cost, and risk, and evaluating which is better supported by evidence
  • Investigate how climate denial has operated — examining the strategies used to contest scientific consensus (manufactured controversy, funding of counter-research, political lobbying) and what this reveals about the relationship between scientific knowledge and political power

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents need to understand both debates as having changed over time — not as static positions for and against. Scaffolding the temporal dimension (what changed? when? what drove the change?) applies the N1 interpretive framework to live debates and prevents students from simply stating current positions.
