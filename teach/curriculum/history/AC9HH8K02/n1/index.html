The feudal hierarchy

AC9HH8K02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 8 History · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can describe the structure of feudal society — including the roles and responsibilities of the king, nobles, Church, knights, peasants, and men and women — and explain the obligations that structured relationships between them.

Success criteria

  • Names the main social groups in the feudal hierarchy and describes the obligations that structured their relationships
  • Distinguishes between at least two groups in terms of their roles and status (e.g. nobility vs clergy, knights vs peasantry)

Prior knowledge

Students explain the organisation and roles of key groups in ancient society and how they influenced change (AC9HH7K10). They have not yet examined how group roles and relationships were structured in the medieval European context — the feudal hierarchy, the Church as an institution, the emerging merchant classes, and continuity and change in daily life across the period.

Width

Minimum
At least three groups in the feudal hierarchy with a description of their roles and the obligations between them.
Typical
The full feudal hierarchy with specific roles, rights and obligations at each level; the distinction between secular and religious authority; the differing roles of men and women across social strata.
Wider
  • Compare the feudal hierarchy in different regions of Europe (e.g. England vs the Italian city-states) to see how the model varied in practice and over time
  • Examine how the feudal hierarchy was justified ideologically through religious and natural order arguments, and why those justifications were eventually contested

Hinge

Students who cannot place groups in the hierarchy and explain the obligations between them cannot make sense of how power was exercised, contested or reformed in subsequent nodes on turning points and power. The hierarchy is the structural scaffold for all group-relationship analysis.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents often reduce feudalism to a flat list of groups. Scaffold the relational logic — each level owes something to levels above and below — and the distinction between land tenure and personal loyalty as organising mechanisms.
