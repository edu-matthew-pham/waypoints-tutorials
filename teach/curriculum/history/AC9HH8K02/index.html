Groups in medieval Europe

AC9HH8K02 · Year 8 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Descriptor

the roles and relationships of different groups in Medieval, Renaissance or pre-modern Europe

Prior knowledge

Students explain the organisation and roles of key groups in ancient society and how they influenced change (AC9HH7K10). They have not yet examined how group roles and relationships were structured in the medieval European context — the feudal hierarchy, the Church as an institution, the emerging merchant classes, and continuity and change in daily life across the period.

By the end

Students explain the roles and relationships of different groups in Medieval, Renaissance or pre-modern Europe — including the feudal hierarchy, the institutional role of the Church, the emergence of merchant and artisan classes, the experiences of women, and continuities and changes in daily life — and describe how these roles both shaped and reflected the power structures of the period.

ACARA elaborations

Examples from ACARA that illustrate this standard. They are examples, not a checklist, and belong to the standard as a whole.

  • describing the structure of feudal society, including the role and responsibilities of the king, nobles, church, knights, peasants, and women and men
  • explaining the continuities and changes in society in one of the following areas: crime and punishment; military and defence systems; towns, cities and commerce; farming and the impact on the environment such as tree clearance
  • explaining how medieval society changed and affected the environment; for example, harnessing water and wind power for milling, urban settlements and agriculture, management practices and legal structures of medieval forests such as the English royal forests
  • describing key changes of life in the late Medieval period such as the spread of literacy and the development of new ways to disseminate ideas through schools and universities, scientific academies, debating societies, literary salons, coffee houses, and the reach of printed books, journals and pamphlets
  • explaining reactions to the Black Death, such as the emergence of flagellants (those who would whip themselves to be free of sin) and the persecution of Jewish people
  • identifying the similarities and differences of daily life between the popolo minuto and popolo grasso
  • describing the way of life of people in Renaissance Italy; for example, the role of men in tending the fields or merchant shops, the role of women in the society and the influence of government in particular city-states such as Naples (a monarchy) and Florence (a republic)
  • identifying the spread of Renaissance culture to England; for example, the rise of literature through Shakespeare
  • The emergence of the modern world
  • describing the contribution of key theories such as Nicolaus Copernicus' De revolutionibus orbium coelestium, Galileo's Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems and Isaac Newton's Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica to the changes brought about by the Scientific Revolution
  • examining the changes in economic activity created by the discoveries of new commodities, making the costs of basic foodstuffs lower and the quality higher, and the advancements of technologies such as in tools, armaments and shipbuilding

Waypoints

  • 1. The feudal hierarchyHingeStudents can describe the structure of feudal society — including the roles and responsibilities of the king, nobles, Church, knights, peasants, and men and women — and explain the obligations that structured relationships between them.
  • 2. The role of the ChurchStudents can explain the role of the Church as a social, political and cultural institution in medieval Europe — including its authority over spiritual and everyday life, its provision of education and welfare, and its relationship with secular rulers.
  • 3. Continuity and change in daily lifeStudents can describe continuities and changes in daily life across medieval society in at least one area — such as crime and punishment, towns and commerce, farming and environment — and explain what drove those changes.
  • 4. Emerging classes and the Scientific RevolutionStudents can describe the emergence of merchant and artisan classes, the spread of literacy and new ideas, and the contribution of key theories to the Scientific Revolution, and explain how these developments modified medieval social and intellectual structures.
