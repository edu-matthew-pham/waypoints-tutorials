Usefulness — what a source can tell us for a specific inquiry

AC9HH7S04 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Year 7 History · Historical skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can distinguish between accuracy and usefulness as separate analytical concepts, and explain that a source with limited accuracy can still be useful as evidence — for different purposes, or for revealing the perspective of its producer.

Success criteria

  • Correctly defines accuracy and usefulness as separate concepts.
  • Identifies information in a source that is useful as evidence for a specific inquiry question.
  • Explains at least one case where a source with limited accuracy is still useful — and why.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Accuracy — fact, opinion, and source reliability.

Width

Minimum
Explain the difference between accuracy (is it factually reliable?) and usefulness (does it help us answer a specific inquiry question?), and identify information within a source that is useful as evidence for a specific historical question.
Typical
Distinguish accuracy from usefulness and apply both to a source. Explain that a biased or inaccurate source is still useful as evidence of perspective, belief, or propaganda — that its bias is itself historically informative. Identify information within a source that is useful for a specific inquiry, including cases where limited evidence about a group (e.g. women, slaves) still provides useful insights into power structures.
Wider
  • Examine cases where a source's limitations make it useful in unexpected ways — where propaganda, myth, or partial accounts reveal the beliefs and priorities of their producers rather than the events they describe.
  • Investigate how the usefulness of a source depends entirely on the inquiry question — a source that is not useful for one question may be highly useful for another — and develop criteria for assessing usefulness relative to a specific question.
  • Explore how historians have found and used sources that were originally dismissed as unreliable — for example, how oral traditions initially dismissed as 'myth' have been vindicated as evidence of real events.

Hinge

Students who conflate accuracy and usefulness cannot produce the integrated evidence assessment in N3 — N3 requires both dimensions to be clearly distinct before they can be synthesised into a combined conclusion about a source's value as evidence.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents commonly conflate accuracy and usefulness — assuming that an inaccurate or biased source is 'not useful'. Multiple examples of sources that are useful precisely because of their bias, partiality, or limited perspective — propaganda revealing official ideology, a landlord's record revealing the economic conditions of tenants — specifically resolve this conflation. Breadth across source types and inquiry contexts is what builds the distinction.
