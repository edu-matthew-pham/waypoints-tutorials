Integrating accuracy and usefulness — evidence assessment

AC9HH7S04 n3 · waypoint 3 of 3 · Year 7 History · Historical skills

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can assess both the accuracy and the usefulness of a source as evidence for a specific historical inquiry, integrating the two dimensions into a reasoned conclusion about the source's value.

Success criteria

  • Assesses both accuracy and usefulness of a source for a specific inquiry question.
  • Synthesises both dimensions into a reasoned conclusion — not two separate boxes but an integrated assessment.
  • Explicitly states what the source can and cannot contribute to answering the inquiry question.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Usefulness — what a source can tell us for a specific inquiry.

Width

Minimum
For a given source and inquiry question, assess both its accuracy and its usefulness and explain what the source can contribute to answering the inquiry question.
Typical
For a primary or secondary source from the History Y7 units, assess its accuracy (factual reliability, bias, corroboration needs) and usefulness (what it can contribute to a specific inquiry question), and synthesise these into a reasoned assessment of the source's value as evidence. Explain both what the source does and does not contribute to the inquiry.
Wider
  • Apply accuracy and usefulness assessment to a set of sources for the same inquiry question, and explain how a historian would combine sources of varying accuracy and usefulness to build a more complete picture.
  • Investigate how the standards for accuracy and usefulness assessment have evolved in historical practice — examining how historians have debated what counts as reliable evidence and how these debates have shaped what questions can be asked.
  • Examine a case where historians have disagreed about the accuracy or usefulness of a significant source — such as debates about the reliability of ancient accounts of battles or the usefulness of highly stylised art as evidence of daily life.

Response modes
