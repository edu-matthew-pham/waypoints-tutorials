Accuracy — fact, opinion, and source reliability

AC9HH7S04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 7 History · Historical skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can distinguish between factual claims and opinions in a source, apply questions to assess a source's accuracy, and explain what factors affect a source's reliability.

Lessons on this site

  • Fact, opinion and accuracy

Success criteria

  • Correctly distinguishes at least two factual claims from two opinions in a given source.
  • Applies at least two accuracy questions to the source and draws a reasoned conclusion about reliability.
  • Identifies at least one factor (bias, limited information, purpose) that affects the source's accuracy.

Prior knowledge

Students can identify the origin, content, context, and purpose of primary and secondary sources (AC9HH7S03). They have not yet applied the concepts of accuracy and usefulness as distinct analytical lenses — understanding what makes a source accurate (factually reliable) vs what makes it useful (valuable for a specific inquiry, even if biased or partial).

Width

Minimum
Distinguish between a factual claim and an opinion in a given source, and identify at least one question that can be used to assess the source's accuracy.
Typical
Apply the accuracy questions ('Where does it come from?', 'What information is provided?', 'Is it factual or opinion?', 'What other sources might support or challenge it?') to assess a primary or secondary source's reliability. Distinguish between facts ('Some gladiators wore helmets') and opinions ('All gladiators were brave'), and explain what factors — bias, purpose, limited information — affect a source's accuracy.
Wider
  • Examine how bias affects accuracy — distinguishing between deliberate distortion (propaganda) and unintentional bias (perspective limitations, cultural assumptions) — and how historians account for both types in source assessment.
  • Investigate how corroboration works as an accuracy-checking strategy — examining how historians compare multiple sources to identify where accounts converge (suggesting accuracy) and diverge (requiring interpretation).
  • Explore how the concept of 'accuracy' differs for different source types — a census record can be assessed for accuracy differently than a personal memoir or a commemorative inscription.

Hinge

Students who cannot reliably distinguish factual claims from opinions, or who apply accuracy assessment imprecisely, will conflate accuracy and usefulness in N2 — the distinction requires a stable accuracy concept to differentiate from.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Consolidation: more practise steps, short feedback loopsThe fact/opinion distinction and the accuracy question routine are precision skills that require practice to become reliable. Students commonly slide between fact and opinion without noticing, or treat all historical claims as equally uncertain. Regular practice applying the accuracy questions to varied source types builds the habitual precision that source analysis requires.
