Long-term structural causes — MAIN

AC9HH9K08 n1 · waypoint 1 of 6 · Year 9 History · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify and describe the four long-term structural causes of WW1 — militarism, alliances, imperialism, nationalism — and explain what each contributed to rising tensions in Europe.

Success criteria

  • Names and describes all four structural causes with at least one concrete example each
  • Distinguishes the four structural causes from each other — describes what is distinctive about each

Prior knowledge

Students can identify causes and effects of significant historical events and describe turning points in earlier periods (AC9HH8K03, AC9HH8K08, AC9HH8K13). They have not yet examined the alliance system or balance-of-power logic in early 20th-century Europe, the mechanisms by which a localised conflict escalated to continental war, or the specific motivational framework — Empire loyalty, adventure, propaganda, peer pressure — that shaped Australian enlistment decisions.

Width

Minimum
Students name all four structural causes and give one concrete description of each — e.g. the arms race between Britain and Germany (militarism), the Triple Alliance and Triple Entente (alliances), European powers competing for colonial territory (imperialism), Pan-Slavic and Pan-German nationalist movements (nationalism).
Typical
Students describe how each structural cause developed during the late 19th and early 20th centuries; provide multiple examples per cause; explain how each cause independently raised the probability of conflict; use the MAIN acronym as an organising framework; distinguish long-term causes from immediate triggers.
Wider
  • Compare how militarism, imperialism and nationalism operated differently in different countries — e.g. how German nationalism differed from Austro-Hungarian imperialism — to show that these causes were not uniform across Europe
  • Examine the role of colonial competition in Africa (Moroccan Crises 1905, 1911) or the Balkans as a zone of competing imperial interests, connecting back to AC9HH9K07 on Australia's development in relation to world powers by 1914
  • Investigate how the press and popular culture in Britain, France and Germany actively promoted nationalist and militarist sentiment — making the connection to propaganda that anticipates the enlistment strand later in this descriptor

Hinge

The four structural causes are the conceptual foundation for understanding escalation. Students who cannot place these causes before the assassination cannot explain why a local crisis in Sarajevo triggered continent-wide mobilisation. The alliance mechanism in particular is the prerequisite for N2 — without understanding that alliance obligations automatically widened any conflict, the July Crisis sequence is incomprehensible.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly conflate causes — treating the alliance system and nationalism as interchangeable, or treating any tension as a 'cause' without understanding structural vs trigger distinction. Explicitly mapping each cause and its distinct mechanism prevents this conflation and builds the conceptual scaffold for N2.
