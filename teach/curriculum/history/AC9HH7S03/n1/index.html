Origin and content — the basic analytical questions

AC9HH7S03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 7 History · Historical skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify the origin of a source (who produced it, when, and where) and describe its content (what it shows, says, or contains), applying the questions 'Who?', 'When?', 'What?'

Success criteria

  • Correctly identifies the producer (who), period (when), and location (where) of a source's origin.
  • Accurately describes the content — what the source shows, contains, or records — without yet interpreting what it means.
  • Distinguishes origin from content — keeps the 'who made it' question separate from the 'what does it show' question.

Prior knowledge

Students can evaluate primary and secondary sources to determine origin, purpose, and perspectives (AC9HS6S04). They have not yet applied these skills specifically to historical sources in the context of historical inquiry — distinguishing origin, content, context, and purpose as four distinct analytical dimensions, and applying disciplinary questions ('Who wrote/produced this?', 'When?', 'Why?', 'What does it show about the past?') systematically.

Width

Minimum
For a given source, identify who produced it, when it was produced, and describe what it shows or contains.
Typical
For a given primary or secondary source, systematically identify its origin — who produced it (individual, institution, group), when it was produced, and where — and describe its content — what it shows, depicts, records, or argues. Apply this to multiple source types (photographs, artefacts, texts, buildings).
Wider
  • Examine cases where origin is difficult to establish — sources without clear attribution, ancient texts with disputed authorship, artefacts without provenance — and discuss what the difficulty of establishing origin means for interpreting the source.
  • Investigate how the origin of a source shapes its content — why an official court record contains certain types of information and omits others, why a personal letter reveals different things than a public inscription.
  • Explore the specific challenge of establishing origin for some ancient sources — such as the Kimberley Bradshaw paintings, where origin in terms of culture, period, and even subject matter remains debated.

Hinge

Students who conflate origin with content (or skip origin to jump to interpretation) will apply OCCP incompletely in N3 — context and purpose analysis requires a reliable origin/content foundation, and the synthesis node cannot integrate dimensions that haven't been clearly separated.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Consolidation: more practise steps, short feedback loopsSystematic application of 'Who? When? What?' to diverse source types is a precision skill that requires practice across many examples. Students frequently conflate origin with content (what is shown vs who made it) or skip origin analysis to jump to interpretation. Regular practice across different source types — artefacts, photographs, texts, buildings — builds the reliable habit of beginning source analysis with origin and content before moving to context and purpose.
