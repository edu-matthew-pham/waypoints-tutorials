Evaluating interpretations

AC9HH8K16 n3 · waypoint 3 of 3 · Year 8 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can evaluate competing interpretations of the chosen Asia-Pacific society — identifying which is better supported by evidence and explaining what the interpretive debate reveals about the nature of historical knowledge.

Success criteria

  • Evaluates at least one interpretation of the chosen Asia-Pacific society using evidence
  • Explains what the interpretive debate reveals about the nature of historical knowledge — including the role of evidence, perspective and the limits of sources

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Competing interpretations.

Width

Minimum
An evaluation of at least one interpretation using evidence to assess its strengths or limitations.
Typical
Evaluation of competing interpretations using source evidence; the significance of Japan's closure to foreigners under the Tokugawa Shogunate and the impact of Perry's visit; the Polynesian expansion debate evaluated against archaeological and environmental evidence; the Khmer decline theories assessed for evidential support.
Wider
  • For Japan: evaluate the significance of the range of reasons for Japan's closure to foreigners under the Tokugawa Shogunate and assess the impact of Perry's visit (1853) — examining both the interpretation that isolation was a strength and the interpretation that it created vulnerability
  • For Polynesia: evaluate how each expansion theory (accidental vs intentional; west-east vs east-west) is supported by different types of evidence, and consider what accepting one interpretation over another means for our understanding of Pacific peoples

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Integration: a combining task at the wider tierEvaluation integrates source-challenge analysis (Node 1) and competing-interpretation identification (Node 2). Invest in helping students move from noting that interpretations differ to making a reasoned judgement about which is better supported and why.
