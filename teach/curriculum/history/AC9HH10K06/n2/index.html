The Anzac legend extended — WW2 in Australian national memory

AC9HH10K06 n2 · waypoint 2 of 4 · Year 10 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain how the Anzac legend developed through WW1 was extended to WW2, and how specific WW2 campaigns — particularly Kokoda — were incorporated into Australian national memory.

Success criteria

  • Explains how Kokoda was incorporated into the Anzac tradition — identifying the qualities that made it suitable
  • Identifies at least one similarity and one difference between WW1 and WW2 commemoration in Australia
  • Explains why some WW2 campaigns are more prominent in national memory than others

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Commemoration as historical and political practice.

Width

Minimum
Students explain how Kokoda became incorporated into the Anzac tradition; identify at least one similarity and one difference in how WW1 and WW2 are commemorated.
Typical
Students examine how the Anzac tradition established through WW1 was available as a commemorative framework for WW2; explain why Kokoda was particularly well-suited to extension of the Anzac legend (similar qualities of endurance, mateship, defence against apparent odds); examine how WW2 commemoration differs from WW1 commemoration — different battles, different theatres, a more defensive and Pacific-focused story; explain why some WW2 campaigns are more prominent in national memory than others.
Wider
  • Examine the role of the Pacific theatre in Australian national memory versus the Middle East and European theatres — analysing why Kokoda and Singapore dominate Australian WW2 memory while the North African and European campaigns receive less attention
  • Investigate how WW2 veterans' own relationship to the Anzac tradition evolved over time — examining whether they embraced or resisted being incorporated into a tradition defined by WW1

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly assume the Anzac tradition automatically applies to all Australian wars without examining why specific campaigns are incorporated and others are not. Scaffolding the question — what qualities does the Anzac legend require? which WW2 campaigns meet those requirements? — makes the extension of the legend visible as a choice rather than an inevitability.
