Framework for evaluating individual and group contributions

AC9HH10K10 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 10 History · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can apply a framework for evaluating the contribution of individuals and groups to social change — distinguishing types of contribution and criteria for assessing extent of change.

Success criteria

  • Explains at least two criteria for evaluating an individual or group's contribution to social change
  • Applies criteria to one named individual and produces an evaluative claim — not merely a description
  • Distinguishes contribution to change from sole causation of change

Prior knowledge

Students have examined the legislative framework of exclusion, early organised campaigns, and the Stolen Generations in K09. They understand the model of organised political activism established by the AAPA, AAL, and the 1938 Day of Mourning. They have not yet examined the specific contributions of individuals across the broader campaign period, the role of allied groups (Christian, union, labour), or the extent-of-change question.

Width

Minimum
Students explain at least two criteria for evaluating the contribution of an individual or group to social change and apply them to one example.
Typical
Students establish criteria for evaluating contribution — what kinds of action produce change (legislation, public opinion, direct action, legal challenge, cultural influence)? how do we assess extent of change (what changed, how durably, how broadly)? apply criteria to one named individual before proceeding to comparison; explain the difference between contribution to change and the achievement of change — individuals rarely produce change alone.
Wider
  • Examine the historiographical debate over the role of individuals versus structural forces in producing social change — applying this to the First Nations rights context to ask: would the 1967 Referendum have happened without specific individuals, or were structural conditions ripe regardless?
  • Investigate the role of oral history in documenting the contributions of First Nations activists — examining how oral sources capture dimensions of the movement that written records miss

Hinge

The extent-of-change evaluation in the progression endpoint requires explicit criteria. Students who lack this framework either praise all contributions uncritically or reduce the question to 'who was most famous.' Without criteria, they cannot evaluate the extent to which individuals brought change — which is the descriptor's central analytical demand.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe 'extent to which they brought change' question requires explicit scaffolding. Making the criteria concrete — type of action, durability of change, breadth of impact, connection to specific legislative or social outcomes — is the prerequisite for genuine evaluation rather than narrative description.
