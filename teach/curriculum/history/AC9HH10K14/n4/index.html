The environmental movement — changing perspectives on sustainability

AC9HH10K14 n4 · waypoint 4 of 4 · Year 10 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain the rise of the environmental movement and analyse how perspectives on sustainability and environmental protection have changed since the 1960s.

Success criteria

  • Explains at least two causes of the environmental movement's rise
  • Describes how perspectives on environmental protection changed from the 1950s to the present
  • Connects at least one specific event or development to a shift in public or policy perspectives

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 3: Public health, longevity, and standard of living.

Width

Minimum
Students explain at least two causes of the rise of the environmental movement; describe how perspectives on environmental protection have changed since the 1960s.
Typical
Students examine the causes of the environmental movement's rise — rapid population growth, urbanisation, industrial pollution, species extinction, nuclear testing, Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962); trace the changing perspectives — from growth-as-progress in the 1950s to environmental concern in the 1970s to the emergence of sustainability as a policy framework; examine key developments in renewable energy technology and sustainability measures; explain how global events (Chernobyl, Exxon Valdez, ozone depletion) accelerated perspective change.
Wider
  • Examine how the environmental movement changed from a marginal counterculture concern in the 1960s to a mainstream political issue by the 1990s — analysing what drove that shift
  • Investigate the development of international environmental agreements — from Stockholm (1972) to Kyoto (1997) to Paris (2015) — evaluating whether international cooperation has been adequate to the scale of the problem

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents need to apply the N1 framework explicitly here — why did perspectives on the environment change? what new information, consequences, or values drove the shift? This prevents description of the environmental movement as a simple narrative of growing awareness.
