Public health, longevity, and standard of living

AC9HH10K14 n3 · waypoint 3 of 4 · Year 10 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain the major improvements in public health and standard of living during the 20th century and discuss how perspectives on population growth and resource depletion changed.

Success criteria

  • Explains at least two improvements in public health or standard of living with evidence
  • Explains how perspectives on population growth changed over the 20th century — identifying what drove the shift
  • Describes the uneven distribution of improvements across different parts of the world

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Technological development and its effects.

Width

Minimum
Students explain at least two improvements in public health or standard of living and explain how perspectives on population growth changed over the 20th century.
Typical
Students examine improvements in life expectancy and their causes — vaccines, antibiotics, public sanitation, improved nutrition, medical technology; examine the growth in world population during the 20th century and its uneven distribution across regions; examine how the celebration of population growth in the post-war period gave way to concern about resource depletion and sustainability; explain how changes in life expectancy varied across different parts of the world — the gap between developed and developing countries.
Wider
  • Examine the demographic transition model — analysing how improvements in health and standard of living produce predictable changes in birth and death rates, and what this reveals about the relationship between development and population growth
  • Investigate the depletion of natural resources — examining how post-war growth assumptions about unlimited resource availability were challenged by the oil crisis (1973) and subsequent environmental data

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly treat population growth as a straightforward good or straightforward problem without examining how perspectives shifted during the 20th century. Scaffolding the shift — from post-war pro-natalist policies to 1970s sustainability concerns — applies the N1 framework to a concrete case.
