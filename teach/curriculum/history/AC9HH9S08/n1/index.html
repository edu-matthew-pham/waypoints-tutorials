Developing a historical argument that acknowledges interpretation

AC9HH9S08 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 9 History · Historical skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can develop a historical argument that identifies different possibilities in interpretation and argues a particular point of view with consistent reference to evidence.

Success criteria

  • Develops a historical argument that takes a clear position and supports it with at least two pieces of evidence
  • Acknowledges at least one alternative interpretation or piece of countervailing evidence, and explains why the argument's position is better supported

Prior knowledge

In Year 7 students created descriptions, explanations and historical arguments using knowledge and concepts (AC9HH7S08). In Year 8 they continued this (AC9HH8S08). Y9 adds two significant extensions: developing arguments that identify different interpretive possibilities and argue a particular point of view, and selecting appropriate communication formats. Students must now produce genuinely argumentative historical writing, not only explanations.

Width

Minimum
Write a historical argument that takes a clear position on a historical question and supports it with at least two pieces of evidence.
Typical
Develop a historical argument that identifies different possibilities in interpretation and argues a particular point of view with consistent reference to evidence; use historical concepts and terms (cause, significance, change, continuity, perspective) in the argument; demonstrate that a historical argument acknowledges countervailing evidence or interpretations rather than ignoring them.
Wider
  • Develop a historical argument that explicitly addresses a contested interpretation — engaging with a counterargument and explaining why the evidence better supports one interpretation over another
  • Produce a historical argument drawing on sources representing multiple perspectives (coloniser and colonised, majority and minority) and reflecting explicitly on how source selection shapes the argument
  • Construct a historical argument that uses both quantitative evidence (data, statistics) and qualitative evidence (primary source quotations, historian's accounts) — demonstrating the integration of different evidence types in argumentation

Hinge

Developing a historical argument that acknowledges interpretive possibilities — rather than simply asserting a position — is the defining advance at Year 9. Students who produce assertion-only arguments without engaging with countervailing evidence or interpretations are not meeting the descriptor's full demand.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe shift from explanation to argumentation that acknowledges interpretation requires explicit modelling. Students need to see what it looks like to acknowledge a counterargument before they can produce this themselves. Model the structure: 'While some historians argue X, the evidence better supports Y because...'
