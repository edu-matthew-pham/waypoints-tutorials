Theories of human evolution

AC9HH7K01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 7 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify competing theories about early human evolution, including the Out of Africa Theory and the Modern Theory of Evolution, and describe what each theory proposes about human origins.

Success criteria

  • Names and briefly describes the Out of Africa Theory as the predominant theory of early human origins.
  • Identifies that historians and scientists work with competing or evolving theories rather than a single settled account of human evolution.
  • Uses the term 'theory' correctly in a historical/scientific context — as an evidence-based interpretation, not a guess.

Prior knowledge

Students can describe significant individuals, events and ideas in Australia's post-Federation history and explain how migration to Australia changed society (AC9HS6K01, AC9HS6K03). They have not yet studied human prehistory, early human evolution, or the movement of peoples before written records. They have not worked with geological or archaeological timescales, or with theories about human origins.

Width

Minimum
Identify and briefly describe the Out of Africa Theory as the major theory of human evolution — that anatomically modern humans evolved in Africa and spread outward.
Typical
Identify and describe both the Out of Africa Theory and the Modern Theory of Evolution, noting what distinguishes them and why historians and scientists work with competing interpretations of human origins.
Wider
  • Explore how the Out of Africa Theory has been refined over time as new genetic and archaeological evidence has emerged, including debates about whether there was a single dispersal or multiple waves.
  • Compare how different knowledge systems — scientific, archaeological, and First Nations oral traditions — offer different but potentially complementary accounts of human origins.
  • Investigate how the concept of 'theory' in historical and scientific inquiry differs from everyday usage, connecting to how historians use evidence to build and revise interpretations.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Repertoire building: more cases to sort or classifyStudents at Year 7 entry have no prior schema for deep-time human history. They need to accumulate a basic repertoire of what these theories propose before they can reason about causes of migration or evaluate evidence. This node builds the conceptual vocabulary (evolution, migration, dispersal, interpretation) that underpins all subsequent nodes.
Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepThe comparison between scientific and First Nations knowledge systems about human origins is inherently rich — it surfaces questions about how we know what we know about the deep past, and raises issues of cultural authority and epistemic pluralism. Advancing students benefit from encountering multiple knowledge systems as complementary rather than competing, building the understanding that different epistemological approaches coexist.
