← AC9HH7K01 Early human migration

Evidence types and interpretations

AC9HH7K01 n3 · waypoint 3 of 3 · Year 7 History · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify different types of evidence used by historians and archaeologists — including excavation and stratigraphy, oral history, and radiocarbon dating — and describe how each type informs interpretations about early human migration.

Success criteria

  • Names at least two distinct evidence types and describes what each can reveal about early human migration.
  • Explains that interpretations about early human migration are based on multiple types of evidence, not a single source.
  • Does not treat evidence as synonymous with proof — recognises that evidence informs and supports interpretations while leaving room for revision.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Environmental causes of migration.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least two types of evidence (e.g. stratigraphy, radiocarbon dating) and describe in basic terms what each can tell us about early human migration.
Typical
Identify and describe three evidence types — excavation and stratigraphy, oral history, and radiocarbon dating — explaining what each reveals about early human migration and why using multiple evidence types produces stronger interpretations.
Wider
  • Examine how genetic evidence (e.g. DNA analysis of ancient remains, mitochondrial DNA tracing) has been used alongside archaeological evidence to map migration routes, including the movement of peoples to Australia.
  • Explore the limitations of each evidence type — what stratigraphy cannot tell us, where radiocarbon dating has error ranges, and how oral histories can preserve long-timescale knowledge — and why historians must triangulate across sources.
  • Investigate specific examples where different evidence types have produced conflicting interpretations of the same migration event, and how historians have responded to that conflict.

Hinge

Students who conflate evidence with proof will apply a naive reliability model in all subsequent source analysis (S03–S04), treating a single source as settling interpretive claims rather than understanding that interpretations are built from converging lines of evidence.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents commonly conflate 'evidence' with 'proof', treating a single piece of evidence as settling an interpretation. Exposure to multiple, distinct evidence types — each with different strengths and limitations — helps students see that interpretations are built from converging lines of evidence, not confirmed by a single source. Broader contexts (stratigraphy, oral history, radiocarbon dating, DNA) are specifically what resolves this misconception.
Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe logic of how evidence informs interpretation — rather than proves it — requires explicit scaffolding. Students need to understand the inference chain: evidence type → what it can detect → what interpretive claim it supports → what it cannot resolve. This reasoning structure is foundational for all subsequent source-analysis skills in the S strand.
