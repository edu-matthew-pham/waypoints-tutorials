The post-war domestic settlement and its limits

AC9HH10K12 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 10 History · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain the post-war domestic settlement — the social and economic pressures that returned women to domestic roles — and why this settlement produced the conditions for organised feminist activism.

Success criteria

  • Describes at least two features of the post-war domestic settlement — including the marriage bar as a specific mechanism
  • Explains the gap between formal citizenship equality and lived employment/legal reality
  • Explains how this gap motivated organised feminist activism

Prior knowledge

Students have examined women's wartime roles and the change-versus-continuity pattern in K04 — including that most wartime gains were reversed post-war. They have studied suffrage from earlier year levels. They have not yet examined the post-1945 women's movement as a sustained organised campaign, the specific legal changes that followed, or the ongoing gap between formal equality and lived experience.

Width

Minimum
Students describe at least two features of the post-war domestic settlement; explain how each created conditions that motivated feminist activism.
Typical
Students examine the post-war return to domesticity — government incentives, social messaging, industrial restructuring that removed women from wartime roles; explain the 'marriage bar' as a concrete legislative mechanism (women required to resign from public service upon marriage); examine the gap between formal citizenship equality and the lived reality of employment and legal discrimination; explain how this gap — experienced by educated women in particular — motivated the second-wave feminist movement of the 1960s–70s.
Wider
  • Examine The Female Eunuch (1970) and The Feminine Mystique (1963) as texts that named the post-war domestic settlement as a problem — analysing how intellectual frameworks shaped the women's movement
  • Compare Australia's post-war domestic settlement with Britain's or the USA's — examining whether the scale and character of the feminist response differed and why

Hinge

Understanding the post-war domestic settlement — specifically the marriage bar and employment discrimination as concrete mechanisms, not just general sexism — is the prerequisite for understanding what the women's movement was campaigning against. Students who lack this context cannot explain why specific campaigns targeted specific changes.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly treat the women's movement as a response to general inequality without examining the specific structural mechanisms that made activism necessary. Making the marriage bar, employment law, and social pressure explicit — as mechanisms, not just attitudes — provides the concrete targets that explain why particular campaigns emerged when they did.
