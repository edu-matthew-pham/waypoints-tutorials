The Stolen Generations — forcible removal of children

AC9HH9K03 n4 · waypoint 4 of 4 · Year 9 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can describe the forcible removal of First Nations children from their families, including the motivations for removal, the practices and laws in place, and the experiences of those separated.

Success criteria

  • Describes the forcible removal of First Nations children — including motivations, laws and practices
  • Describes the experiences of separation for children and families, using at least one specific example or account

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 3: First Nations responses to colonisation.

Width

Minimum
Students describe that First Nations children were forcibly removed from families under colonial and state policies, identify at least two motivations given for removal, and describe the experience of separation for children and families.
Typical
Students describe the ideological motivations for removal — assimilation ideology, the belief that Aboriginal cultures were dying out, the intent to 'breed out' Aboriginal identity; the specific laws and government structures that enabled removal (Protection Acts, Boards); the range of experiences — children sent to institutions, domestic service, separated from language and culture, denied contact with family; the long-term effects on individuals, families and communities; connect to the earlier colonial project of dispossession from N1–N2 — removal as a continuation of the project to eliminate First Nations culture and presence.
Wider
  • Examine personal accounts from members of the Stolen Generations — survivor testimonies, the Bringing Them Home report — to ground the general pattern in specific human experiences
  • Connect the Stolen Generations to the long-term intergenerational effects documented in contemporary data — examining how historical policy continues to shape contemporary outcomes

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Repertoire building: more cases to sort or classifyIndividual survivor testimonies and case studies accumulate into an understanding of the Stolen Generations as a systemic and widespread practice, not an isolated series of events. Multiple personal accounts build the picture that statistics and policy descriptions alone cannot.
