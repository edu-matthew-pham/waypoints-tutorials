Causes of contacts and conflicts

AC9HH7K12 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 7 History · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can describe the causes of significant contacts or conflicts involving the ancient society — including motivations such as resources, power, expansion, religion, and trade — and explain how these causes shaped what happened.

Success criteria

  • Describes at least two distinct causes of a significant contact or conflict.
  • Distinguishes between different types of causes (resource, power, trade, religion) rather than listing them all as equivalent.
  • Explains how at least one cause shaped the nature of the contact or conflict that followed.

Prior knowledge

Students can describe the key beliefs, values, and practices of the ancient society and explain how they shaped the society's organisation (AC9HH7K11). They have not yet examined how contacts and conflicts — within or with other societies — produced specific historical developments and changes.

Width

Minimum
Describe at least two causes of a significant contact or conflict involving the ancient society.
Typical
Describe the range of causes that drove contacts and conflicts involving the ancient society — including resource competition, desire for territorial expansion, power rivalries between rulers, religious motivations, and trade interests — and explain how these causes interacted to produce specific contacts or conflicts.
Wider
  • Examine the causes of a specific major conflict in depth — such as the causes of the Persian Wars, the Punic Wars, or the campaigns of Alexander the Great — distinguishing between immediate triggers and longer-term structural causes.
  • Investigate how contacts (trade, diplomacy, cultural exchange) created conditions that sometimes led to conflict — examining the relationship between peaceful contact and the conflicts that followed.
  • Explore how different ancient sources described the causes of the same conflict differently, revealing how each side understood its own motivations and those of its opponents.

Hinge

Students who reduce contacts and conflicts to single causes cannot produce the multi-dimensional effects analysis in N2 — the effects (conquest, trade expansion, treaties) are each caused by different combinations of the causes identified in N1, and single-cause thinking produces flat effects accounts.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents tend to identify single causes for conflicts ('they wanted more land') without understanding the multi-causal nature of ancient contacts and conflicts. The reasoning scaffold — multiple motivations interacting, short-term triggers vs long-term structural causes, how trade contacts created dependencies that led to conflict — needs to be made explicit to move beyond simplistic causal accounts.
