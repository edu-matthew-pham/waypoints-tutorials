Historical explanation — reasoning with evidence

AC9HH7S08 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Year 7 History · Historical skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can create a historical explanation that uses historical knowledge, concepts, and terms to explain a causation, continuity/change, or significance question, with evidence from sources to support the reasoning.

Lessons on this site

  • Explain with evidence

Success criteria

  • Addresses a 'why' or 'how' question — the text explains, not just describes.
  • Uses at least one historical concept explicitly in the explanation.
  • References evidence from sources to support reasoning — evidence is integrated into the argument, not listed separately.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Historical description — communicating knowledge with evidence.

Width

Minimum
Write an explanation that answers a 'why' or 'how' question about a historical topic, using at least one historical concept (causation, continuity/change, significance) and referring to at least two pieces of evidence.
Typical
Create a historical explanation of a causation, continuity/change, or significance question from the Y7 units — for example, explaining why First Nations Australians are considered the world's oldest continuing culture, or explaining the causes of the development of a specific ancient society — using historical knowledge accurately, applying relevant concepts, and referencing specific evidence from sources to support each step of the reasoning.
Wider
  • Develop a more extended explanation that accounts for multiple causes or factors, using evidence to support each factor and explaining how the factors relate to each other.
  • Examine the structural requirements of a historical explanation — claim, evidence, reasoning, counter-consideration — and apply this structure explicitly to improve an explanation.
  • Investigate how explanation differs from description — examining texts that look like explanations but are actually just descriptions, and identifying what makes a genuine explanatory argument.

Hinge

Students who produce extended description when asked for explanation cannot construct the position-with-evidence argument in N3 — the claim→evidence→reasoning structure must be reliable before it can serve as the architecture of a historical argument.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe move from description to explanation — from 'what happened' to 'why/how/why this matters' — is a cognitively demanding shift that requires explicit reasoning scaffolding. Students frequently write extended descriptions when asked for explanations, or use evidence as decoration rather than reasoning support. The scaffold of claim → evidence → reasoning → conclusion needs to be made explicit and practised before students can independently produce explanations that reason with evidence.
