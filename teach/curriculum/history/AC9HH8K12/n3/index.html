Economic and environmental features

AC9HH8K12 n3 · waypoint 3 of 3 · Year 8 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can describe the economic activities and environmental context of the chosen Asia-Pacific society and explain how these shaped the way of life of different groups.

Success criteria

  • Describes at least one economic activity of the chosen society and explains how it related to the environmental context
  • Identifies how economic features shaped the roles and experiences of at least one social group

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Religious and cultural practices.

Width

Minimum
A description of at least one economic activity and its relationship to the social or environmental context.
Typical
Economic and environmental features across agriculture, trade, resource use and technology. Examples: Khmer fishing, market trading and temple construction labour; the environmental management of feudal Japan — Tokugawa regulations on farming and tree harvesting; Polynesian economic practices — aquaculture in Nauru, agricultural practices in Hawaii, exchange systems in Rapa Nui; Rapa Nui men's fishing and household-clan exchange of goods.
Wider
  • For feudal Japan: examine the Tokugawa Shogunate's attempts to curb deforestation — heavy regulations on farmers, managing tree harvesting, lighter construction techniques — as an example of environmental management as governance
  • For Polynesian societies: investigate how environmental challenges were overcome on different islands to make settlement possible (aquaculture in Nauru, agriculture in Hawaii) to show how Polynesian societies adapted to diverse Pacific environments

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents need to see the link between economic capacity, environmental context and social organisation — e.g. how Khmer water management enabled the agricultural surplus that supported temple construction and a ruling class. Scaffold this causal chain explicitly.
