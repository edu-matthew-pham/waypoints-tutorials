Developing a range of historical questions

AC9HH9S01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 9 History · Historical skills

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can develop a range of historical questions — not just a single question — that require historical argument and go beyond factual recall.

Success criteria

  • Develops at least two historical questions that require historical argument rather than factual recall
  • Uses at least one historical concept (cause, effect, change, continuity, significance, or perspective) to frame a question

Prior knowledge

In Year 7 students developed historical questions (AC9HH7S01). In Year 8 students refined this by developing questions to inform inquiry (AC9HH8S01). Y9 extends this with the expectation that students develop a range of questions and modify them iteratively as the inquiry develops — adding the dimension of ongoing revision as evidence and understanding grows.

Width

Minimum
Develop at least two distinct historical questions about a topic being studied, each requiring historical argument rather than factual recall.
Typical
Develop a range of questions about aspects of the past that require historical argument; use historical concepts (cause, effect, change, continuity, significance, perspective) to frame questions at different levels; generate questions that could drive a sustained historical inquiry.
Wider
  • Develop inquiry questions for contrasting topic types (e.g. a political event and a social movement) and reflect explicitly on how the choice of question shapes the scope and direction of the inquiry
  • Investigate how historians have framed their inquiry questions about the same event in different historical periods — examining how the questions historians ask change over time — and use this to develop stronger questions about a topic being studied
  • Develop a research question collaboratively and document each revision with a rationale, examining how questioning is an iterative intellectual process

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents frequently conflate historical questions with factual questions ('What happened at Gallipoli?' vs 'Why did Gallipoli become central to Australian national identity?'). Explicit modelling of what makes a question require historical argument is necessary before students can generate these independently.
