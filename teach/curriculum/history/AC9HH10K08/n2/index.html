Changing attitudes to migrants — decade by decade

AC9HH10K08 n2 · waypoint 2 of 4 · Year 10 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can analyse how Australian public attitudes to migration changed across the 1950s–1980s, using sources to identify shifts in perspective and explain their causes.

Success criteria

  • Describes at least two shifts in public attitudes to migration across the post-war period
  • Explains the cause of each identified shift — not merely notes that it occurred
  • Uses at least two different source types to support the analysis

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: The White Australia policy — logic, operation, and dismantling.

Width

Minimum
Students describe at least two shifts in public attitudes to migration across the post-war period and explain the cause of each shift.
Typical
Students use primary and secondary sources to trace changing attitudes — initial ambivalence toward European 'New Australians' in the 1950s; growing acceptance as assimilation proceeded; rising concern as Asian migration increased in the 1970s; the multiculturalism debate of the 1980s (Galbally Report, Fraser government policy); examine how media representation, political rhetoric, and social contact shaped attitudes at each stage; explain the causes of attitudinal shifts in terms of changing migrant sources, policy changes, and international context.
Wider
  • Examine the 1988 Fitzgerald Report on immigration as a moment of policy contestation — analysing how it both responded to and shaped public attitudes
  • Investigate the sources used to trace attitudes — newspaper editorials, survey data, political speeches — and evaluate each source type's strengths and limitations for analysing public opinion

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepTracking attitudes decade by decade prevents overgeneralisation — 'Australians were racist' or 'Australians gradually became tolerant' — and builds the evidential basis for explaining causes rather than outcomes.
