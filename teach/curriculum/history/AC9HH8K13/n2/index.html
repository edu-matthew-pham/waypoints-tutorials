← AC9HH8K13 Turning points in Asia-Pacific

Causes and features

AC9HH8K13 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Year 8 History · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can describe the causes and key features of the chosen turning point, drawing on primary and secondary sources — including archaeological, environmental and written evidence.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two causes of the chosen turning point, distinguishing between immediate triggers and longer-term conditions
  • Describes key features using at least one source type (archaeological, written or environmental) as evidence

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: The development and its historical context.

Width

Minimum
At least two causes and a description of the key features of the turning point, with reference to at least one source.
Typical
Multiple causes (immediate and longer-term) with detailed description of features. Examples: the Khmer decline — Tai invasions, Jayavarman VII's building program, population growth, religious revolution, environmental challenges, water management failure; the role of the daimyo in ending the Ashikaga Shogunate; Polynesian expansion — construction techniques, wayfinding, moai construction techniques and transport; the decline of the moa and application of Rahui.
Wider
  • For the Khmer: examine the significance of Angkor Wat as a demonstration of imperial power — and how the same resources invested in its construction (labour, materials, administrative capacity) may have contributed to the empire's eventual fragility
  • For the moai: investigate the construction and transport of moai on Rapa Nui — techniques and theories about their meaning as representations of ancestors or chiefs — as a case study in how monuments can reveal the values and capacities of a society

Hinge

A well-evidenced causal account is prerequisite to analysing effects on continuity and change. Students who cannot describe what happened and why cannot explain what it changed.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Repertoire building: more cases to sort or classifyThe causes of major turning points in Asia-Pacific societies are often multi-factor and span environmental, political and cultural domains. Students need sufficient accumulated specific knowledge before they can make well-supported causal claims.
