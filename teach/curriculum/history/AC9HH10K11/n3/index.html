Key events — reconciliation and recognition, 1992–present

AC9HH10K11 n3 · waypoint 3 of 4 · Year 10 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain the significant events in the reconciliation and recognition phase of the civil rights movement, from the Redfern Speech to the Uluru Statement from the Heart.

Success criteria

  • Explains at least three events in the reconciliation phase and what each contributed
  • Explains the Uluru Statement from the Heart and its three demands
  • Explains why reconciliation is an ongoing process — distinguishing symbolic recognition from structural change

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Key events — legal and political changes, 1962–1992.

Width

Minimum
Students explain at least three events in the reconciliation phase and explain what each contributed.
Typical
Students trace: the Wik decision (1996), Keating's Redfern Speech (1992 — its significance as a statement of responsibility by a prime minister), the Bringing Them Home report (1997) and its recommendations, the first Sorry Day (1998), the Apology to the Stolen Generations (2008 — what it said and what it did not include), the Uluru Statement from the Heart (2017 — its three demands: Voice, Treaty, Truth); explain how Reconciliation is an ongoing process, not a single event; examine the gap between symbolic recognition and structural change.
Wider
  • Examine the political debate over the Apology — analysing the Howard government's refusal to apologise and the Rudd government's decision to apologise, and what the debate reveals about how political leaders understand historical responsibility
  • Investigate the Uluru Statement from the Heart in depth — examining its three pillars, the process that produced it, and the arguments for and against a constitutionally enshrined Voice to Parliament

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents need to understand reconciliation as a process with multiple phases and components — not as a single event or a completed project. Scaffolding the distinction between symbolic recognition (apology, Sorry Day) and structural change (land rights, closing the gap) is the key analytical move.
