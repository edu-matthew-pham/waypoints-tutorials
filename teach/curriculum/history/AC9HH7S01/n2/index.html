Developing questions using historical concepts

AC9HH7S01 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Year 7 History · Historical skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can develop historical inquiry questions using the key historical concepts — causation, continuity and change, perspective, and significance — as question frames.

Lessons on this site

  • Build a question from a big idea

Success criteria

  • Develops at least two historical inquiry questions using different concept frames.
  • Questions are genuinely investigable using historical evidence — not answerable by simple recall.
  • Correctly identifies which historical concept (causation, change, perspective, significance) each question draws on.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: What makes a question historical?.

Width

Minimum
Develop at least two historical inquiry questions using different historical concept frames (e.g. one causation question and one continuity/change question) about the same topic.
Typical
Develop a range of historical inquiry questions using the key historical concept frames — causation ('Why did...?', 'What caused...?'), continuity and change ('How did... change/continue?'), perspective ('How did... experience/view...?'), and significance ('Why is... historically significant?') — applied to topics from the K-strand content.
Wider
  • Examine how changing the concept frame changes the investigation — taking the same historical topic and developing four different questions (causation, change, perspective, significance) to explore how each would lead to a different inquiry.
  • Investigate how complex historical questions combine multiple concept frames — 'Why did the Roman Empire fall and what continued after its decline?' combines causation and continuity/change — and why complex questions sometimes produce richer inquiries.
  • Explore how the questions historians actually ask have changed over time — from 'great men' questions to social history questions to cultural history questions — as evidence that what counts as a historical question is itself historically situated.

Hinge

Students who revert to recall questions under independent practice cannot produce the appropriately scoped questions in N3 — N3 builds directly on reliable question formulation with concept frames, and fluency with those frames is the prerequisite.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Consolidation: more practise steps, short feedback loopsApplying historical concept frames to generate questions is a skill requiring practice and precision. Students commonly revert to recall or opinion questions when working independently, even after understanding the concept distinction. Regular practice — developing questions across different frames, on different topics, checking against criteria — is the investment that builds reliable question formulation.
