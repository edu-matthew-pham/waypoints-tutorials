What makes a question historical?

AC9HH7S01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 7 History · Historical skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can distinguish between a general question about the past and a historical inquiry question, and explain what makes a question suitable for historical inquiry — investigable using evidence, focused on historical significance or causation.

Lessons on this site

  • Can this be investigated?

Success criteria

  • Correctly distinguishes a historical inquiry question from a recall or opinion question.
  • Identifies at least two characteristics of a good historical inquiry question (investigable, focused on causation/change/significance/perspective).
  • Can sort a given set of questions into 'suitable for historical inquiry' and 'not suitable', with reasons.

Prior knowledge

Students can develop questions about objects, people, places, and events in the past and present (AC9HS6S01). They have not yet distinguished historical inquiry questions from general questions — specifically the disciplinary requirements of historical questions: they must be investigable using evidence, focused on historical significance, causation, continuity/change, or perspective, and they must be open enough to require analysis rather than simple recall.

Width

Minimum
Identify the difference between a recall question ('When did X happen?') and a historical inquiry question ('Why did X happen?' or 'How did X change?'), and explain why the inquiry question is more useful for historical investigation.
Typical
Describe the characteristics of a historical inquiry question — that it must be investigable using evidence, focused on historical significance, causation, continuity/change, or perspective, and open enough to require analysis. Distinguish these from recall questions, opinion questions, and questions that cannot be answered by historical evidence.
Wider
  • Examine how the framing of a question shapes what evidence is needed and what conclusions are possible — comparing how differently framed questions about the same event would lead to different investigations.
  • Investigate the concept of historical significance and how it can be operationalised as a question — what makes something worth investigating historically, and how students can ask questions that focus on significance.
  • Explore how historians generate inquiry questions — from curiosity, from gaps in existing knowledge, from new evidence — and examine published historical inquiries to identify the questions that drove them.

Hinge

Students who cannot distinguish historical inquiry questions from recall questions will produce recall questions when applying concept frames in N2 — the concept frames are applied correctly only if students understand what makes a question genuinely investigable.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe concept of a 'historical inquiry question' as distinct from a general question requires explicit reasoning about what history as a discipline is trying to do. Students need the distinction made explicit — recall vs analysis; answerable by fact vs requiring evidence-based argument — before they can apply it reliably. Without this scaffold, students produce recall or opinion questions rather than inquiry questions.
