Sources, evidence and interpretive uncertainty

AC9HH8K11 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 8 History · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain how the nature and availability of sources shapes the interpretations historians can make about the chosen empire or expansion — including how different source types produce different perspectives.

Success criteria

  • Explains how at least one source type shapes the interpretations historians can make about the chosen empire
  • Identifies at least one limitation of the available sources and explains how it affects historical understanding

Prior knowledge

Students understand how historians construct and evaluate interpretations (AC9HH8K06) and the events, individuals and society of the chosen empire (AC9HH8K07–K10). They have not yet applied interpretive analysis specifically to the contested historiography of medieval empires and expansion — including how oral traditions, primary sources, death tolls and post-colonial perspectives shape historical understanding.

Width

Minimum
An explanation of how at least one source type (e.g. oral tradition, primary chronicle, archaeological record) shapes interpretations of the chosen empire.
Typical
The relationship between source type and interpretive possibility across the chosen empire. Examples: Icelandic sagas as oral histories corroborated or contested by archaeological evidence (Erik the Red's Greenland settlements, L'Anse aux Meadows); how the written accounts of monks shaped Viking historiography; the validity of various death tolls linked to Mongol expansion and how these are evaluated against primary and secondary sources.
Wider
  • Analyse the extent to which historians' interpretations are corroborated with oral histories in Icelandic sagas — examining where archaeological and saga accounts align and where they diverge — as a case study in how oral and written sources produce different historical pictures
  • Examine why death toll estimates linked to Mongol expansion (e.g. 1,000,000 in Nishapur, 60 million in China) are contested, and how historians evaluate such claims using primary sources and demographic reasoning

Hinge

Students who do not understand how source availability and type shape interpretation cannot critically evaluate competing historical accounts. The source-interpretation link is prerequisite to all interpretive analysis.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents often treat historical sources as simply reliable or unreliable. Scaffold the more nuanced idea: different source types (oral, written, archaeological) produce different kinds of evidence that historians must weigh against each other. This is more complex than a simple accuracy check.
