Archaeological methods — stratigraphy and physical evidence

AC9HH7K08 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 7 History · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can describe stratigraphy as an archaeological method, explain how it is used to date discoveries and interpret change over time, and describe how physical evidence (artefacts, human remains) contributes to understanding early societies.

Success criteria

  • Explains stratigraphy correctly — deeper layers are older, layer contents reveal what was happening at that time.
  • Describes how specific evidence within a layer (human remains, weapons, charcoal) supports an interpretation of an event.
  • Does not treat stratigraphy as simply 'digging things up' — recognises it as a systematic method for reading change over time.

Prior knowledge

Students can explain the cultural obligations of First Nations Australians regarding heritage sites and ancestral remains, and describe how collaboration between Traditional Owners and researchers supports cultural preservation (AC9HH7K07). Students also bring prior understanding of evidence types from AC9HH7K01 (stratigraphy, radiocarbon dating, oral history as evidence for early human migration). They have not yet examined the full range of methods used to investigate early (ancient) societies, nor the specific roles of archaeology and heritage conservation in that investigation.

Width

Minimum
Describe stratigraphy as the analysis of earth layers to date and interpret archaeological finds, and explain how the depth of a find relates to its age.
Typical
Describe stratigraphy as an archaeological method — how layers of sediment are read to establish chronology — and explain how physical evidence within those layers (human remains, weapons, charcoal, pottery) is used to interpret events and change over time. Use a specific example, such as a charcoal layer containing human remains and weapons indicating the capture and destruction of a settlement (e.g. Troy).
Wider
  • Examine how a cross-sectional drawing of an archaeological excavation represents stratigraphic layers, and what specific evidence types found at different layers can reveal — connecting the visual representation to the interpretive practice.
  • Investigate the limitations of stratigraphy — including disturbance of layers by later activity, the challenge of establishing exact dates without additional dating methods, and what stratigraphy cannot tell us about why events happened.
  • Explore how stratigraphy was used at a specific ancient site to reconstruct its history — for example, the multiple occupation layers at Çatalhöyük or the stratigraphic evidence at Troy.

Hinge

Students who do not understand stratigraphic logic — deeper = older, layer contents reveal activity at that time — cannot interpret how physical evidence within layers supports interpretations. N2 and N3 assume students can read layered evidence before adding DNA and written sources alongside it.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe logical structure of stratigraphic reading — deeper = older, layer contents = evidence of activity at that time, transitions between layers = change events — is non-obvious to Year 7 students. Without explicit scaffolding of this reasoning chain, students cannot construct or interpret a cross-sectional diagram or understand why a charcoal layer with weapons indicates destruction rather than simply 'things were buried here'.
