Achievements and impact

AC9HH8K15 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Year 8 History · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain the key achievements of the chosen individual or group and describe their impact on the society and on later developments.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two achievements of the chosen individual or group
  • Explains the impact of at least one achievement on the society and on later developments

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Role and context.

Width

Minimum
At least two achievements with an explanation of their impact on the society.
Typical
Multiple achievements analysed for immediate and longer-term impact. Examples: Jayavarman VII's expansion of the Khmer Empire and construction program; the legacy of Tokugawa Ieyasu in establishing the Edo Shogunate and Japan's subsequent isolation; the achievements of Polynesian peoples in navigation, settlement of diverse island environments, development of distinctive cultures and resource management practices.
Wider
  • For the Khmer: examine the influence of Jayavarman VII's building program on both the empire's power and its later fragility — asking whether great achievement can also contain the seeds of decline
  • For Polynesia: describe the achievements of one Polynesian group — their navigation techniques, settlement strategies, cultural innovations — and evaluate what these achievements reveal about the capabilities and values of Pacific peoples

Hinge

Achievement analysis is the core demand of this descriptor. Students who narrate events rather than explaining achievements and impacts have not met the standard. Legacy analysis depends on solid achievement analysis here.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents often describe what a ruler did without connecting it to impact. Scaffold the distinction: an achievement is not an event but a consequential act. Each achievement needs an explanation of what it changed or enabled for the society.
