Key inquiry question

AC9HH8S01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 8 History · Historical skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can develop a key historical question that focuses an inquiry — framed to invite explanation rather than description, grounded in a specific historical context.

Success criteria

  • Develops a key historical question framed to invite explanation (using why or how) about a specific historical context
  • The question is appropriately scoped — neither too broad nor too narrow — to focus a historical inquiry

Prior knowledge

Students develop questions about objects, people, places and events in the past and present (AC9HH7S01). They can formulate basic historical questions. They have not yet developed multi-level question hierarchies (key question plus related sub-questions), questions about historical significance with measurable criteria, or the capacity to review and revise questions when inquiry produces unexpected findings.

Width

Minimum
One key historical question framed to invite explanation (why/how) about a specific historical context.
Typical
A key question that focuses the inquiry and is appropriately scoped — not too broad (e.g. 'What happened in the Middle Ages?') and not too narrow (e.g. 'What year did Rome fall?'). Examples: 'How did the decline of Rome's western empire change the organisation of European society?' or 'Why did Easter Island (Rapa Nui) society decline?'
Wider
  • Develop multiple candidate key questions for the same inquiry and evaluate which is best scoped — identifying what each question foregrounds and what it leaves out
  • Examine how different key questions about the same event (e.g. the Black Death) would direct inquiry in different directions — towards causes, effects, experiences or significance

Hinge

A poorly framed key question either produces description rather than explanation or is unanswerably broad. Students who cannot frame an inquiry-focused question cannot generate sub-questions, assess significance or review their inquiry purposefully.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents need explicit practice distinguishing inquiry questions (which invite explanation and require research) from factual questions (which have known answers) and opinion questions (which require no evidence). Scaffold the criteria: does this question require historical investigation to answer?
