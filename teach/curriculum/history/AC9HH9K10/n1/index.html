What makes an event a turning point — the concept

AC9HH9K10 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 9 History · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain what distinguishes a turning point from a significant event — that a turning point marks a shift in momentum or trajectory, not merely a notable occurrence — and apply this distinction to a historical example.

Success criteria

  • Distinguishes a turning point from a significant event with a definition and example
  • Applies the turning point concept to a WW1 event and justifies the classification

Prior knowledge

Students can describe the theatres where Australians fought, the conditions of trench warfare, and the development of military technology (AC9HH9K09). They have not yet examined the concept of a military turning point, evaluated the significance of strategic-level events (Russian Revolution, American entry, Armistice), or worked with opposing-perspective sources to construct historical understanding of specific battles.

Width

Minimum
Students define turning point as an event after which the course of events changed direction, distinguish it from a significant event (which may confirm rather than change direction), and apply the distinction to one WW1 example.
Typical
Students explain the distinguishing criteria for a turning point (shift in military momentum, strategic balance, or political will); apply the criteria to multiple events to decide which qualify; acknowledge that turning point judgements are contestable and depend on how you define 'trajectory change'; recognise that historians can disagree about which events were turning points.
Wider
  • Apply the turning point concept retroactively to the pre-war period — was there a point before which WW1 could have been prevented? Does the concept of turning point work for the outbreak as well as the conduct of the war?
  • Compare 'turning point' as a historical concept to 'watershed' and 'tipping point' — are these the same idea, or do they mark genuinely different things?

Hinge

The analytical concept of a turning point is the prerequisite for evaluating the significance of the Russian Revolution, American entry and the Armistice in N3. Students who treat 'turning point' and 'important event' as synonymous cannot engage in the evaluation task the descriptor demands — they will list events rather than assess significance.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly use 'turning point' and 'important' interchangeably. Making the distinction explicit — a turning point changes direction, not merely matters — is the analytical scaffold required for the significance evaluation in N3.
