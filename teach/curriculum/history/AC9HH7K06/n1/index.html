Social organisation — kinship, moieties, and totems

AC9HH7K06 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 7 History · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can describe the social organisation systems of early First Nations Australians — including moieties, totems, and kinship groups — and explain how these systems determine roles, relationships, and responsibilities within communities.

Success criteria

  • Describes at least two social organisation structures (e.g. moieties, kinship, totems) and explains what each determines about roles or relationships.
  • Explains that these systems are interconnected — not isolated categories but a coherent social architecture.
  • Does not treat kinship in purely Western biological terms — recognises it as a broader system of roles and obligations.

Prior knowledge

Students can describe the technological achievements of early First Nations Australians across different environments, including food systems and land management (AC9HH7K05). They have not yet examined the social organisational structures — moieties, totems, kinship — or the cultural practices and their transmission mechanisms as topics in their own right.

Width

Minimum
Describe at least one social organisation system (kinship, moieties, or totems) and explain what it determines about roles or relationships within a community.
Typical
Describe moieties, totems, and kinship groups as interconnected social organisation systems, explaining how they together determine roles and relationships — including marriage laws — and how they developed their particular significance in specific contexts such as Central Australia during increasing desertification.
Wider
  • Examine the practical functions of kinship systems in detail — how they organise obligations of care, resource sharing, conflict resolution, and marriage — connecting social structure to everyday life.
  • Explore how social organisation systems vary across different regions and language groups, reflecting the diversity of First Nations Australians' societies rather than a single uniform structure.
  • Investigate how social organisation systems underpin land tenure — the connection between kinship group, Country, and the rights and responsibilities that flow from that connection.

Hinge

Students without a schema for kinship, moieties, and totems cannot understand N3's cultural practices as expressions of these social structures — lore, rites of passage, and funerary customs will be described in isolation from the social organisation that produces and sustains them.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkKinship systems, moieties, and totems are conceptually unfamiliar to most Year 7 students who have no prior schema for social organisation systems different from Western family or class structures. The reasoning chain — how moieties divide a community, how totems mark group identity and obligations, how kinship determines who is 'family' in a system very different from biological parenthood — needs to be explicitly scaffolded before students can reason about continuity and change.
