Women's roles and rights — continuities and changes by 1914

AC9HH9K07 n2 · waypoint 2 of 2 · Year 9 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain the continuities and changes in women's roles in Australian society by 1914 — including advocacy for rights, suffrage, political representation and pacificism — and identify key figures who shaped these changes.

Success criteria

  • Describes at least two changes and two continuities in women's roles in Australian society by 1914
  • Identifies at least two key figures in women's advocacy and explains what each contributed

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Federal social legislation and its effects — Australia in comparative context.

Width

Minimum
Students describe at least two changes in women's roles by 1914 (e.g. achieving the vote, entering some professions, public advocacy) and identify at least two key figures.
Typical
Students trace the continuities — women's domestic and caring roles remained primary; women excluded from many professions, trades and public roles; limited property rights persisting in some states; and the changes — the federal vote (1902), state vote in most colonies earlier, women entering teaching and nursing as professions, women's public advocacy roles (temperance, suffrage, peace movements); examine specific figures (Elizabeth Macquarie, Caroline Chisholm, Catherine Helen Spence, Louisa Lawson, Muriel Matters, Vida Goldstein) and what their activism achieved and what it did not; explain how Australia's position compared to Britain and the USA on women's political rights by 1914.
Wider
  • Examine the pacificism of figures like Vida Goldstein — connecting women's rights advocacy to anti-militarism, and explaining why this connection was made by some women at this time
  • Investigate the experience of working-class women — whose lives were shaped more by labour conditions than by suffrage campaigns — and examine what is missing from accounts focused on suffragist leaders

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents commonly equate women's rights history with suffrage — the vote as the single story — without recognising the continuities in women's domestic and economic subordination, or the range of advocacy that extended beyond the vote.
