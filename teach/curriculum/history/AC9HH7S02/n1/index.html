Primary and secondary sources — the disciplinary distinction

AC9HH7S02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 7 History · Historical skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain the distinction between primary and secondary sources as used in historical inquiry — primary sources are from the time of the event being investigated; secondary sources are later interpretations — and correctly classify a given set of sources.

Lessons on this site

  • Primary or secondary? It is about when, not how old

Success criteria

  • States the correct disciplinary definition of primary and secondary sources (primary = from the time of the event).
  • Correctly classifies at least four sources, including at least one non-obvious case.
  • Does not classify based on age alone — recognises that a modern document about a modern event is still a primary source.

Prior knowledge

Students can locate, collect, and organise information from primary and secondary sources (AC9HS6S02). They have not yet applied the primary/secondary distinction specifically to historical inquiry — including the disciplinary definition of primary sources as sources from the time of the event being investigated, and the practical skills of locating sources in digital repositories and research databases.

Width

Minimum
Define primary and secondary sources using the disciplinary definition, and correctly classify at least four given sources (a mix of obvious and less obvious examples).
Typical
Explain the distinction between primary and secondary sources — primary sources are from the time of the event (scrolls, coins, artefacts, artwork, inscriptions, human remains); secondary sources are later interpretations of the past — and correctly classify a range of source types including edge cases (e.g. a modern translation of an ancient text, a photograph of an artefact).
Wider
  • Examine edge cases that challenge the primary/secondary distinction — a modern translation of an ancient text (primary content, secondary medium?), a later copy of a lost original, a documentary that uses primary source footage — and discuss how historians navigate these ambiguities.
  • Investigate how the primary/secondary distinction differs across disciplines — in science, a 'primary source' means something different than in history — and why the historical definition is tied to the concept of 'from the time of the event'.
  • Explore how the same object can function as both a primary source (evidence about the time it was made) and a secondary source (evidence about how later people interpreted the past) depending on the inquiry question.

Hinge

Students who misclassify sources based on age rather than temporal relationship to the event being studied will produce systematically wrong source organisation in N3 and import the error into S03–S04 source analysis work — all downstream source skills depend on reliable primary/secondary classification.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Consolidation: more practise steps, short feedback loopsThe primary/secondary distinction is a precision skill with common errors — students frequently classify based on medium (old = primary, modern = secondary) rather than temporal relationship to the event being studied. Regular practice classifying a variety of sources, including deliberate edge cases, builds the precision needed before source analysis work in S03–S04 can proceed reliably.
