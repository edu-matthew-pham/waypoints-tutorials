Comparing and explaining perspectives

AC9HH10S06 n1 · waypoint 1 of 1 · Year 10 History · Historical skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can compare the perspectives of different groups or individuals in sources and explain how their different values, beliefs, locations or experiences influenced those perspectives.

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Compare the perspectives of at least two groups or individuals on a historical event or issue
  • Explain how at least one influencing factor (values, beliefs, location, experience) shaped each perspective
  • Explain why some perspectives from the period studied may not have been recorded in the historical record

Prior knowledge

Students can compare perspectives in sources and explain how they are influenced by significant events, ideas, locations, beliefs and values at Year 9 level.

Width

Minimum
Compare the perspectives of at least two groups or individuals on the same historical event and explain how at least one influencing factor shaped each perspective.
Typical
Compare interpretations and evaluate differences in relation to the ideas, locations, beliefs and values that have influenced them; explain the links between continuities in the treatment of First Nations Australians, women and migrants; analyse the views of men and women at different times regarding gender equality, explaining how these views reflect changing values and attitudes; explain why some perspectives in the past may not have been recorded.
Wider
  • Apply perspective comparison to a topic where the dominant historical record reflects only one perspective — such as colonial accounts of frontier conflict — practising how to reconstruct missing perspectives from indirect evidence
  • Examine the perspectives of First Nations Australians on a major event studied in the course (e.g. the 1967 Referendum, the Apology) alongside non-Indigenous perspectives, evaluating how the same event carries fundamentally different significance depending on whose standpoint is centred

Hinge

Students who cannot explain why perspectives differ — only that they differ — cannot engage with S07's requirement to analyse contested historical interpretations. The move from 'these perspectives differ' to 'these perspectives differ because of X influencing factor' is the analytical prerequisite for interpretive analysis.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkScaffold the explanation of perspective difference: first identify the difference, then identify the influencing factor, then explain the causal link between factor and perspective. Without this scaffolding students tend to describe perspectives without explaining them.
Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepExtend perspective comparison to include First Nations perspectives on events studied in the K-strand — the 1938 Day of Mourning, the 1967 Referendum, the Apology — alongside non-Indigenous perspectives. This breadth of standpoint is both elaboration-supported and directly relevant to the course's central themes.
