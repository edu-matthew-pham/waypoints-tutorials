Structural elements for argumentative effect

AC9HH8S08 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Year 8 History · Historical skills

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can use structural elements — an introduction that outlines an argument and a conclusion that summarises and restates it — to communicate historical explanations or analysis with argumentative effect.

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Writes an introduction that outlines a specific historical argument (not just a topic) with reference to the evidence that will support it
  • Writes a conclusion that summarises and restates the argument in light of the evidence presented

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Historical terms and concepts in context.

Width

Minimum
A piece of historical writing with an identifiable introduction that outlines an argument and a conclusion that summarises and restates it.
Typical
Historical writing with deliberate structural design: an introduction that states the argument (not just the topic) and signals the reasoning to follow; body paragraphs that develop the argument with evidence; a conclusion that restates the argument in light of the evidence presented. The structure as a rhetorical tool, not just an organisational container.
Wider
  • Compare two introductions to the same historical question — one that states a topic and one that states an argument — and explain why the second is more effective for historical writing
  • Practice writing conclusions that go beyond summary — adding a statement of significance or connecting the historical argument to a broader question about the period

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents often confuse introducing a topic with introducing an argument. Scaffold the distinction: an argument-introduction states a claim that will be supported by evidence; a topic-introduction merely announces what will be discussed. The structure signals the reasoning, not just the content.
