Usefulness and reliability are question-relative

AC9HH9S04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 9 History · Historical skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain that the reliability and usefulness of a source depends on the questions asked of it — a source that is unreliable for one question may be highly useful for another.

Success criteria

  • Explains that a source's usefulness and reliability depend on the inquiry question, not on fixed criteria
  • Identifies what at least one source is useful for and what it is not useful for, with reference to specific inquiry questions

Prior knowledge

In Year 7 students identified and described accuracy and usefulness of sources (AC9HH7S04). In Year 8 they continued this (AC9HH8S04). Y9 introduces the key principle that usefulness and reliability depend on the questions being asked — the same source may be useful for one inquiry and unreliable for another. This represents a shift from applying fixed criteria to question-relative judgement.

Width

Minimum
Explain how the usefulness of one source depends on the inquiry question being asked, giving an example of what the source is useful for and what it is not.
Typical
Identify that the reliability and usefulness of a source depends on the questions asked of it; apply this principle to both primary and secondary sources — explaining what each source is evidence of, not just whether it is 'biased'; explain how a propaganda poster that is an unreliable account of events is a highly useful source for understanding what the government wanted citizens to believe.
Wider
  • Apply question-relative usefulness judgements to contrasting source types (e.g. an official government report and a private diary about the same event) — practising the shift from 'is this source useful?' to 'useful for what, and for whom?'
  • Investigate how historians themselves have debated the usefulness of specific contested sources (e.g. colonial accounts, propaganda) — examining how professional historians apply question-relative judgements to the same sources
  • Apply usefulness and reliability analysis to a digital source and assess how the medium of delivery (digitised image vs original document) affects source use

Hinge

The conceptual shift from 'this source is biased/not biased' to 'this source is useful for X but not for Y' is the defining intellectual move of this standard. Students who remain in the binary (biased/reliable) framing cannot perform the more sophisticated analysis the descriptor requires.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe question-relative usefulness principle is counter-intuitive for many students — they expect biased sources to be dismissed, not to be useful for different questions. Explicit modelling with a propaganda example (unreliable as history, highly useful as evidence of government intent) is the most effective way to establish this distinction.
