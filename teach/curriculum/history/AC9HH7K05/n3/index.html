Land and water management technologies

AC9HH7K05 n3 · waypoint 3 of 3 · Year 7 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can describe specific land and water management technologies developed by early First Nations Australians — including cultural burning, aquaculture systems, weirs, and water management infrastructure — and explain how these contributed to daily life and sustainable resource management.

Success criteria

  • Describes at least two specific land or water management technologies with reference to named communities or sites.
  • Explains how each technology contributed to sustainable resource management — not just what it was, but what problem it solved.
  • Does not treat these technologies as primitive — uses language that reflects the sophistication and scale of the engineering involved.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Food production and procurement systems.

Width

Minimum
Describe at least one land or water management technology (e.g. cultural burning, eel traps, stone fish traps, weirs) and explain its purpose and contribution to resource management.
Typical
Describe multiple land and water management technologies across different environments — cultural burning practices for land management, aquaculture systems including eel traps (Budj Bim), mollusc harvesting (Kombumerri People), stone fish traps (Brewarrina), weirs and irrigation — and explain how each technology demonstrates deliberate, sustained management of the landscape.
Wider
  • Examine the Budj Bim aquaculture system in detail — its age (at least 6,600 years, older than the Egyptian pyramids), its engineering, how it was identified as a world heritage site, and what it reveals about the scale and sophistication of First Nations water management.
  • Investigate cultural burning practices as a landscape management technology — examining how controlled burns were used to manage vegetation, promote food sources, and maintain ecological diversity, and how this knowledge is being integrated into contemporary land management.
  • Explore the Brewarrina fish traps (Baiame's Ngunnhu) as among the world's oldest human-made structures — examining the archaeological evidence for their age, how they functioned, and what they reveal about long-term community infrastructure.

Response modes

Students must connect technology, food systems, and land management as a coherent whole.
