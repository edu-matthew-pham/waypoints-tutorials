Migration routes across the continent

AC9HH7K02 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Year 7 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can describe likely migration routes taken by early First Nations Australians across the continent, and identify the types of evidence — ancient sites, geographical features, genetic research — used to map those routes.

Success criteria

  • Describes at least one type of evidence used to map migration routes across the continent.
  • Explains that multiple migration routes are proposed, reflecting the limits of available evidence rather than historical confusion.
  • Identifies at least one specific ancient site or geographical feature in the context of dispersal across the continent.

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Seafaring and arrival — causes.

Width

Minimum
Describe at least one proposed migration route across the continent and identify one type of evidence used to support it (e.g. ancient habitation sites, DNA research).
Typical
Describe how researchers use ancient habitation sites, geographical features (waterways, terrain), and genetic research (including DNA from hair samples) to model how early First Nations Australians dispersed across the continent, and explain why multiple routes are proposed rather than a single path.
Wider
  • Examine specific ancient sites — such as Lake Mungo (Willandra Lakes), Madjedbebe, and others — and what their location and dating reveal about the chronology and routes of dispersal.
  • Investigate how DNA research has been used to track genetic connections of First Nations Australians to specific parts of Australia, and what this reveals about population dispersal.
  • Explore how ancient landscape features — including now-submerged coastlines, ancient river systems, and vegetation corridors — shaped where people could travel and settle.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents commonly expect a single, definitive migration route — a misconception that multiple evidence types and the probabilistic nature of ancient site distribution can resolve. Examining different site locations, different evidence methods, and why researchers propose multiple dispersal routes rather than one helps students see that historical interpretation of deep-time events is inherently multi-stranded.
