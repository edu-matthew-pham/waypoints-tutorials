← AC9HH10K01 Causes of the Second World War

Rise of fascism and militarism as structural responses

AC9HH10K01 n2 · waypoint 2 of 5 · Year 10 History · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain how specific inter-war conditions — economic collapse, institutional failure, ideological grievance — produced the rise of fascist and militarist regimes, and can identify multiple cases demonstrating that this was a systemic phenomenon, not uniquely German.

Success criteria

  • Explains at least two inter-war conditions that contributed to the rise of the Nazi Party, connecting each condition to a specific political or social mechanism
  • Identifies one other fascist or militarist regime and explains its rise in terms of inter-war conditions — demonstrating that this was not uniquely German
  • Explains how Nazi ideology exploited existing resentments as a political strategy, not merely describes its content

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Inter-war settlement and its instabilities.

Width

Minimum
Students explain two inter-war conditions that contributed to the rise of the Nazi Party in Germany; identify one other fascist or militarist regime (Italy or Japan) and explain its rise in terms of similar conditions.
Typical
Students explain how the Great Depression, the failure of Weimar democracy, national humiliation from Versailles, and the appeal of Nazi ideology combined to produce Hitler's rise from 1933; explain the parallel rise of Mussolini's Italy and Imperial Japan as cases of fascism and militarism filling the collective security vacuum; explain how Nazi ideology exploited existing resentments as a political strategy — not merely describe its content.
Wider
  • Examine the ideological contest of the inter-war period — comparing fascism, communism, and liberal democracy as competing responses to post-WWI instability — and analyse why fascism proved more electorally viable than communism in Germany
  • Investigate the Spanish Civil War (1936–39) as a rehearsal for WW2 — examining how it demonstrated fascist military capability, tested international responses, and anticipated the ideological fault lines of the coming war
  • Explore the role of propaganda in the consolidation of fascist regimes — examining how Goebbels' media apparatus and Imperial Japan's nationalist education system manufactured consent for expansionist policies

Hinge

All subsequent nodes require a structural understanding of what fascist and militarist regimes were and wanted. Students who explain fascism only as 'Hitler was bad' or 'people were angry after WW1' cannot explain why appeasement seemed rational (N3), why Australia's entry had the political character it did (N4), or why the war's significance extends beyond its events (N5). The Hitler-as-sole-cause misconception, if unresolved here, produces systematically inadequate causal arguments throughout the unit.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkIntroduce ideology as a historical concept explicitly — students need to move from 'events and individuals' to 'ideas and their political effects.' The key scaffold is explaining how Nazi ideology exploited specific existing conditions (economic fear, national humiliation, anti-Semitism) as a deliberate political strategy, not simply describing its content.
Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepUsing Italy and Japan as parallel cases alongside Germany is the concrete corrective to Hitler-as-sole-cause. Students who encounter fascism and militarism as a multi-country phenomenon cannot maintain the 'uniquely German pathology' framing.
