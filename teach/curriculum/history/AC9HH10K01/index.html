Causes of the Second World War

AC9HH10K01 · Year 10 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Descriptor

the causes, outbreak and course of the Second World War and the significance of Australian involvement

Prior knowledge

Students have explained the causes of World War One using the MAIN framework (Militarism, Alliance systems, Imperialism, Nationalism) and can distinguish long-term structural causes from short-term triggers (AC9HH9K08). They have examined the effects of WW1 on Australian society including conscription debates and Australia's relationship with Britain (AC9HH9K11), and have encountered the Treaty of Versailles as a post-war settlement — though depth of treatment of its specific terms is variable (AC9HH9K12 elaboration). They have not yet examined the inter-war period (1919–1939) as a causal field, the rise of fascism as a structural phenomenon, the diplomatic sequence leading to WW2, or Australia's political decision-making around entry into WW2.

By the end

Students explain the causes and outbreak of the Second World War across European and Asia-Pacific contexts, analyse the course of the war, and evaluate the significance of Australian involvement.

ACARA elaborations

Examples from ACARA that illustrate this standard. They are examples, not a checklist, and belong to the standard as a whole.

  • investigating significant events and ideas during the inter-war years between the First World War and the Second World War, including the Treaty of Versailles (territorial concessions required by Germany and the imposition of war reparations), the Russian Revolution and the Great Depression, rise of Adolf Hitler and the Nazi Party in Germany from 1933
  • analysing the major events leading up to the outbreak of the Second World War, such as the Japanese invasion of Manchuria, the Spanish Civil War, Italo–Abyssinian Wars, the annexation of Austria, the Munich Agreement and the annexation of the Sudetenland, the invasion of Poland in 1939, the occupation of China and the attack on Pearl Harbor in 1941
  • investigating the causes of the effects of the Treaty of Versailles on Germany, the structural weaknesses of the League of Nations, the rise of fascist and militarist regimes, and the failure of appeasement policies
  • examining the reasons for Australia’s involvement in the Second World War, and the responses to the conflicts of prime minister Robert Menzies, “Australia is also at war” and prime minister John Curtin, “Australia looks to America”

Waypoints

  • 1. Inter-war settlement and its instabilitiesHingeStudents can explain how the post-WW1 settlement — the Treaty of Versailles and the League of Nations — created structural conditions of resentment, economic punishment, and failed collective security that formed the necessary preconditions for WW2.
  • 2. Rise of fascism and militarism as structural responsesHingeStudents can explain how specific inter-war conditions — economic collapse, institutional failure, ideological grievance — produced the rise of fascist and militarist regimes, and can identify multiple cases demonstrating that this was a systemic phenomenon, not uniquely German.
  • 3. The diplomatic sequence and the failure of appeasementStudents can analyse the major pre-war events of 1933–1939 as a causal chain in which each failure to respond to aggression made the next act of aggression more likely, and can explain why appeasement was a rational policy that proved catastrophically wrong.
  • 4. Australia's entry — political decision, not automatic reflexHingeStudents can explain Australia's entry into WW2 as a political decision reflecting Australia's identity as a British dominion, and can explain the significance of the Menzies/Curtin contrast as marking a genuine shift in Australia's strategic identity.
  • 5. Significance of Australian involvement — evaluative integrationStudents can evaluate the significance of Australia's involvement in WW2 across multiple dimensions, constructing a historical argument rather than listing outcomes.
