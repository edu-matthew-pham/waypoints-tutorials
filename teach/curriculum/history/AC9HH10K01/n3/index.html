← AC9HH10K01 Causes of the Second World War

The diplomatic sequence and the failure of appeasement

AC9HH10K01 n3 · waypoint 3 of 5 · Year 10 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can analyse the major pre-war events of 1933–1939 as a causal chain in which each failure to respond to aggression made the next act of aggression more likely, and can explain why appeasement was a rational policy that proved catastrophically wrong.

Success criteria

  • Sequences at least three major pre-war events and explains how each contributed to the outbreak of war — not merely lists them
  • Explains the appeasement policy: what it was, why Western powers adopted it, and what assumptions it made about Hitler's intentions
  • Explains why the invasion of Poland in 1939 was the specific trigger for Britain's — and therefore Australia's — declaration of war

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Rise of fascism and militarism as structural responses.

Width

Minimum
Students identify three events in the 1933–1939 sequence and explain how each represented an escalation that earlier non-response enabled; explain the appeasement policy and why it failed, using Munich as evidence.
Typical
Students trace the escalating sequence across both theatres: Japanese invasion of Manchuria, Italo-Abyssinian Wars, remilitarisation of the Rhineland, Spanish Civil War, annexation of Austria, Munich Agreement and Sudetenland, invasion of Poland (1939), occupation of China; explain appeasement as a rational policy reflecting genuine strategic calculations — fear of another total war, military unpreparedness, trust in Hitler's stated limited aims — and analyse why those assumptions proved wrong; explain why Poland's invasion was the specific trigger for Britain's declaration of war.
Wider
  • Examine the Munich Agreement in depth as a case study in diplomatic failure — analysing Chamberlain's reasoning, Hitler's response, and the historiographical debate over whether appeasement was inevitable or a strategic miscalculation
  • Compare the timeline of aggression in the European and Asia-Pacific theatres — examining whether the two paths to war were connected or largely independent until 1941
  • Investigate the role of domestic public opinion and political constraints in Britain and France in limiting governments' responses to fascist aggression — examining how democratic accountability complicated strategic decision-making

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Repertoire building: more cases to sort or classifyStudents need a working repertoire of key pre-war events across both theatres before causal analysis is meaningful. A chronological mapping task — annotating each event with its theatrical context, the aggressor, and the international response — builds the knowledge base that supports the appeasement analysis.
Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkAppeasement as a rational policy that failed requires explicit scaffolding — students default to condemning it as cowardice or weakness. The reasoning scaffold is: what did Chamberlain know? what did he assume? where did his assumptions break down? This is the entry point to genuine historical judgement rather than hindsight condemnation.
