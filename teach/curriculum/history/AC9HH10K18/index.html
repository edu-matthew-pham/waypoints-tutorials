Continuity and change in Australian life

AC9HH10K18 · Year 10 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Descriptor

continuities and changes in perspectives, responses, beliefs and values that have influenced the Australian way of life

Prior knowledge

Students have examined Cold War domestic effects, the long boom, and cultural transformation (K16); the Vietnam War's domestic effects and multiculturalism (K17); and First Nations and women's rights movements (K09–K12). They understand continuity-and-change as an analytical concept. They have not yet examined how Australians began to question traditional national values in the 1960s–70s as a connected pattern, or how specific governments shaped Australia's engagement with the Asia-Pacific.

By the end

Students explain continuities and changes in perspectives, beliefs, and values that have influenced the Australian way of life — including challenges to traditional notions of Australian identity in the 1960s–70s, changing religious practice, and shifting approaches to Asia-Pacific engagement.

ACARA elaborations

Examples from ACARA that illustrate this standard. They are examples, not a checklist, and belong to the standard as a whole.

  • explaining why environmental movements gained increasing public voice and identifying different perspectives
  • investigating how Australians in the 1960s and 1970s, influenced by the events of those decades, began to question the traditional notions of egalitarianism, a fair go, classlessness and democracy; for example, conscription and the Vietnam War, the Women’s Liberation Movement and the dismissal of the Whitlam government
  • comparing and contrasting the policies relating to engagement with the Asia-Pacific region of the governments led by prime minister Paul Keating (1991–1996) and prime minister John Howard (1996–2007)
  • examining the nature of religion in Australia; for example, the changing attitudes to religious practice, the increase of non-Christian religions and non-traditional Christian churches

Waypoints

  • 1. Traditional Australian values and their post-war characterHingeStudents can describe the traditional Australian values of egalitarianism, classlessness, democracy, and a 'fair go' as they were understood in the post-war period, and explain why these values seemed stable in the 1950s.
  • 2. Challenges to traditional values — Vietnam, feminism, and the Whitlam dismissalStudents can explain how Australians in the 1960s and 1970s began to question traditional notions, examining conscription and the Vietnam War, the Women's Liberation Movement, and the Whitlam dismissal as key challenges.
  • 3. Changing religious landscapeStudents can examine the changing nature of religion in Australia — including the declining practice of mainstream Christianity, the growth of non-Christian religions, and the emergence of non-traditional churches.
  • 4. Asia-Pacific engagement — Keating versus HowardStudents can compare the Asia-Pacific engagement policies of the Keating and Howard governments, evaluating how each reflected different approaches to Australian identity and values.
