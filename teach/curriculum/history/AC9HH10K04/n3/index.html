Women's wartime roles — change versus continuity

AC9HH10K04 n3 · waypoint 3 of 5 · Year 10 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can evaluate the extent to which women's wartime roles represented genuine lasting change versus temporary wartime disruption, using continuity-and-change analysis.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least one wartime change to women's roles that persisted after the war and at least one that was reversed
  • Explains the difference in terms of structural versus temporary wartime factors — not merely describes the outcomes
  • Connects the WW2 pattern to the WW1 precedent — assessing whether WW2 represented acceleration or repetition

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Women's wartime roles — what changed.

Width

Minimum
Students identify at least one change that persisted after the war and at least one that was reversed, and explain the difference in terms of structural versus wartime factors.
Typical
Students compare women's roles during the war (N2) with their roles before and after; evaluate which changes persisted and which were reversed when men returned; connect to WW1 precedents (Y9 prior knowledge) to assess whether WW2 represented an acceleration or a repetition of earlier patterns; analyse the structural forces that reversed wartime gains — government policy, social pressure, economic structures; connect forward to K12's women's movement content as the longer-term response to this pattern.
Wider
  • Compare the extent of role change for Australian women with women in Britain or the USA — examining whether the scale and durability of change differed across countries and why
  • Examine the post-war return to domesticity as a deliberate policy outcome — analysing how government incentives, social messaging, and industrial restructuring combined to reverse wartime gains

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly describe wartime changes as straightforwardly progressive — the war liberated women. The continuity dimension (most gains reversed post-war) requires explicit scaffolding. The framework is: what changed during the war? which changes persisted? which were reversed? what does the pattern tell us about the structural limits of wartime change?
