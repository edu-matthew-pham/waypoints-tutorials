Values and attitudes in individual sources

AC9HH8S06 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 8 History · Historical skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify the values and attitudes revealed by an individual source and describe what these suggest about the society or individual that produced it.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two values or attitudes revealed by a source and explains what they suggest about the source's producer
  • Distinguishes between what a source explicitly states and what its values or assumptions reveal implicitly

Prior knowledge

Students identify perspectives, attitudes and values of the past in sources (AC9HH7S06). They can identify the perspective in a source. They have not yet used additional sources to show how an individual account is broadly representative of or contrasts with wider societal values, or analysed how historians' own contexts shape their interpretations.

Width

Minimum
Identification of at least two values or attitudes revealed by a source, with an explanation of what these suggest about the source's producer.
Typical
Values and attitudes identified across multiple source types: a medieval chronicle's values about religious authority; bushido as a value system revealed through samurai texts; Zhou Daguan's Chinese perspective on Khmer society; the monk-written accounts of Viking raids revealing Christian values about violence and the sacred. Understanding that a source's values are not just stated but embedded in what is described, emphasised or omitted.
Wider
  • Examine Matteo Villani's diary, Boccaccio's Decameron or Fordun's Chronicle of the Scottish Nation as sources that reveal how individuals understood and valued the social order disrupted by the Black Death
  • Analyse how Viking warrior culture — particularly the significance of honour — is revealed through both the content of Viking sagas and the decisions made by saga characters, going beyond explicit statements of value

Hinge

Identifying values and attitudes in sources is prerequisite to the corroboration and representativeness analysis in Node 2. Students who can only describe what a source says, rather than what it reveals about values and attitudes, cannot assess how representative it is.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents often describe the content of a source rather than the values it reveals. Scaffold the distinction: what does the author include, emphasise or omit — and what does that tell us about what they valued or assumed?
