Australia's involvement — HV Evatt and Jessie Street

AC9HH10K15 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Year 10 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain Australia's specific involvement in the development of the UDHR, including the roles of HV Evatt and Jessie Street.

Success criteria

  • Explains HV Evatt's specific role in the UDHR's adoption
  • Explains Jessie Street's contribution to the human rights language in the UN charter
  • Evaluates why Australia's involvement was substantive rather than merely symbolic

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Origins — the post-war human rights moment.

Width

Minimum
Students explain Australia's role in developing the UDHR, identifying HV Evatt and Jessie Street and describing their specific contributions.
Typical
Students examine HV Evatt's role as President of the UN General Assembly (1948–49) and his chairmanship of the UN committee overseeing the UDHR's final adoption; examine Jessie Street's contribution to the human rights language in the UN charter — particularly the inclusion of women's rights and the non-discrimination principle; explain why Australia's involvement was more than symbolic — Evatt and Street made substantive contributions to the document's content; connect to K05's content on Australia's post-war international aspirations.
Wider
  • Examine the drafting process in depth — analysing how competing national interests (Soviet bloc, colonial powers, the USA) shaped which rights were included and how they were framed
  • Investigate Jessie Street's broader activism — examining how her involvement in the UDHR connected to her domestic campaigns for women's rights and First Nations rights in Australia

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents need to understand Australia's involvement as substantive — not merely presence at a document signing. Making Evatt's chairing role and Street's specific textual contributions explicit builds the case for Australia's genuine contribution rather than peripheral participation.
