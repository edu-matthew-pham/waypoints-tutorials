The Anzac legend — what it claims and what values it asserts

AC9HH9K12 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 9 History · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can describe the Anzac legend as a set of specific values and ideals — mateship, courage, larrikinism, egalitarianism, endurance — and explain where these ideals came from and what they claim about Australian national character.

Success criteria

  • Describes the core ideals of the Anzac legend with at least three examples
  • Explains where the legend came from and what it claims about Australian national character

Prior knowledge

Students can identify that historical interpretations differ and explain why (AC9HH8K06, AC9HH8K11). They have studied the Gallipoli campaign, the Western Front experience, and the effects of the war on Australian society (AC9HH9K09, AC9HH9K11). They have not yet examined what the 'Anzac legend' claims as a set of values and ideals, how commemoration functions as a cultural practice, or how historians have debated and contested the legend's nature and significance over time.

Width

Minimum
Students identify the core ideals associated with the Anzac legend (mateship, courage, endurance under adversity, egalitarian disregard for rank, larrikinism), describe where each ideal came from — the Gallipoli and Western Front experience as interpreted by C.E.W. Bean and others — and explain what the legend claims about what it means to be Australian.
Typical
Students describe the Anzac legend's specific components; trace the legend's origins in C.E.W. Bean's reporting and official histories; explain how the legend was constructed from selective elements of the actual experience (not all soldiers displayed these virtues; the legend emphasises some experiences and omits others); explain what purposes the legend served — national identity, meaning-making for grief, connection to a founding national story; examine how the legend positions Gallipoli as the 'birth' of the Australian nation.
Wider
  • Examine specific texts that constructed the legend — Bean's dispatches, early ANZAC Day speeches, war poetry and art — and analyse the choices made in how soldiers and their experience were represented
  • Investigate who was excluded from the original legend — First Nations soldiers, women, non-Anglo Australians — and what that exclusion reveals about whose story the legend was originally telling

Hinge

The Anzac legend's content must be understood before its historiographical debate (N3) can proceed. Students who cannot describe what the legend actually claims cannot meaningfully compare historians' views about its nature and significance — they will discuss the debate without knowing what is being debated.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly treat the Anzac legend as self-evidently true — the actual soldiers were exactly as described — rather than as a specific historical construction that emphasised some elements and omitted others. Making the legend's constructedness explicit is the scaffold for the interpretive debate that follows.
Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepExamining how the legend was constructed from specific sources — Bean's journalism, specific speeches, specific images — and what each source emphasised, builds a more complex understanding than treating the legend as a single, unified thing.
