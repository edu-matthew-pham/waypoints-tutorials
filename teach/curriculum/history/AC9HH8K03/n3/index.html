Effects: what changed and what continued

AC9HH8K03 n3 · waypoint 3 of 4 · Year 8 History · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain the effects of the chosen turning point — describing what changed for different groups and what remained the same — and use the concepts of continuity and change to analyse its historical significance.

Success criteria

  • Explains at least one change and one continuity resulting from the turning point, with reference to specific evidence
  • Identifies how the turning point affected different social groups differently

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Causes and features of the turning point.

Width

Minimum
At least two effects of the turning point (one change and one continuity) with an explanation of how they affected at least one social group.
Typical
Multiple effects across social, economic, religious and political domains; continuities explained by showing the widespread nature of similarities despite the event's significance; different effects for different groups (e.g. nobility, Church, peasants, women).
Wider
  • For the Black Death: analyse the effects of labour shortages, peasant uprisings, weakening of feudal structures and challenges to religious power as a multi-domain chain of consequence
  • For the Enlightenment: examine how rulers in different European nations responded differently to new and dissenting ideas — comparing France, England, Spain and Russia — to show how the same turning point produced different continuities and changes
  • For the French Ancien Régime: investigate how increasing centralisation of administrative power in the monarchy represented both continuity and the conditions for revolutionary change

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents tend to focus only on change and overlook continuity. Using multiple domains and perspectives — including groups for whom little changed — builds a more complete and accurate analysis.
