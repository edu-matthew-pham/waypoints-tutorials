What is a historical interpretation?

AC9HH7S07 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 7 History · Historical skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain what a historical interpretation is — that it is a constructed argument about the past based on evidence, not a simple statement of fact — and distinguish between a historical fact and a historical interpretation.

Success criteria

  • Correctly defines historical interpretation as a constructed argument about the past, not a statement of fact.
  • Distinguishes between a historical fact and a historical interpretation, with an example of each.
  • Explains that interpretations are based on evidence — they are reasoned arguments, not opinions.

Prior knowledge

Students can identify perspectives, attitudes, and values of the past in sources (AC9HH7S06). They have not yet applied the concept of historical interpretation — understanding that historians construct different interpretations of the same events, that interpretations are argument-based rather than simply 'what happened', and that explaining why different interpretations exist is a distinct historical skill.

Width

Minimum
Explain the difference between a historical fact (verifiable from evidence) and a historical interpretation (an argument about meaning, significance, or causation constructed from evidence), and give an example of each.
Typical
Explain that historical interpretations are constructed arguments about significant events, individuals, or groups — that historians use evidence to build cases for particular understandings of the past, and that the same evidence can support different interpretations. Distinguish clearly between facts (what the evidence directly shows) and interpretations (what the evidence is taken to mean).
Wider
  • Examine a specific example where historical interpretation has changed over time — such as the interpretation of the fall of Rome, the significance of Alexander the Great, or the legacy of a First Nations leader — and analyse why the interpretation changed (new evidence, changed values, new questions).
  • Investigate the concept of historiography — the history of historical writing — and examine how different historians in different periods have interpreted the same event differently.
  • Explore what makes a historical interpretation strong or weak — examining criteria such as use of evidence, logical reasoning, accounting for counter-evidence, and consistency with established facts.

Hinge

Students who conflate facts with interpretations will treat competing historical interpretations as factual disagreements rather than constructed arguments in N2 — the analysis of why interpretations differ requires understanding that they are arguments, not rival facts.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe distinction between historical fact and historical interpretation is conceptually demanding for Year 7 students, who frequently conflate them. The reasoning scaffold — this is what the evidence directly shows (fact) vs this is the argument the historian constructs about what it means (interpretation) — needs to be made explicit with concrete examples before students can apply it reliably.
