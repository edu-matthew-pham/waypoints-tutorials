← AC9HH7K13 Significant individual in ancient society

Context — who the individual was and why they matter

AC9HH7K13 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 7 History · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in History: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify the significant individual and describe their position and role within the ancient society — including when they lived, what position they held, and why they are considered historically significant.

Success criteria

  • Identifies the significant individual by name, period, and position.
  • Describes the historical context in which the individual operated — not just biographical facts but the world they inhabited.
  • Explains why the individual is considered historically significant — connected to specific actions or impacts, not just reputation.

Prior knowledge

Students can describe the causes and effects of contacts and conflicts within ancient societies and explain how they drove historical change (AC9HH7K12). They have accumulated understanding of the ancient society's environment (K09), social structure (K10), beliefs and practices (K11), and dynamics of change (K12). They have not yet examined how a specific individual's role and achievements reflect, shaped, and were shaped by the historical context built across K09–K12.

Width

Minimum
Identify the significant individual, state when and where they lived, describe their role or position, and give a brief reason for their historical significance.
Typical
Describe the significant individual's position in the ancient society — their role (ruler, general, philosopher, reformer), the period in which they lived, and the historical context that shaped their circumstances. Explain what makes them historically significant — not just that they were famous, but what they did or represented that mattered.
Wider
  • Examine the sources of evidence available about the individual — what types of sources exist, who produced them, and what biases or perspectives they may reflect.
  • Investigate the historiographical debate about the individual — how have historians interpreted them differently over time, and why? For example, Alexander the Great has been interpreted as liberator, conqueror, and visionary; Cleopatra's legacy has been shaped by Roman propaganda.
  • Compare the individual's position to that of a comparable figure from another ancient society — examining what patterns of leadership, achievement, or significance recur and what is distinctive.

Hinge

Students without a factual foundation for the individual's identity, position, and context cannot anchor the achievement analysis in N2 or the significance argument in N3 — evaluation without biographical grounding produces unsupported claims.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Repertoire building: more cases to sort or classifyBefore students can reason about the individual's influence and achievements, they need a solid factual foundation — who the person was, when they lived, what position they occupied, what the historical context was. Without this base, analysis of achievements and influence is unanchored. This node builds the knowledge prerequisite for the evaluative and contextual work in Nodes 2 and 3.
