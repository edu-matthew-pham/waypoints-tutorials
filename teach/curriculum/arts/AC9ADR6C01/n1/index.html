Developing character

AC9ADR6C01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Years 5–6 The Arts · Drama: creating and making · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in The Arts: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can develop a character with consistent physical, vocal and relational qualities, distinguishable from their own default behaviour.

Success criteria

  • Student creates a character distinguishable from their own default through at least two consistent choices (voice and physical)
  • Student sustains character across a scene including during reactions to others
  • Student can describe the character's perspective or intention

Prior knowledge

Students can improvise and devise drama using elements of drama to communicate ideas and meaning (Y3-4). They can shape improvisations using voice, movement and language, and devise drama collaboratively with communicative intent.

Width

Minimum
Student creates and sustains one character across a short scene, making at least two distinct characterisation choices (voice and physicality).
Typical
Students explore physical, fictional or emotional spaces to create characters; they vary voice (projection, dynamics, pace, pause, pitch) and use movement and gesture to communicate characters' intentions; they rehearse and negotiate with others to interpret scripts and create characters and their relationships.
Wider
  • Students use empathy to develop characters from different perspectives, considering situations from multiple characters' viewpoints and challenging stereotypes
  • Students develop a 'new scene' to include in a dramatic re-telling of a known narrative, creating a character who was not in the original

Hinge

Consistent character development is the anchor for sustaining dramatic action; if characters collapse under pressure (breaking in and out of role or losing character consistency), the dramatic action cannot be sustained across longer or more complex forms — particularly in scripted drama.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Consolidation: more practise steps, short feedback loopsCharacter consistency develops through rehearsal and deliberate practice; students need repeated opportunities to 'inhabit' a character across different scenarios before they can sustain it through a full drama.
Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepDeveloping characters across different contexts — improvised, scripted, empathy-based, culturally situated — builds transferable characterisation skills and prevents students from defaulting to a single character type.
