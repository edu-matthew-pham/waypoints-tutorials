Reflecting on own influences and practice choices

AC9AVA8D02 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Years 7–8 The Arts · Visual arts: developing practices and skills

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in The Arts: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can reflect on the influences on their own practice choices and document how those influences are shaping specific decisions

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least one specific influence on a practice decision
  • Explains the connection between influence and decision — not just 'I decided to...'
  • Documentation uses visual arts terminology and goes beyond surface description

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Identifying influences on artistic choices.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least one influence on a specific choice in own practice; explain the connection between the influence and the decision made
Typical
Use teacher and student set goals to reflect on and practise skills over time; document how specific influences (investigation of other artists, material properties, personal experience) are shaping practice choices; evaluate outcomes
Wider
  • Develop an extended reflection documenting how multiple influences converge and sometimes conflict in own practice decisions
  • Explore colour theory in depth (harmonious/contrasting palettes, emotional associations, cultural meanings) and apply systematically — document the reasoning at each compositional decision
  • Investigate sustainable material practices and reflect on how sustainability constraints become productive influences rather than limitations

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Consolidation: more practise steps, short feedback loopsSustained reflective practice — setting goals, practising, evaluating against criteria, adjusting — requires disciplined iteration rather than broader contexts. The skill is in the reflection cycle itself: students who practise the cycle repeatedly develop genuine self-awareness as artists. More influence types don't help; more rounds of the reflection cycle do.
Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly describe what they did without reasoning through why they made specific choices or how influences shaped them. Scaffolding the reasoning (what influenced this decision, how did I respond to it, what alternative did I reject and why?) builds the reflective vocabulary that makes documentation meaningful rather than descriptive.
