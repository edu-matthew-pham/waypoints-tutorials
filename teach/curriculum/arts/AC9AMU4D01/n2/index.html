← AC9AMU4D01 Develop skills for manipulating elements

Listening to evaluate and compare effects

AC9AMU4D01 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Years 3–4 The Arts · Music: developing practices and skills

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in The Arts: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can listen to the effects they create by manipulating elements and evaluate which manipulations work better for the musical intention.

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Can compare two versions of a musical passage and identify at least one difference in element manipulation
  • Can give a reason for preferring one version over the other in terms of the music's intended effect

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Manipulating elements during performance.

Width

Minimum
Listen to two versions of a short piece (or two attempts at the same section) with different element manipulations; describe one difference and say which they prefer and why.
Typical
Listen to and evaluate effects of element manipulation across multiple dimensions — compare how easy/difficult different manipulations are to perform accurately, ask 'What works and why', listen to diverse music examples to identify how others have used elements and compare with their own choices.
Wider
  • Listen to diverse music examples and identify how rhythm, pitch and timbre are used — use these observations to inform choices in their own compositions or performances
  • Record two rehearsal attempts with different manipulations, listen back and write a brief comparison — practise the language of musical evaluation

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkEvaluative listening is a skill that needs scaffolding — students default to 'I liked it' without structure. Build evaluation language: 'What changed between the two versions?', 'Which version better communicates the mood?', 'How difficult was version two to perform accurately?' These questions develop the feedback loops that drive musical improvement.
