Visual arts as activism

AC9AVA10E02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 9–10 The Arts · Visual arts: exploring and responding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in The Arts: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can investigate how First Nations Australian artists use visual communication as a tool for activism — expressing calls for change through artworks, protest posters and street art.

Success criteria

  • Examines at least one example of First Nations Australian visual art as activism
  • Explains what change is being called for and how at least one visual choice communicates the message

Prior knowledge

Students have explored how First Nations Australian arts continue and revitalise cultures, and engaged with site-specific and community-based works (AC9AVA8E02, implied from progression). They understand arts as ongoing cultural practice. They have not yet engaged with First Nations Australian visual arts as explicitly political, activist or identity-challenging, or investigated 'first contacts' and colonisation from multiple perspectives within this frame.

Width

Minimum
Examine one example of First Nations Australian visual art as activism (protest poster, street art, campaign); explain what change is being called for and how visual choices communicate the message.
Typical
Research artworks, protest posters and street art by First Nations Australian artists used as activism; analyse how visual communication tools express calls for change; investigate the relationship between visual choices and activist purpose.
Wider
  • Investigate how First Nations Australian artists are caring for Country/Place through visual arts campaigns — explore how environmental activism is visualised and who the intended audience is
  • Compare protest posters by First Nations Australian artists from different historical periods — analyse how visual communication strategies have evolved and what stays constant

Hinge

The activist dimension is the key new conceptual territory at Y9-10 compared to earlier bands. Students who only understand First Nations Australian arts as cultural continuation and celebration cannot engage with the challenging and political dimensions the descriptor requires.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents may not have encountered First Nations Australian visual arts as explicitly political. Structured investigation (what change is being called for? who is the intended audience? how do visual choices serve the activist purpose?) builds the analytical framework needed.
