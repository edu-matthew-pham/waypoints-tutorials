Researching and developing personal approaches

AC9AVA10C02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 9–10 The Arts · Visual arts: creating and making · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in The Arts: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can research approaches to their subject or concept and develop a direction that reflects a personal point of view — not just reproducing a known style.

Lessons on this site

Success criteria

  • Researches at least two approaches to representing a subject or concept
  • Identifies one that reflects a personal point of view and gives a specific reason

Prior knowledge

Students can plan and create artworks with coordinated visual choices and can evaluate feedback in their practice. They make deliberate aesthetic decisions. They have not yet developed a sustained personal voice, self-initiated research process, or created artworks that deliberately challenge established perspectives.

Width

Minimum
Research at least two approaches to representing a subject or concept; identify one that resonates with a personal point of view and explain why.
Typical
Research approaches to representing subjects and concepts from particular viewpoints; annotate research with own images and written evaluations; develop a personal direction distinct from direct imitation; document the development of personal expression.
Wider
  • Investigate how artists across cultures represent similar themes from distinctly different viewpoints — analyse how personal, cultural and contextual factors shape the approach; develop a response that reflects your own position
  • Explore how visual arts can represent personal experiences — investigate how other artists have approached self-awareness, emotional awareness or wellbeing; develop your own approach

Hinge

Students who research only to imitate rather than to find a personal direction produce technically competent work without personal expression — the descriptor explicitly requires expression, and without self-directed research the work stays in exemplar convergence.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkDeveloping a personal point of view from research requires explicit reasoning — 'Why does this approach resonate? What does it allow me to do that others don't?' Without this reasoning scaffold, students default to whichever approach looks most achievable.
