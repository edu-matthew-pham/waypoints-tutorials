Designing representations with media languages

AC9AMA8C01 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Years 7–8 The Arts · Media arts: creating and making

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in The Arts: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can design representations using technical and symbolic elements — sound, lighting, framing, colour, font — that serve intended meaning for a specific audience

Success criteria

  • Designs at least two technical or symbolic elements with clear reasons
  • Explains how each choice constructs meaning for the audience
  • Design choices go beyond genre convention — connected to specific representational intent

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Planning with pre-production tools.

Width

Minimum
Design at least two technical or symbolic elements in a media arts work with a clear reason for how each serves the intended meaning for the audience
Typical
Design representations using a range of technical and symbolic elements; explain how each choice constructs intended meaning; consider how choices address issues such as bias, identity or stereotyping
Wider
  • Design representations that deliberately challenge stereotyping, bias or unequal power relationships — evaluate whether the technical and symbolic choices achieve the intended challenge
  • Experiment with technical and symbolic elements to construct representations that address social issues (identity, sustainability, social justice) — evaluate the effectiveness for the intended audience
  • Design representations that are safe, legal, ethical and responsible — develop criteria for evaluating own representational choices before production

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents commonly design representations using familiar conventions without reasoning about whether those conventions serve the intended meaning or simply reproduce dominant representations. Designing across different representational challenges — identity, power, social issues — forces students to develop deliberate media language design rather than defaulting to genre norms.
