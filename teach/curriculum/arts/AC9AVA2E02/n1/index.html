Country/Place in First Nations visual arts

AC9AVA2E02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 1–2 The Arts · Visual arts: exploring and responding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in The Arts: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify that many First Nations Australian artworks feature Country/Place as central — connecting where we are to who we are.

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least one way a First Nations Australian artwork represents or communicates place
  • Explains why place might be important to the artist or their community

Prior knowledge

Students have begun to explore arts across cultures and communities (AC9AVA2E01). They have some awareness that arts serve different purposes in different communities. They have not yet specifically explored First Nations Australian visual arts or the concept of Country/Place in art-making.

Width

Minimum
Look at one First Nations Australian artwork and identify one way it represents or communicates place.
Typical
Explore artworks by First Nations Australian artists that feature places as central; discuss what the artworks communicate about why place is part of identity; engage with artists' own voices through culturally authoritative resources.
Wider
  • Listen to First Nations Australian artists describe the importance of Country/Place to their art-making — using resources created or co-created by First Nations Australians
  • Explore artworks that represent First Nations Australians' interpretations of the night sky — discuss how different times of year and different perspectives shape the sky as subject matter

Hinge

The Country/Place concept is foundational to understanding First Nations Australian visual arts at this level — without it, students interpret these artworks through a generic 'landscape' lens that misses the cultural function.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe concept that place is part of identity is culturally unfamiliar for many students. Explicit discussion with prompts ('Why might where you are be part of who you are?') is needed before students can engage with the artworks meaningfully.
