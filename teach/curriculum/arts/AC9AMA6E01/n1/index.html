Representations and power

AC9AMA6E01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Years 5–6 The Arts · Media arts: exploring and responding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in The Arts: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain how media language choices (framing, colour, costume) construct power relationships and status in representations of characters.

Success criteria

  • Student can identify at least two media language choices (framing, colour, costume, setting) that construct a character's status or power
  • Student can explain how each choice communicates that status to the audience

Prior knowledge

Students can compare media arts works made for different purposes and explain how context shapes representations. They have not yet explored how media languages and technologies construct power relationships, stereotyping and bias — or systematically compared media representations across historical and cultural contexts.

Width

Minimum
Identify how framing, colour or costume is used in one media work to establish a character as high or low status — explaining the specific language choices that create that power relationship.
Typical
Explore how status and power relationships are constructed through framing, colour, costume and setting (hero/villain creation); investigate stereotyping and bias in game characters and media representations; apply Viewpoints questions to analyse power: 'How can I use framing to demonstrate a power relationship between characters?'
Wider
  • Investigate how status and power relationships are constructed through framing, colour and costume in a specific media form (e.g. advertising, game design, TV) — applying findings to original character design that deliberately constructs or challenges a power relationship
  • Compare how the same type of character (hero, villain, authority figure) is constructed differently in media works from different cultural contexts — identifying what the differences reveal about cultural values and assumptions

Hinge

Understanding how media languages construct power and status is the conceptual foundation for media literacy at Y5-6 — without it, students cannot critically analyse stereotyping, bias or ideological positioning in media.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents need explicit scaffolding to move from 'this character looks powerful' to 'the low-angle shot and dark costume construct power because...' Use Viewpoints questions as an analytical framework.
