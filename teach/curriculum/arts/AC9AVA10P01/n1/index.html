Evaluating exhibitions

AC9AVA10P01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 9–10 The Arts · Visual arts: presenting and performing · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in The Arts: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can visit or investigate exhibitions (physical or virtual) and evaluate the curatorial and presentational choices made — analysing how these choices create meaning and communicate artists' intentions.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two curatorial choices in an exhibition
  • Explains how each choice serves the exhibition's purpose or communicates the artist's/curator's intentions

Prior knowledge

Students can prepare artworks for formal display, create exhibition materials and make deliberate presentation decisions for different audiences (AC9AVA8P01, implied from progression). They have produced artist statements and curated class exhibitions. They have not yet evaluated existing exhibitions as a research base for their own curatorial decisions, or planned exhibitions that communicate a broader curatorial argument.

Width

Minimum
Visit or investigate one exhibition; identify at least two curatorial choices (spatial arrangement, sequencing, labelling, lighting, grouping) and explain how each serves the exhibition's purpose.
Typical
Visit and evaluate exhibitions in physical or virtual galleries, museums or community sites; analyse how artists and curators work together; investigate how curatorial choices create visual narrative and communicate intention; produce written, oral or multimedia evaluations.
Wider
  • Create a virtual or scale model of an exhibition of a researched artist — use this to communicate your understanding of the artist's intentions and refine based on peer feedback
  • Evaluate an exhibition of contemporary First Nations Australian art — analyse how the curatorial choices reflect Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property rights and community protocols

Hinge

Exhibition evaluation is the research base for the student's own curation — students who evaluate superficially cannot derive curatorial principles to apply to their own work. The depth of evaluation determines the quality of the curatorial reasoning that follows.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkCuratorial analysis requires a systematic framework — students need to analyse spatial arrangement, sequencing, labelling and audience flow as deliberate choices, not just notice what they see. Structured evaluation tools build this analytical capacity.
