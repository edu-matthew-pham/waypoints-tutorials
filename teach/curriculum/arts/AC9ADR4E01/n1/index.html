Purpose and audience across contexts

AC9ADR4E01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Years 3–4 The Arts · Drama: exploring and responding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in The Arts: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify the purpose of a drama — entertainment, cultural transmission, infotainment, community ritual — and describe what an audience needs to know to engage with it.

Success criteria

  • Student identifies a purpose for a specific drama from a given cultural context
  • Student describes at least one thing an audience needs to know to engage with that drama

Prior knowledge

Students can explore where, why and how people across cultures experience drama (Y1-2). They understand that drama communicates feelings, ideas and cultural knowledge for different communities.

Width

Minimum
Student identifies one purpose for a specific drama they have experienced or studied, and names one thing an audience needs to understand.
Typical
Students listen to actors or makers discuss how they developed characters and what they found interesting about the imaginary world they created; they reflect on live or recorded performances asking what purpose the drama serves; they compare expectations and requirements of performers and audience in different cultural settings.
Wider
  • Students compare a drama from Australia with one from a country or region in Asia, identifying similarities and differences in purpose and audience relationship
  • Students examine puppetry or physical theatre in their community and compare it with examples from other cultures, times or places

Hinge

Understanding purpose and audience is prerequisite to the analytical inquiry asked by the descriptor — 'how drama is created/performed' is only interpretable if students have a frame for 'why'; without it, observation stays at surface description.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkUse structured reflection questions before and after viewing drama: 'What is the purpose of this drama?', 'What does the audience need to know?', 'Why do you think people from this culture create drama for this purpose?'
