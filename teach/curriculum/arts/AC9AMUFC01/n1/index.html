← AC9AMUFC01 Create works that communicate ideas

Ideas from experience as starting points

AC9AMUFC01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Foundation The Arts · Music: creating and making · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in The Arts: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify a personal experience or idea as the starting point for an arts work and describe what they want to communicate.

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Can name one personal experience or idea they want to communicate in their arts work
  • Can describe (in any form — drawing, speaking, gesturing) what they want the work to show or express

Prior knowledge

No formal prior arts learning assumed. Students bring play, curiosity and informal experience with voice, movement and sound from early childhood. This is their first formal encounter with creating arts works within a school context.

Width

Minimum
Identify one personal experience (e.g. a favourite activity or a feeling) and describe what they want to show or express.
Typical
Draw on personal or social experiences, play, imagination or observation as starting points; name what they want to communicate — e.g. 'I want to show how running feels' or 'I want to tell the story of my pet.'
Wider
  • Draw on literature, artworks or stories from their culture as starting points — compare how their idea connects to or differs from the source
  • Use dramatic play scenarios as starting points for both musical and visual ideas — ask 'What could the sound of this story be?'

Hinge

Students who cannot identify a personal idea or intent before making will produce work without communicative purpose. The next phase — making deliberate choices in a form — requires a starting intention. Without it, students move through the activity without the central act of the descriptor being engaged.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkUse questioning to help students articulate their starting idea before they begin making — 'What do you want to show?', 'Who is this for?', 'What should people feel when they see/hear it?' This scaffolds the intent-setting that the rest of the making depends on.
