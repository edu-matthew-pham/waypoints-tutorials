Permission and protocols for sharing

AC9AMA2P01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Years 1–2 The Arts · Media arts: presenting and performing · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in The Arts: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify and apply at least one permission consideration before sharing a media arts work.

Success criteria

  • Student can identify at least one permission requirement before sharing a media work
  • Student applies that permission principle in at least one class sharing activity

Prior knowledge

Students can share arts works informally and respond to audience questions. They have not yet shared specifically media arts works in ways that require permission, protocol consideration, or audience relationship thinking.

Width

Minimum
Identify one permission requirement before sharing a media work (e.g. asking peers before using their images) and apply it in a class sharing context.
Typical
Apply permission principles across sharing contexts — obtaining individual permission for photographs, understanding that cultural material may belong to a group rather than an individual; consult relevant people or online safety resources; develop class rules for sharing.
Wider
  • Develop a class set of rules for using and creating images, sounds and text in media arts works — presenting the rules to the class and explaining the reasoning behind each rule with a real scenario
  • Explore the difference between individual permission and permission for cultural material belonging to a collective group — using a specific scenario (e.g. a class news bulletin) to ground the distinction

Hinge

Permission principles are the non-negotiable precondition for any public sharing — without this, sharing activities cannot proceed ethically, and the habit is foundational to all future media arts practice.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkUse real scenarios to build reasoning about permission — 'Before we share this photo of Jake, what do we need to do? Why?' Scenario-based reasoning transfers better than abstract rule recitation.
