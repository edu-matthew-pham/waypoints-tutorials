Identifying personal influences

AC9AVA10D02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 9–10 The Arts · Visual arts: developing practices and skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in The Arts: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can investigate and reflect on the role of their own culture, family and personal experiences as influences on their visual arts practice.

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least one personal influence (cultural, familial or experiential)
  • Explains how it has shaped at least one aspect of their practice — a choice, direction or recurring concern

Prior knowledge

Students have begun identifying an emerging personal style and have investigated artists from diverse contexts. They can make informed choices based on documented experimentation. They have not yet systematically reflected on how personal, cultural and contextual influences shape their own practice, or used investigation of others' influences to understand their own.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least one personal influence (cultural background, family, personal experience) and explain how it has shaped one aspect of their practice.
Typical
Investigate how culture, family and personal experiences influence what they are drawn to, what they make and how they make it; document reflections in journal form; connect personal influences to specific choices visible in their work.
Wider
  • Explore current issues of personal importance (local or global) as a starting point for artwork — document how the issue connects to your own experiences and values, and how this shapes your visual response
  • Investigate how your visual arts practice can explore and represent personal experiences — research how other artists have approached self-awareness and emotional awareness; develop your own approach

Hinge

Personal influence reflection is the foundation for this descriptor — students who cannot identify their own influences cannot meaningfully compare them with other artists' influences, and cannot use influence understanding to resolve making choices.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkInfluence reflection is not automatic — students need structured prompts ('What experiences have shaped what I make? What would be absent from my work without my background?') to surface influences that are invisible precisely because they are familiar.
