Identifying dramatic potential

AC9ADR4C01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 3–4 The Arts · Drama: creating and making · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in The Arts: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify a situation, issue or story worth exploring through drama and name what ideas or perspectives it raises.

Success criteria

  • Student names a situation or perspective they want to explore through their drama
  • Student can explain what ideas or feelings their drama is trying to communicate

Prior knowledge

Students can create and co-create fictional situations using elements of drama in dramatic play and process drama (Y1-2). They can take on roles, contribute ideas, and communicate non-verbally within a group improvisation.

Width

Minimum
Student identifies one scenario or situation from a given starting point (story, image, issue) and names what they want to explore through drama.
Typical
Students create drama by exploring and experiencing roles and situations they initiate and develop; they consider how empathy assists them to take on roles and 'stand in the shoes' of another; they explore roles in a 'miniature world' or from everyday interactions.
Wider
  • Students improvise drama exploring situations from another time or place, imagining themselves as participants in an event
  • Students devise dialogue for a previously 'wordless story' or dramatic action for a story known only through spoken/audio sources
  • Students devise drama communicating their connection to a place in their community, drawing on First Nations Australians' drama about Country/Place as a starting point

Hinge

If students cannot identify what a drama might be about — cannot articulate the idea, perspective or meaning they want to explore — shaping and devising collapses into random action; the purpose-driven crafting in subsequent nodes depends on this.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkUse guiding questions ('What is this story really about?', 'What do you want an audience to think or feel?', 'Whose perspective are you exploring?') before improvisation begins.
Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepOffer varied stimulus material — images, texts, community contexts, historical moments — to ensure students with different entry points can each find genuine dramatic potential.
