Responsible use of media technologies

AC9AMA2D01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 1–2 The Arts · Media arts: developing practices and skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in The Arts: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify at least one principle of responsible media technology use and apply it when capturing media elements.

Success criteria

  • Student can identify at least one responsible use principle for media technology
  • Student applies that principle when capturing or sharing media in class activities

Prior knowledge

Students have used digital devices for play and idea exploration. They can capture images or sounds informally. They have not yet explicitly considered responsible use, or deliberately combined visual and audio elements to communicate.

Width

Minimum
Identify one responsible use principle (e.g. asking permission before taking photos of people) and apply it during a media capture activity.
Typical
Discuss and apply responsible use principles when capturing images, sounds and text — including permission to use images of people, care with cultural material, and safe online sharing; connect responsible use to real situations (school excursion photos, class news bulletin).
Wider
  • Develop a class set of rules for using and creating images, sounds and text in media arts works — consulting relevant people (teachers, cultural knowledge holders) and online safety resources, and presenting the rules to the class
  • Explore what permission means in different contexts (individual permission vs permission for cultural material belonging to a group) through specific case examples

Hinge

Responsible use principles are a prerequisite for all public sharing of student work — if not established here, subsequent presenting and sharing activities lack the ethical grounding that ACARA requires.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkUse structured scenarios to build reasoning about responsible use — 'Is it OK to take this photo? What do we need to do first?' This develops transferable principles rather than rules memorised for a single context.
