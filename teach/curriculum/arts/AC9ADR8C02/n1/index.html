Evaluating element and convention choices

AC9ADR8C02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 7–8 The Arts · Drama: creating and making

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in The Arts: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can evaluate their use of elements and conventions against intended dramatic purpose and identify specific refinements

Success criteria

  • Evaluates at least one element or convention against intended purpose
  • Identifies at least one specific refinement with a reason — not just 'make it better'
  • Evaluation is connected to dramatic intent, not just personal preference

Prior knowledge

Students can make basic evaluations of own drama work (AC9ADR6C02). They have not yet systematically evaluated element and convention choices against intended dramatic purpose, or used that evaluation to make and document specific refinements.

Width

Minimum
Evaluate at least one element or convention in own drama against the intended purpose; identify at least one specific refinement and explain why it improves the drama
Typical
Evaluate use of elements, conventions and design choices against dramatic intent; use Viewpoints questions to interrogate own choices; identify and document specific refinements with reasoning
Wider
  • Use Viewpoints questions systematically to evaluate own drama — 'What are your intentions?', 'Is meaning clear?', 'How could elements be used differently?' — document what each question reveals about gaps between intention and execution
  • Evaluate how costume, props and setting contribute to dramatic meaning — assess whether design choices support or undermine the intended effect
  • Evaluate how empathy is built into the dramatic experience — assess whether character portrayal and dramatic choices treat subjects with the care they deserve

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly evaluate drama as 'good' or 'needs work' without specifying what element or convention choice is at issue and why. Scaffolding the evaluation (what is the intention, which element or convention serves that intention, does the current choice achieve it, what specific change would improve it?) builds targeted evaluation rather than general impression.
