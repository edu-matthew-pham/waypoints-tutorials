Marketing chains and ethical trade

AC9TDE10K04 n3 · waypoint 3 of 3 · Years 9–10 Design and Technologies · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Design and Technologies: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can examine the marketing chain of agricultural products and analyse the effect of processing, advertising and cash crop dynamics on price, demand and communities.

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two distinct stages in a marketing chain
  • Explains how a decision at one stage affects price, demand or community outcomes at another

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Environmental interdependencies in production.

Width

Minimum
Describe at least two stages in the marketing chain of an agricultural product; explain how processing or marketing at one stage affects price or demand.
Typical
Analyse the marketing chain of a range of agricultural products — from production through processing, distribution and retail to consumer; explain how product processing and advertising affect demand and price; examine the impact of cash crops on communities, including who benefits and who bears risk.
Wider
  • Trace the full marketing chain of a specific agricultural product (e.g. coffee, cotton, cacao) and make a judgement on where ethical trade practices are and aren't present
  • Examine how food labelling (including country of origin, fair trade, organic) affects consumer decisions and producer pricing — evaluate how effective labelling is as an ethical tool
  • Investigate the impact of a cash crop economy on a specific community — analyse how dependence on a single export crop affects food security and economic resilience

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkMarketing chain analysis requires students to trace value and decision-making across multiple actors — producers, processors, distributors, retailers, consumers. Without a structured chain-tracing approach, students focus on production and ignore downstream dynamics that determine who benefits and who bears risk.
