Developing a design brief

AC9TDE10P01 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Years 9–10 Design and Technologies · Processes and production skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Design and Technologies: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can independently develop a design brief that defines the problem, identifies user needs, and establishes the scope and constraints of a designed solution.

Lessons on this site

Success criteria

  • Brief names a specific problem (not just a domain or product type)
  • At least two user needs are stated and connected to the problem
  • At least one constraint or scope boundary is explicit

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Analysing needs and design opportunities.

Width

Minimum
Write a design brief that names the problem, identifies at least two user needs, and states at least one constraint or scope boundary.
Typical
Develop a design brief independently that incorporates: a clear problem definition, identified user needs (including diverse users), sustainability and ethical considerations, universal design or Safety by Design principles where relevant, and scope and constraints that define what the solution will and will not address.
Wider
  • Develop a design brief for a community need by iterating through consultation with community members — document how the brief changed through consultation
  • Develop a design brief that explicitly addresses Indigenous cultural and intellectual property rights — explain what considerations this adds to the brief that a standard brief would not include
  • Compare a brief developed from desk research alone with one developed through user interviews — analyse what the interviews added and why it matters

Hinge

A design brief without specific constraints and assessable criteria makes N3 material and technology selection arbitrary — students cannot justify selections against criteria that don't exist, and the entire design rationale collapses.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkA design brief is a reasoning document — students who write briefs by filling in a template without connecting user needs to constraints to scope produce briefs that don't actually define the problem. The reasoning chain (who is this for? → what do they need? → what constrains the solution? → what is in/out of scope?) is what makes a brief useful.
