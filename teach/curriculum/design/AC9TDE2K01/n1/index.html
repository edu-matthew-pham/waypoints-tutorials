Identifying community needs that design addresses

AC9TDE2K01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Years 1–2 Design and Technologies · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Design and Technologies: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify a personal or local community need and explain how a familiar product, service or environment is designed to meet it

Success criteria

  • Names a specific community need (not just a use)
  • Explains how a product, service or environment is designed to meet that need

Prior knowledge

Students can explore how familiar products, services and environments are designed by people (AC9TDEFK01). They can identify design decisions and connect them to a purpose. They have not yet connected design to community-level needs or to sustainability considerations.

Width

Minimum
Identify one community need (e.g. medical care, shelter, healthy food access) and explain how a product, service or environment is designed to meet it
Typical
Identify community needs across multiple contexts (health, shelter, food, communication, community access); explain how specific design and production decisions address each need; compare how the same need is met in different ways
Wider
  • Explore how First Nations Australians have long understood their dependence on living systems — for example how the Ngarrindjeri Peoples sustainably make woven items from a grass-like sedge; identify the community need being met and how the design reflects that need
  • Ask questions about the design of shelters in public spaces (school, sportsground) — identify the community need and how the shelter design addresses it including accessibility

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents readily identify that products exist for a purpose but often describe use rather than need — 'it's for sleeping' rather than 'it meets the community's need for shelter'. Multiple community contexts (medical care, shelter, food production, communication) where the need is explicitly named before the design is examined builds the need-first reasoning habit.
