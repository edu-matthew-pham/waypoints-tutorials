What competing factors means in design

AC9TDE6K01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Years 5–6 Design and Technologies · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Design and Technologies: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify that designers must weigh multiple factors that can pull in different directions, and name at least three factors that commonly compete

Success criteria

  • Names at least three factors that designers must consider
  • Explains at least one example where two factors conflict with each other

Prior knowledge

Students can examine design and technologies occupations and factors including sustainability that impact on the design of products, services and environments (AC9TDE4K01). They have identified that designers consider multiple factors. They have not yet explained how competing factors are weighed against each other — the tension between them and how practitioners resolve it.

Width

Minimum
Name at least three factors a designer must consider (e.g. cost, sustainability, function, aesthetics, safety) and give one example where two factors pull in opposite directions
Typical
Explain that design involves balancing competing factors that cannot all be maximised simultaneously; identify and describe how specific factors (aesthetics, function, sustainability, safety, cost) compete in a familiar product or service context
Wider
  • Investigate how First Nations Australians considered competing factors in designing fish harvesting technologies — comparing selective harvesting techniques (fish traps, fish poisons allowing bycatch release) with high-yield non-selective methods like trawling
  • Investigate how Safety by Design principles (prevention, protection, proactive change) compete with other design factors such as cost and ease of use in a product or service context
  • Examine how engineers use biomimicry to resolve competing factors — finding a natural solution where function, sustainability and materials performance are already optimised

Hinge

Students who cannot identify competing factors have no framework for design decision-making — every downstream capability (P01 investigation, P04 criteria negotiation, P03 material selection) requires reasoning through trade-offs. Without this, design decisions reduce to personal preference.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents tend to treat factors as independent checklists rather than tensions. Multiple design contexts — each featuring a different pair of competing factors — help students see that the conflict between factors is the design problem, not just a constraint list.
