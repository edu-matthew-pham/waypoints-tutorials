Food characteristics that influence selection

AC9TDE6K04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Years 5–6 Design and Technologies · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Design and Technologies: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify and explain how food characteristics — nutritional value, availability, spoilage, taste — influence which foods are selected for healthy eating

Success criteria

  • Names at least two food characteristics
  • Explains how each characteristic influences whether a food is selected for healthy eating

Prior knowledge

Students can describe the ways food can be selected and prepared for healthy eating (AC9TDE4K04). They understand that food choices affect health. They have not yet explained how specific food characteristics (nutritional value, spoilage, preparation requirements, taste) drive selection and preparation decisions.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least two food characteristics and explain how each influences whether a food is selected for a healthy meal
Typical
Explain how nutritional value, availability, spoilage risk, preparation requirements and taste each influence food selection; apply the Australian Dietary Guidelines to plan a healthy meal or lunchbox for a specific individual — explaining selection decisions in terms of food characteristics
Wider
  • Investigate how First Nations Australians have long selected foods based on nutritional value, seasonal availability, spoilage characteristics, and preparation requirements — exploring how this knowledge guided sustainable food choices over generations
  • Explore how flavour profiles (sour, salty, sweet, spicy, umami — particularly from Asian cuisines) influence food selection and preparation preferences across different cultural groups
  • Investigate how food service options at a local restaurant or café reflect food characteristic trade-offs — identifying how chefs balance nutrition, taste, availability and spoilage in menu design

Hinge

Food selection reasoning in N1 cascades directly into N2 — if students cannot identify characteristics (spoilage risk, nutritional value) as the basis for selection, they cannot connect those same characteristics to preparation decisions. The two nodes share the same conceptual structure.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents tend to select foods based on taste preference and treat healthy eating as a rule set rather than a characteristic-based reasoning process. Multiple contexts — cultural traditions, dietary guidelines, food service — help students see food selection as applying a set of criteria to characteristics, not following a prescribed list.
