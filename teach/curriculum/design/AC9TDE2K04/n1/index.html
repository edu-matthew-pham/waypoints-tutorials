Selecting foods for healthy eating

AC9TDE2K04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Years 1–2 Design and Technologies · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Design and Technologies: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify a range of foods and categorise them into food groups for healthy eating

Success criteria

  • Correctly categorises at least three foods into food groups
  • Names at least two different food groups

Prior knowledge

Students have explored how plants and animals are grown for food (AC9TDE2K03). They can identify plant and animal food sources. They have not yet organised foods into health categories or connected preparation methods to healthy eating outcomes.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least three familiar foods and categorise them into food groups using the Australian Guide to Healthy Eating
Typical
Identify a wide range of foods and categorise them into the 5 food groups; discuss ways to eat a variety of food groups; begin to design a simple menu that includes multiple food groups
Wider
  • Explore both the Australian Guide to Healthy Eating and the Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Guide to Healthy Eating — identify foods in each of the 5 food groups from both guides; compare foods that are familiar to different communities
  • Explore how people from countries of Asia select food for healthy eating based on available plants and animals and cultural practices — identify similarities and differences with the Australian guide
  • Explore the local supermarket to observe the variety and placement of foods — consider how shelf design may influence food selection for healthy eating

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents readily categorise foods they personally eat but struggle with the full range of food groups when their exposure is narrow. Multiple food contexts — including foods from First Nations and Asian food traditions — builds a richer, less culturally narrow understanding of 'healthy eating'.
