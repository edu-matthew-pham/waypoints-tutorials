Product life cycle thinking

AC9TDE10K01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Years 9–10 Design and Technologies · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Design and Technologies: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain what product life cycle thinking is and how it influences design decisions.

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Correctly explains what product life cycle thinking considers beyond the point of sale
  • Gives a specific example of how it has influenced at least one design decision

Prior knowledge

Students can analyse how people in design and technologies occupations consider ethical and sustainability factors when designing solutions (AC9TDE8K01). They can identify how designers respond to community needs and sustainability constraints. They have not yet analysed how ethical, security and sustainability factors combine to drive innovation, nor examined product life cycle thinking, mass production systems, or the role of global professional collaboration.

Width

Minimum
Explain what product life cycle thinking means and give one example of how it has influenced a design decision (e.g. material choice, design for disassembly, re-use planning).
Typical
Explain how life cycle thinking shifts decisions across the design process — material selection, production method, packaging, end-of-life — and give examples of products where life cycle thinking has produced a different design outcome than conventional approaches.
Wider
  • Analyse the full product life cycle of a specific everyday object and identify where the greatest environmental impact occurs — explain what design change at that stage would have most effect
  • Compare a product designed with life cycle thinking (e.g. modular, repairable) with a conventional equivalent — evaluate the trade-offs for manufacturer, consumer and environment
  • Investigate how First Nations Australians' traditional production practices embody life cycle thinking — for example, the sustainable production and use of culturally significant pigments through calcination

Hinge

Life cycle thinking is the analytical framework used at N2, N3 and N4 — students who cannot apply it cannot evaluate mass production systems, professional design contributions, or the consequences of decisions through a sustainability lens.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepLife cycle thinking is abstract without concrete examples. Multiple product cases — designed for disassembly, with lower-carbon material substitutions, and traditional sustainable practices — each make a different aspect of the concept visible. Students who see only one case often reduce it to recycling.
