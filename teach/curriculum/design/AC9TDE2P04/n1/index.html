Sequencing making steps

AC9TDE2P04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Years 1–2 Design and Technologies · Processes and production skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Design and Technologies: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can sequence the steps needed to make a designed solution in a logical order

Success criteria

  • Sequences at least four steps in a logical order
  • Can explain why at least one step must come before another

Prior knowledge

Students can use materials and equipment to make solutions (AC9TDEFP01, AC9TDE2P02). They have followed steps to make things. They have not yet formally sequenced steps for making as a planning activity, recorded procedures, or identified roles for cooperative group making.

Width

Minimum
Sequence at least four steps for making a simple product in the correct order; explain why the order matters
Typical
Use lists or storyboarding to plan and sequence steps for making across different production contexts; record the procedure; explain why steps must be in a particular order and what happens if they are not
Wider
  • Record the ordered steps for making a salad or other food preparation task — describe each step and explain what would happen if the order changed
  • Create a planning list or storyboard for an electronic planting calendar — sequence the steps and explain the order decisions

Hinge

Step sequencing is the planning input for Node 2's cooperative role assignment — without a sequence to distribute across group members, roles cannot be meaningfully identified and the cooperative making has no structure.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStep sequencing in design feels obvious for simple tasks but students often conflate steps with materials or confuse what to do with when to do it. Multiple production contexts — food preparation (where order is safety-critical), construction (where joins must be made before assembly), garden tasks — each expose different dimensions of why sequence matters.
