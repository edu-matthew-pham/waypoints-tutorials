Business practices for consumer protection and ethical decision-making

AC9HE9K05 n4 · waypoint 4 of 4 · Year 9 Economics and Business · Knowledge and understanding

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Economics and Business: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify business practices that protect consumer safety and reflect on the importance of ethical decision-making and corporate social responsibility in consumer and financial contexts.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two business practices that protect consumer safety
  • Explains why ethical decision-making and CSR are important in consumer and financial contexts
  • Considers consequences for multiple stakeholders (individuals, community, environment)

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 3: Individual strategies to manage consumer and financial risk.

Width

Minimum
Identify one practice businesses use to protect consumer safety (e.g. mandatory product standards, cooling-off periods) and explain why ethical decision-making matters for consumers and businesses.
Typical
Identify and explain practices businesses use to protect consumer safety: mandatory and voluntary standards (product safety requirements), product safety recalls (removing dangerous products from market), and cooling-off periods (allowing consumers to reverse financial decisions). Reflect on the importance of ethical decision-making and corporate social responsibility when making consumer and financial decisions — including consequences for customers, families, communities and the environment.
Wider
  • Investigate a specific Australian product recall or consumer protection enforcement action and analyse what the business did, what it should have done, and the consequences of its actions for consumers and its own reputation
  • Examine how corporate social responsibility reporting has changed expectations of business ethical behaviour in Australia, and whether voluntary CSR commitments effectively protect consumers or whether mandatory standards are needed
  • Analyse how Indigenous consumer protection issues (e.g. predatory financial products targeting remote communities) reveal gaps in Australia's consumer protection system and what businesses and government should do differently

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Integration: a combining task at the wider tierThis node integrates risk types (Node 1), rewards (Node 2), and individual strategies (Node 3) into an account of how businesses manage the risk-reward relationship on behalf of consumers. Students should connect: what risks exist → what rewards are offered → how businesses protect consumers from harm while offering rewards.
Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkEthical decision-making requires students to reason about consequences across multiple stakeholders simultaneously. A stakeholder consequence framework (individual consumer → family → community → environment) structures the reflection so students move beyond 'ethics is good' generalisations.
