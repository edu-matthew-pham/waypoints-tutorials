Why countries trade

AC9HE9K03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 9 Economics and Business · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Economics and Business: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain the reasons countries trade, including resource variations, comparative advantage, surplus goods and specialist goods.

Success criteria

  • Explains at least two distinct reasons why countries trade
  • Uses a concrete example to illustrate each reason
  • Uses terms such as 'specialisation', 'comparative advantage', 'resources' correctly

Prior knowledge

From AC9HE9K02 (direct predecessor): how economic decision-making involves interdependence of consumers, businesses, financial sector and government, including globalisation and supply chains. From AC9HE9K01: Australia's financial sector and its global role. Students understand economic interdependence but have not yet specifically investigated why countries trade or analysed Australia's trade relationship with Asia.

Width

Minimum
Explain two reasons why countries trade (e.g. variations in resource availability and the ability to sell surplus production).
Typical
Explain the full range of reasons countries trade: variations in quantity and quality of economic resources within and between countries; ability to sell surplus goods and services; need to buy goods and services not produced domestically; comparative advantage (specialising in what they produce most efficiently). Use concrete examples for each reason.
Wider
  • Examine comparative advantage theory through a concrete bilateral example (e.g. Australia-Japan trade) and explain how specialisation creates mutual benefit even when one country could produce both goods
  • Investigate how trade agreements (e.g. ASEAN-Australia-NZ FTA, ChAFTA) reduce barriers to trade and explain how they change the reasons and incentives for trading
  • Analyse how non-economic reasons for trade (diplomatic relationships, national security, cultural exchange) interact with economic reasons in shaping Australia's trade partnerships

Hinge

Students who understand trade only as 'buying what you can't make' will be unable to analyse Australia's trade patterns in subsequent nodes — many Australian exports (iron ore, coal, education) are sold to countries that could obtain alternatives, making the resource-advantage and surplus explanations essential for accurate pattern analysis.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkTrade reasons are often conflated by students (especially comparative advantage, which is counterintuitive). A structured framework — resource difference → specialisation → mutual benefit — helps students reason about why trade creates gains rather than treating it as a zero-sum exchange.
