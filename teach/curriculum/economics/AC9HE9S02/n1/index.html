Locating sources using contextually specific search strategies

AC9HE9S02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 9 Economics and Business · Skills

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Economics and Business: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can locate online sources using contextually specific economic and business terms and criteria relevant to the issue being investigated.

Success criteria

  • Uses specific economic and business search terms or criteria to locate sources
  • Identifies at least two sources relevant to the investigation issue
  • Explains why each located source is likely to contain relevant evidence

Prior knowledge

From Y8 AC9HE8S02 (direct predecessor): locate, select and organise information and data from a range of sources. Students can locate and organise sources. Y9 adds 'analyse' to the capability — specifically analysing for relevance (does this help answer the question?) and reliability (who collected it, when, why?) — and adds selecting and presenting data using specialised digital tools and processes.

Width

Minimum
Locate at least two relevant sources using specific economic and business search terms (not generic terms) for a nominated issue.
Typical
Locate online sources using contextually specific terms (e.g. 'global markets', 'trade between Australia and Asia', 'consumer scam risk') or criteria (e.g. 'find case studies related to trade between Australia and Asia'). Demonstrate that search strategies are tailored to the issue's economic and business context rather than generic, and that the search approach identifies sources with the type of evidence needed to address the inquiry question.
Wider
  • Apply structured search strategies across different source types — official government statistics (ABS, RBA), industry reports, academic summaries, news sources — and explain why each type is appropriate for different evidence needs
  • Locate sources for a contested economic issue (e.g. effects of trade liberalisation) from multiple perspectives — government, business peak bodies, consumer advocates — and document the search process
  • Use specialist economic databases or tools (e.g. World Bank Open Data, DFAT trade statistics portal, ABS.Stat) to locate primary statistical data and explain when primary data is preferable to secondary commentary

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Repertoire building: more cases to sort or classifyStudents benefit from a repertoire of specific source types and databases for Economics and Business investigations. Introducing each source type with a demonstration of its strengths — and when to prefer it — builds search capability beyond 'Google it'.
