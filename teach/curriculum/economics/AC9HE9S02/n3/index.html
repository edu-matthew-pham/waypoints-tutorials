Selecting for reliability

AC9HE9S02 n3 · waypoint 3 of 4 · Year 9 Economics and Business · Skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Economics and Business: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can select information and data by evaluating reliability — considering how and when data was collected, who collected it, and for what purpose.

Success criteria

  • Applies at least two reliability criteria (who, when, purpose) to evaluate a source
  • Explains how reliability judgements affect how a source should be used in an investigation
  • Distinguishes between sources with different reliability profiles for the same topic

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Selecting for relevance.

Width

Minimum
Apply at least two reliability questions (who collected it? when? for what purpose?) to evaluate one source, explaining how the answers affect how the source should be used.
Typical
Select and analyse information and data for reliability using the elaboration questions: 'How and when was it collected?', 'Who collected the data?', and 'For what purpose was the data collected?' Apply these criteria to assess the reliability of different source types — official statistics, industry-funded reports, advocacy group data, news articles — and explain how reliability judgements affect how sources can be used in an investigation.
Wider
  • Apply reliability analysis to a pair of sources on the same economic issue that reach different conclusions — explaining what the reliability differences reveal about why the conclusions differ
  • Examine how economic data published by government agencies (ABS, RBA, Treasury) differs in reliability characteristics from data published by industry peak bodies or lobby groups, and explain when each is appropriate to use
  • Investigate a specific case of misleading economic statistics in Australian public debate — tracing how data collection methods, purpose or framing affected the reliability of claims made

Hinge

Students who cannot evaluate reliability will use industry-funded or advocacy data as equivalent to peer-reviewed or official statistics, leading to invalid conclusions in analysis tasks and weak justification in evaluative responses.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkReliability judgement requires applying multiple criteria simultaneously (when, who, why) and drawing a reasoned conclusion about trustworthiness. A structured evaluation framework prevents students from treating reliability as binary (reliable/not reliable) rather than as a spectrum with specific limitations.
Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepDifferent source types (official statistics, industry data, advocacy reports) have systematically different reliability profiles. Encountering each type in context — rather than learning reliability as an abstract concept — builds transferable evaluation skill.
