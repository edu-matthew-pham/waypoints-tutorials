Values and entrepreneurial decision-making

AC9HE7K03 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Year 7 Economics and Business · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Economics and Business: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can analyse how values — including identifying opportunities, stakeholder negotiation, and compliance — influence entrepreneurial decision-making.

Success criteria

  • Identifies a specific value held by an entrepreneur and explains how it shaped a business decision
  • Connects the decision to an outcome for the business or community

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Entrepreneurial knowledge and skills.

Width

Minimum
Identify one example of how a personal or cultural value has influenced an entrepreneur's decision; explain the decision and its consequence.
Typical
Analyse how values influence entrepreneurial decision-making across three areas: identifying and taking advantage of opportunities, negotiating with stakeholders, and complying with laws and regulations; apply to at least one specific entrepreneur with concrete evidence.
Wider
  • Examine how First Nations Australian entrepreneurs navigate the intersection of business values and cultural responsibilities — using the Gumatj Aboriginal Corporation or a similar example to explore decision-making that balances economic and community obligations
  • Investigate a case where an entrepreneur's values led to a decision that prioritised community or environmental benefit over maximum profit, and evaluate the business outcome
  • Analyse a business decision that involved legal or regulatory compliance — what values drove compliance or non-compliance, and what were the consequences?

Hinge

Understanding that values shape entrepreneurial decisions is necessary for the integration node, which requires students to explain how characteristics influence business success. Students who focus only on skills without considering values will produce incomplete explanations of entrepreneurial success and failure.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents often treat values as personal opinions rather than as active factors in business decision-making. Scaffold with specific decision examples where the value is identifiable and traceable to an outcome: 'This entrepreneur valued community employment, so they chose X over Y, resulting in Z.'
Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepFirst Nations entrepreneurial contexts offer particularly rich value-decision cases — where cultural, community and economic values intersect — and help students move beyond a profit-centric model of entrepreneurial motivation.
