What counts as a cost and a benefit

AC9HE7S04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 7 Economics and Business · Skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Economics and Business: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify the potential costs and benefits of an economic decision, recognising that both financial and non-financial factors count.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least one financial and one non-financial cost and benefit for a given decision
  • Explains why each factor counts as a cost or benefit

Prior knowledge

Students can develop evidence-based conclusions and propose actions with assessed effects (AC9HS6S05, AC9HS6S06). The Year 7 demand adds the economic frame of costs and benefits — structuring a response to an issue by explicitly identifying and weighing the trade-offs involved, and explaining that a decision selects one alternative over another.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least one cost and one benefit for a given decision; include at least one non-financial factor.
Typical
Identify the potential costs and benefits of an economic decision — including both financial and non-financial factors such as time, opportunity cost, social impact; distinguish direct from indirect costs and benefits; apply to realistic consumer and community decisions.
Wider
  • Analyse costs and benefits in a government resource allocation decision — where community benefit may be broad and indirect, and costs distributed unevenly across groups
  • Examine how a First Nations community might assess the costs and benefits of a new economic development proposal — weighing financial returns against cultural, environmental and community impacts
  • Evaluate a historical business decision by identifying costs and benefits at the time versus in retrospect — exploring how uncertainty affects cost-benefit analysis

Hinge

Students who reduce costs and benefits to financial amounts only will be unable to construct a complete response to an economic issue. Non-financial costs and benefits — time, social impact, opportunity cost, environmental effects — are essential to the full economic analysis demanded by the descriptor and the integration node.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe cost-benefit scaffold needs to be explicit: costs = what you give up (money, time, other options); benefits = what you gain (money, time, satisfaction, community good). Students need the non-financial categories named and modelled before applying independently.
