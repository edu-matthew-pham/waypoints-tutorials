Identifying costs and benefits

AC9HE8S04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 8 Economics and Business · Skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Economics and Business: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify the potential costs and benefits of a decision, including non-financial dimensions, and explain what is foregone when a choice is made.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least one cost and one benefit of a specific economic or business decision
  • Explains what is foregone (opportunity cost) when the decision is made

Prior knowledge

Students can develop a response to an economic and business issue identifying potential costs and benefits (AC9HE7S04). At Year 8, the expectation develops toward responses that combine known knowledge with new ideas and that require explicit identification and description of costs and benefits.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least one potential cost and one potential benefit of a specific economic or business decision, and explain what is foregone (opportunity cost) when that decision is made.
Typical
Identify and describe the potential costs and benefits of an economic or business decision — including financial and non-financial dimensions. Explain that making a decision involves choice and foregoing an alternative want, using appropriate examples (e.g. doing casual work involves benefits such as money earned and costs such as less certainty about work hours).
Wider
  • Practise identifying costs and benefits from the perspectives of multiple stakeholders affected by the same decision — distinguishing private costs/benefits (to the decision-maker) from social costs/benefits (to the broader community)
  • Investigate how businesses systematically conduct cost-benefit analysis before major decisions, including how they handle costs and benefits that are hard to quantify (e.g. environmental impact, reputational risk)
  • Research a government policy decision (e.g. a new tax, infrastructure investment) and evaluate the costs and benefits identified in the official policy rationale versus critiques of that analysis

Hinge

Costs and benefits identification is the analytical foundation of the response. Students who conflate financial and non-financial costs, or who omit opportunity cost, will produce incomplete responses that do not meet the descriptor's demand for explicit cost-benefit reasoning.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents often list benefits without matching them with genuine costs, or identify only financial dimensions. Structured cost-benefit scaffolds — requiring equal treatment of each side and explicit consideration of opportunity cost — build balanced analysis before it is applied in a full response.
