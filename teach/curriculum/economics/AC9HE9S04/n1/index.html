Applying cost-benefit analysis to consumer and financial decisions

AC9HE9S04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 9 Economics and Business · Skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Economics and Business: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can apply cost-benefit analysis to consumer and financial decision-making, including estimating costs and considering ability to pay.

Success criteria

  • Estimates costs (short-term and long-term) for at least two options in a decision
  • Identifies the benefits associated with each option
  • Compares options using the cost-benefit analysis and explains which option it supports

Prior knowledge

From Y8 AC9HE8S04 (direct predecessor): develop a response to an economic and business issue, identifying potential costs and benefits. Students can develop a response with costs and benefits identified. Y9 adds two moves: (1) evaluating the response — not just developing it — and (2) using cost-benefit analysis or explicit criteria to decide on a course of action. The 'evaluate' and 'decide' moves are both new additions to the Y8 capability.

Width

Minimum
Apply cost-benefit analysis to one consumer or financial decision — estimating monthly and annual costs and comparing them against identified benefits.
Typical
Apply strategies to mitigate risk in consumer and financial decision-making using cost-benefit analysis — applying the elaboration example (estimating monthly and annual costs of each option and considering ability to pay). For each option: estimate short-term and long-term costs; identify direct and indirect benefits; assess affordability (ability to pay without financial stress); and explain which option the analysis supports and why.
Wider
  • Apply cost-benefit analysis to a financial decision that affects multiple household members (e.g. taking on a mortgage vs continuing to rent) — identifying costs and benefits from each member's perspective and explaining how the decision is made when perspectives differ
  • Apply cost-benefit analysis to a business decision where some costs and benefits are quantifiable and others are not (e.g. reputational benefit, environmental cost) — explaining how non-quantifiable factors should be weighted
  • Compare how cost-benefit analysis would be conducted differently for a First Nations community enterprise versus a privately-owned business, given different goals and community obligations

Hinge

Cost-benefit analysis is the analytical method underpinning decision justification in this descriptor. Students who treat CBA as 'list good and bad things' rather than as a structured comparative analysis cannot produce the 'justify using criteria' outputs required in later nodes or in AC9HE9S05 arguments.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkCBA requires a structured protocol: identify options → estimate costs (short and long term) → identify benefits → assess affordability → compare and justify. Without this structure, students produce unstructured pros-and-cons lists rather than analysis that can be used to justify a decision.
