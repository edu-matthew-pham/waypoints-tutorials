Types of work

AC9HE7K04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 7 Economics and Business · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Economics and Business: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify and describe different types of work — full-time, part-time, casual, at home, paid, unpaid, volunteer — and explain that not all work is formally recognised or remunerated.

Success criteria

  • Names at least four distinct types of work with an example of each
  • Distinguishes between paid and unpaid work and explains why both contribute to individuals and communities

Prior knowledge

Students have explored consumer choices and personal financial decision-making (AC9HS6K08). They have encountered the idea that people earn income but have not formally examined the full range of work types, income sources, or the social and identity dimensions of work — including unpaid, volunteer and First Nations community contributions.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least four types of work with a concrete example of each; distinguish between paid and unpaid work.
Typical
Identify and describe the full range of work types — full-time, part-time, casual, at home, paid, unpaid, unrecognised and volunteer; explain that unpaid and volunteer work contributes to community even when not formally recognised; use examples from students' own communities.
Wider
  • Investigate how First Nations Australians' management of Country/Place constitutes a form of work — including cultural maintenance, land management and knowledge transmission — that may not be captured in formal employment data
  • Examine how unpaid domestic work is distributed and valued differently across households, and discuss whether it should be counted in national economic measures
  • Explore how volunteer work sustains community organisations and services — interview a local volunteer or examine a community organisation's reliance on non-paid contributions

Hinge

Students who only recognise formal paid employment as 'work' will be unable to explain the full range of income sources in the next node, and will miss the social and identity contributions of work that are central to the descriptor's scope. The narrow view also prevents engagement with First Nations and community work contexts.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepExpanding beyond wage work to include unpaid, volunteer and cultural custodianship work requires multiple concrete examples from different community contexts. Each new context adds a genuinely distinct category to students' understanding of what 'work' encompasses.
