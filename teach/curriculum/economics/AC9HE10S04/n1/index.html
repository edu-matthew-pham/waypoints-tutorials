What cost-benefit analysis involves

AC9HE10S04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 5 · Year 10 Economics and Business · Skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Economics and Business: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain what cost-benefit analysis involves and identify the types of costs and benefits relevant to economic and business decisions.

Success criteria

  • Explains what cost-benefit analysis is and how it differs from a simple list of pros and cons
  • Identifies at least two types of costs and two types of benefits relevant to a named decision

Prior knowledge

Students can develop and evaluate responses to economic and business issues using cost-benefit analysis or criteria at Year 9 level (AC9HE9S04). They have practised identifying and weighing costs and benefits to justify a decision, and have used criteria-based evaluation in business contexts. Y10 builds on this with more complex financial calculations, multi-variable cost-benefit analysis, and the explicit use of opportunity cost reasoning.

Width

Minimum
Explain what cost-benefit analysis is and identify at least two types of costs and two types of benefits that might be considered in a financial decision.
Typical
Explain cost-benefit analysis as a structured approach to comparing the financial and non-financial costs and benefits of a course of action; identify types of costs (financial, opportunity, social, environmental) and benefits (financial returns, non-financial gains, social value) relevant to economic and business decisions.
Wider
  • Investigate how governments use formal cost-benefit analysis in infrastructure or policy decisions and evaluate the challenges of assigning monetary values to non-financial costs and benefits
  • Compare cost-benefit analysis with criteria-based evaluation (e.g. using efficiency, profitability or equity as criteria) and explain when each approach is more appropriate
  • Examine how the same decision can produce very different cost-benefit outcomes depending on who bears the costs and who receives the benefits

Hinge

Students who treat cost-benefit analysis as a simple list of pros and cons — without understanding that costs and benefits must be identified systematically, valued and compared — cannot produce the structured evaluation the descriptor requires.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkMake the structure of CBA explicit: identify all relevant costs and benefits → assign relative weight or value → compare → conclude. The systematic nature of the method is what distinguishes it from informal pros-and-cons thinking.
