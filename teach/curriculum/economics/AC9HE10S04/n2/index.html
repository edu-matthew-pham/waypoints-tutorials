Opportunity cost

AC9HE10S04 n2 · waypoint 2 of 5 · Year 10 Economics and Business · Skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Economics and Business: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify the opportunity cost in a decision and explain how it affects the evaluation of a course of action.

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Correctly defines opportunity cost in own words
  • Identifies the opportunity cost in a named decision and explains how it should factor into the evaluation

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: What cost-benefit analysis involves.

Width

Minimum
Define opportunity cost and give one example of an opportunity cost in a consumer or business decision.
Typical
Explain opportunity cost as the value of the next-best alternative foregone; apply opportunity cost reasoning to consumer and business decisions — for example, employing more staff instead of upskilling existing staff to improve productivity — and explain how opportunity cost affects the evaluation of a choice.
Wider
  • Apply opportunity cost reasoning to a government policy decision (e.g. investing in public transport vs road infrastructure) and evaluate whether the opportunity cost is acknowledged in public debate
  • Investigate how sunk cost fallacy can distort decision-making by causing people to ignore opportunity costs in favour of costs already committed
  • Compare how a business and an individual household would calculate the opportunity cost of the same decision differently, and explain why

Hinge

Opportunity cost is the distinct economic reasoning tool in this descriptor. Without it, student evaluation reduces to identifying immediate financial costs and misses the comparative logic that defines economic decision analysis.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Consolidation: more practise steps, short feedback loopsOpportunity cost reasoning needs to become automatic — practise applying it to multiple contexts (consumer, business, government) until students routinely ask 'what is being given up?' rather than only 'what does this cost?'.
