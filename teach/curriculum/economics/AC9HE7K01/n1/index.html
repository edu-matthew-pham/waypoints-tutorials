Needs, wants and unlimited desire

AC9HE7K01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Year 7 Economics and Business · Knowledge and understanding · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Economics and Business: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can distinguish between needs and wants, and explain why human wants are effectively unlimited.

Lessons on this site

  • Needs, wants and why wanting never stops

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two needs and two wants, distinguishing them with a clear reason
  • Explains why human wants are unlimited, using a concrete personal or community example

Prior knowledge

Students have explored influences on consumer choices and strategies for making informed personal consumer and financial choices (AC9HS6K08). They can identify that people make decisions about spending and saving but have not formally engaged with the economic concepts of scarcity, resource allocation, or opportunity cost.

Width

Minimum
Identify personal examples of needs and wants; explain that wants are unlimited and cannot all be satisfied.
Typical
Distinguish needs from wants across personal, household and community contexts; explain the concept of unlimited wants using concrete local examples; identify that a community's combined wants always exceed available resources.
Wider
  • Compare how needs and wants differ across cultural or socioeconomic contexts; for example, investigate how a remote First Nations community defines community needs versus wants and how these are prioritised
  • Examine how advertising and social influence expand perceived wants over time, using examples from contemporary media
  • Investigate how definitions of 'need' shift over time as societies develop; for example, whether internet access is now a need

Hinge

If students do not distinguish needs from wants and grasp that wants are unlimited, the concept of scarcity and the resulting need for allocation choices will not make sense. Scarcity is only meaningful when unlimited wants collide with finite resources — students who blur needs and wants will treat resource limits as a solvable supply problem rather than a structural economic condition.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents often conflate needs and wants or assume that wants could eventually all be met with enough production. Invest in the reasoning scaffold: why unlimited wants are a permanent structural feature of economic life, not a temporary shortage problem.
Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepBroader contexts — community, cultural, historical — reveal that unlimited wants are not a personal weakness but a universal economic condition, strengthening the conceptual foundation for scarcity.
