← AC9S7H03 Science responses impact society

Ethical, environmental, social and economic considerations

AC9S7H03 n3 · waypoint 3 of 4 · Years 7–8 Science · Human endeavour

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explore ethical, environmental, social and economic considerations associated with a proposed scientific response

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two types of consideration relevant to a specific response
  • Explains why each consideration matters for that response
  • Distinguishes between the consideration types rather than conflating them

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Impacts can be positive and negative.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least two types of consideration (from ethical, environmental, social, economic) relevant to a proposed scientific response; explain why each matters
Typical
Analyse a proposed scientific response across all four consideration types; explain why stakeholders may hold different views depending on which consideration they prioritise
Wider
  • Investigate the ethical, environmental, social and economic implications of others using First Nations Australians' knowledges of medicinal properties of endemic plants
  • Investigate ethical and economic implications of cross-cultural partnerships that build on First Nations Australians' land management techniques
  • Research how properties of gases were used in WWI gas warfare and the subsequent development of international arms control treaties — examine the ethical implications

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents commonly conflate the four consideration types or address only one (usually environmental). Examining the same response through all four lenses across different contexts — medicinal plants, land management, gas warfare — helps students distinguish the considerations and understand why different stakeholders prioritise differently. Breadth across contexts is what builds the four-lens thinking habit.
Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe four-lens analysis requires an explicit reasoning scaffold — students need to be supported in applying each consideration type systematically rather than defaulting to whichever lens feels most prominent. Without structure, students describe one consideration in depth and mention others superficially.
