← AC9S4U04 Properties of materials

Properties determine material selection

AC9S4U04 n3 · waypoint 3 of 4 · Year 4 Science · Chemical

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain why a specific material is used for a particular purpose by connecting one or more of its properties to the demands of that use.

Lessons on this site

Success criteria

  • Names the relevant property and explains how that property meets the demands of the use for a given material-object pair
  • Explains why a different material would be less suitable for the same use (naming the missing property)

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Material classes: fibres, metals, glass, plastics.

Width

Minimum
Students explain the selection of a material for a given object by naming the relevant property and explaining why it matters for that use.
Typical
Students explain how the combination of properties of a material determines its suitability for a specific use, and recognise that no material is universally 'best' — selection depends on the demands of the use.
Wider
  • Examine familiar objects made from combinations of materials (shoes: rubber sole, leather/synthetic upper, foam insole) and explain the property rationale for each component
  • Investigate how knowledge of material properties has influenced people's decisions about plastic use and disposal (AC9S4H02)
  • Research a case where material selection failed — a product that used the wrong material for its purpose — and analyse the property mismatch

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents frequently explain material selection with generic justifications ('glass is used for windows because it's see-through') without articulating the property-to-demand connection ('transparency allows light in while the hardness and weather resistance keep the elements out'). Making the reasoning chain explicit — demand → required property → material that has that property — produces better explanatory quality.
