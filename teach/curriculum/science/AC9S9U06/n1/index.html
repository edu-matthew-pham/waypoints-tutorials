The nuclear model of the atom — protons, neutrons and electrons

AC9S9U06 n1 · waypoint 1 of 5 · Year 9 Science · Chemical · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can describe the structure of the nuclear atom in terms of protons, neutrons and electrons, and use atomic number and mass number to describe specific atoms.

Lessons on this site

  • What's inside an atom?

Success criteria

  • Describes the nuclear atom: protons and neutrons in the nucleus, electrons in shells
  • Uses atomic number and mass number to determine proton, neutron and electron counts
  • Describes the relative mass and charge of protons, neutrons and electrons

Prior knowledge

Students can use particle theory to explain physical properties of substances (AC9S7U05) and classify matter as elements, compounds and mixtures using symbols and formulas (AC9S8U06). They have a functional particle model but it is the simple undifferentiated particle model — particles have no internal structure. They have not yet encountered subatomic particles, the historical development of atomic models, or the concept of nuclear instability and radioactive decay.

Width

Minimum
Students describe the nucleus as containing protons and neutrons, with electrons surrounding it, and use atomic number to identify an element.
Typical
Students describe the relative mass and charge of protons, neutrons and electrons; explain that atomic number equals proton number and determines the element's identity; explain that mass number equals protons plus neutrons; describe electrons as occupying shells around the nucleus; connect to the prior undifferentiated particle model as a simplification that is now being replaced.
Wider
  • Compare the mass and charge of subatomic particles quantitatively — what does the near-zero mass of the electron reveal about where most atomic mass is located?
  • Examine how the discovery of the nucleus changed scientific understanding — what did Rutherford's gold foil experiment show that Thomson's plum pudding model could not explain?

Hinge

The nuclear model is the structural foundation all subsequent nodes require. Students who cannot correctly assign protons, neutrons and electrons to an atom from its atomic and mass numbers cannot define isotopes in N2, understand why some isotopes are unstable in N3, or follow decay sequences in N4.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents enter with a functional but structure-free particle model. Introducing internal atomic structure requires the prior model to be explicitly extended — particles now have constituent parts. The historical transition from Thomson to Rutherford's model is the elaboration's evidence-based framing for this conceptual shift.
