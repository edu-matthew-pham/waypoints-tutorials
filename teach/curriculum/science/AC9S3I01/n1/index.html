← AC9S3I01 Questioning and predicting

Questions target patterns and relationships

AC9S3I01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Years 3–4 Science · Inquiry · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can pose investigable questions that go beyond naming or describing — targeting a pattern (what changes when X changes?) or relationship (how does X affect Y?) in an observed phenomenon.

Success criteria

  • Poses a question that targets a relationship or pattern (not just a description) for a given observation
  • Explains what observable pattern or relationship the question is designed to explore

Prior knowledge

Students can pose simple questions and make predictions based on their experiences (AC9S1I01, 1-2 band). They have not yet posed questions that target patterns or relationships specifically, or made predictions that are explicitly grounded in prior observations rather than intuition.

Width

Minimum
Students reframe a descriptive question into a relationship question for a given observation context, and explain what distinguishes the two.
Typical
Students pose their own relationship questions for two different science contexts (biological, physical or materials-based) and explain what observable pattern or relationship each question targets.
Wider
  • Examine how ecologists pose questions about population patterns — e.g. 'What happens to prey populations when predators are removed?' — and compare to descriptive questions about the same topic
  • Explore how First Nations Australians pose questions about patterns in Country — observing landscape and seasonal indicators to predict resource availability
  • Investigate the difference between a question that can be tested in a classroom and one that requires field observation or a longer time scale

Hinge

A relationship question is the prerequisite input to investigation planning (AC9S3I02). Students who cannot distinguish relationship questions from descriptive questions will design descriptive observation tasks rather than fair tests, collapsing the planning scaffold in the next standard.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe shift from descriptive ('what is this?') to relationship ('how does X affect Y?') questioning is non-obvious and requires explicit modelling. Students need to see the structural difference between question types before they can generate relationship questions independently. Without this scaffold, students default to description questions that do not guide investigation design.
