← AC9S3I04 Processing and representing data

Visual and physical models represent relationships

AC9S3I04 n3 · waypoint 3 of 3 · Years 3–4 Science · Inquiry

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can construct or use a visual or physical model to represent a scientific relationship, explain what the model shows, and identify its limitations.

Success criteria

  • Constructs or interprets a model and explains what relationship or process it represents
  • Identifies at least one element of the model and explains what it stands for in the real phenomenon
  • Describes at least one way the model simplifies or differs from the real phenomenon

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Column graphs display and compare quantities.

Width

Minimum
Students construct or use a given visual or physical model (food chain diagram, force arrow diagram, water cycle model) and explain what relationship or process it represents.
Typical
Students construct a model for a relationship from Y4 science content, explain what each element of the model represents, and describe at least one thing the model simplifies or leaves out.
Wider
  • Use force arrows to model forces on objects in a range of scenarios — stationary, moving, floating — and discuss how the model shows the relationship between forces
  • Use a virtual food chain simulation to explore the effects of changing producer or consumer numbers, comparing model predictions with real-world examples
  • Evaluate a physical model of the water cycle (water cycle in a bag, terrarium) — what does it show accurately, and what does it simplify?

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents tend to treat models as pictures rather than as representations of relationships — they describe what the model looks like rather than what it shows about how things interact. Encountering models across varied science contexts (food chains, forces, water cycle) helps students develop a more general understanding of what models are for and how to read them as representations of relationships.
