Texts have purpose, audience and appropriate vocabulary

AC9S3I06 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 3–4 Science · Inquiry · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify the purpose of a science text, its intended audience, and how purpose and audience influence the vocabulary and structure of the text.

Success criteria

  • Identifies the purpose and audience of a given science text
  • Explains at least one way the text's vocabulary or structure is shaped by its purpose or audience

Prior knowledge

Students can share questions, predictions, observations and ideas with others in simple forms (AC9S1I06, 1-2 band). They have not yet written or created texts that are explicitly structured for a purpose and audience, or used scientific vocabulary deliberately for communicative effect.

Width

Minimum
Students identify the purpose and audience of a given science text and explain at least one way the text is shaped by those factors.
Typical
Students compare two texts communicating similar science content for different purposes or audiences (e.g. an informative report vs a persuasive poster) and identify structural and vocabulary differences.
Wider
  • Examine how First Nations Australians share information about water sources or ecological knowledge using different forms — oral, visual, spatial — and discuss what each form communicates that others don't
  • Explore a range of text forms used in science communication (journal abstract, news article, infographic, documentary) and identify what purpose and audience each serves
  • Discuss how digital tools change what is possible in science communication — interactivity, multimedia, global reach

Hinge

Purpose-audience awareness is the prerequisite for deliberate text construction in Node 3. Students who have not internalised how purpose and audience shape vocabulary and structure default to a single writing style regardless of the task, making Node 3's selection-and-creation demand impossible to meet.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe purpose-audience-vocabulary relationship is non-obvious. Students default to a single writing style regardless of purpose or audience without scaffolding. Making the relationship explicit (who will read this → what do they need to understand → what vocabulary suits them → what structure helps them?) supports more deliberate text construction.
