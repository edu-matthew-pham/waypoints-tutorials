Validity and reproducibility as method criteria

AC9S9I02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 9–10 Science · Inquiry · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain what validity and reproducibility mean as criteria for a scientific investigation method, and identify whether a described method satisfies each criterion.

Success criteria

  • Can define validity and reproducibility with precision — not just 'it works' and 'you can do it again'.
  • Can identify a specific feature of a method that threatens validity or reproducibility.
  • Does not conflate the two criteria — can explain how a method could be reproducible but not valid.

Prior knowledge

Students can plan and conduct investigations to test predictions, identifying variables and describing methods (AC9S7I02). They have not yet explicitly focused on validity and reproducibility as criteria for method quality, or systematically identified and controlled sources of error as part of planning.

Width

Minimum
Define validity (the method measures what it claims to measure) and reproducibility (the method can be repeated by others to obtain consistent results), and apply one criterion to evaluate a described method.
Typical
Define both validity and reproducibility, explain why each matters for scientific investigation, and evaluate a described method against both criteria — identifying specific features of the method that support or undermine each.
Wider
  • Determine the reproducibility of a specific field investigation design — e.g. a survey of traffic passing a school — examining what features of the method determine whether another person running it would get the same result.
  • Distinguish internal validity (are the results due to the independent variable, not something else?) from external validity (can the results be generalised?) as two dimensions of validity relevant at Year 9.

Hinge

Students who treat validity and reproducibility as generic 'good science' terms without specific meaning cannot systematically identify sources of error (Node 2) or evaluate their own method quality. These criteria are the framework for all subsequent investigation planning — without them, error identification becomes arbitrary.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkValidity and reproducibility need to be operationalised as questions students ask of a method: 'Is the measurement capturing what we want to measure, or is it capturing something else?' and 'If someone else ran this, what might they do differently that would produce a different result?' These question structures need explicit modelling.
