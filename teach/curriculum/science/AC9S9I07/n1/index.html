Constructing multi-source arguments

AC9S9I07 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 9–10 Science · Inquiry · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can construct an argument that draws on multiple sources of evidence, evaluating the credibility of each source and explaining how the evidence collectively supports the conclusion.

Success criteria

  • Can construct an argument using at least two sources with explicit credibility evaluation.
  • Can explain how the evidence collectively supports the conclusion — not just list the sources.
  • Does not construct an argument from a single source — uses multiple independent sources.

Prior knowledge

Students can construct evidence-based arguments (AC9S7I07). They have not yet applied ethical considerations and cultural protocols specifically to secondary data use, or constructed arguments based on analysis of multiple sources — including evaluation of source credibility as part of the argument.

Width

Minimum
Construct an argument for one claim using evidence from at least two sources, identify the credibility basis for each source, and explain how the two sources together support (or complicate) the conclusion.
Typical
Construct an argument based on multiple sources — including at least one primary and one secondary source — evaluating the credibility, relevance and consistency of evidence, and explaining how the combined evidence supports the conclusion more strongly than any single source would.
Wider
  • Examine secondary data to determine the credibility of the source and the validity and reproducibility of the data — applying AC9S9I06 validity criteria to the evidence selection process.
  • Identify multiple sources of evidence consistent with a scientific claim (e.g. the effectiveness of a vaccine) and explain why consistency across independent sources strengthens the argument.
  • Research methods used by scientists in studies reported in media — evaluating the validity of headlines against the original evidence to build critical media literacy as an argument skill.

Hinge

Students who construct arguments from a single source — or who select evidence without evaluating credibility — cannot distinguish a well-supported argument from an opinion. The multi-source, credibility-evaluated argument structure is the capstone inquiry skill; weakness here undermines the argument quality regardless of source content.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe structure of a multi-source argument needs explicit scaffolding: 'Claim → Evidence 1 (credibility basis, how it supports) → Evidence 2 (credibility basis, how it supports) → how they work together.' Students often produce description of sources rather than an argument that uses them. Model the distinction.
