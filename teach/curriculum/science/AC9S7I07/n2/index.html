Constructing evidence-based arguments

AC9S7I07 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Years 7–8 Science · Inquiry

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can construct an argument supported by evidence and reasoning to support a conclusion or evaluate a claim

Success criteria

  • Constructs an argument that links evidence to a conclusion through reasoning
  • The reasoning explains why the evidence supports the conclusion
  • Does not simply list evidence without connecting it to the conclusion

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Drawing conclusions from evidence.

Width

Minimum
Construct an argument that links evidence to a conclusion with reasoning; evaluate one claim by assessing the evidence behind it
Typical
Construct arguments supported by evidence and reasoning to support or reject a hypothesis; evaluate the credibility of sources; assess whether cited evidence is sufficient to support a claim
Wider
  • Evaluate a contested scientific claim by systematically assessing the credibility, relevance and sufficiency of evidence from multiple sources
  • Investigate cultural, historical and archaeological evidence used in the scientific debate about the role of early First Nations Australians in the extinction of Australian megafauna
  • Construct competing arguments for and against a claim and evaluate which is better supported

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents commonly state conclusions and list evidence without connecting them through reasoning — the 'because' is missing. Constructing arguments across different science content areas (ecology, forces, classification, megafauna extinction) helps students practise the evidence-reasoning-conclusion structure rather than relying on the familiarity of one context to carry the argument.
