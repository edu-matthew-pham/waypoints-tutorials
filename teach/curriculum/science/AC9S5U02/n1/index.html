Weathering breaks down rocks in place

AC9S5U02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 5 Science · Earth and space · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can distinguish weathering from erosion — explaining that weathering breaks down rock material in place through mechanical and biological means — and identify agents of weathering with examples.

Success criteria

  • Explains that weathering breaks down rock material in place without moving it
  • Names at least two weathering agents and explains the mechanism by which each breaks down rock

Prior knowledge

Students can compare observable properties of soils and rocks and explain their importance as Earth resources (AC9S3U02). They have not yet distinguished weathering from erosion, examined the agents driving each, or connected these processes to landscape change over time.

Width

Minimum
Students explain that weathering breaks down rock material in place, name at least two types of weathering (mechanical, biological) and give one example of each.
Typical
Students describe mechanical weathering (wind abrasion, freeze-thaw cycles, thermal expansion) and biological weathering (plant roots, organisms), explain how each breaks down rock without moving the material, and distinguish this from erosion which involves movement.
Wider
  • Investigate how freeze-thaw weathering operates by modelling water expanding in cracks — examining the mechanical force that splits rock without chemical change
  • Examine how biological weathering by tree roots and organisms operates at a timescale invisible to observation but visible in landscape features
  • Connect weathering to rock cycle processes (AC9S8U04) — examining how weathered material becomes sediment in the rock cycle

Hinge

The weathering/erosion distinction is definitionally prerequisite for Node 2 — students who conflate the two processes cannot accurately describe the erosion → transportation → deposition sequence, and will describe all surface change as 'erosion'. This conflation is a known persistent misconception that causes systematic errors across N2 and N3.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents consistently conflate weathering and erosion, treating them as synonyms or as sequential steps in the same process. The distinction — weathering breaks down in place, erosion moves material — is fundamental to understanding how landscapes change and must be established explicitly before students can accurately describe the full sequence.
