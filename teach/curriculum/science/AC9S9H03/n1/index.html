Adoption factors: the concept

AC9S9H03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Years 9–10 Science · Human endeavour · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain that scientific knowledge being valid or published is not sufficient for broad societal adoption, and identify the types of factors that affect whether adoption occurs.

Success criteria

  • Can explain why scientific validity alone is insufficient for societal adoption.
  • Can identify at least two distinct categories of adoption factors.
  • Does not treat 'getting the word out' as the only obstacle — recognises structural, economic and cultural factors.

Prior knowledge

Students can explain how scientific knowledge and its applications affect people's lives (AC9S7H03) and examine how scientific communication shapes policy (AC9S7H04). They have not yet systematically analysed the factors that determine whether scientific knowledge or practices are widely adopted — moving from describing the relationship between science and society to analysing the conditions for adoption.

Width

Minimum
Explain that valid scientific knowledge does not automatically get adopted by society, and identify at least two categories of factors that influence adoption (e.g. economic cost, accessibility, cultural values, government support).
Typical
Identify multiple categories of adoption factors across scientific, economic, social and political dimensions, and explain that adoption typically requires favourable conditions across several factors simultaneously — not just evidence quality alone.
Wider
  • Examine why citizen science projects attract public participation — connecting community engagement to the accessibility and trust factors in adoption.
  • Consider the adoption gap: why does significant time often pass between scientific validation and widespread adoption, and what factors close or widen this gap?

Hinge

Students who treat scientific validity as the only adoption factor cannot meaningfully analyse the cases at Node 2 — where economically or culturally feasible practices get adopted despite mixed evidence, or where well-evidenced practices face resistance due to other factors. The multi-factor framing is prerequisite for the analysis.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe claim that 'valid science is not enough' runs counter to a naive view of how knowledge works. Scaffold with a concrete counter-example first: 'We have known for decades that smoking causes cancer — why did it take so long for smoking rates to fall?' The reasoning from this case generates the factor categories students need.
