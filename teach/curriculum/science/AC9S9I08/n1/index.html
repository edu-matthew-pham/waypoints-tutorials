Purpose, audience and communication choices

AC9S9I08 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 9–10 Science · Inquiry · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can analyse scientific texts for different purposes and audiences and explain how the author's choices — of content, language and text features — reflect the purpose and audience.

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Can identify at least two communication choices in a text and connect each to the text's purpose or audience.
  • Can explain why different purposes or audiences require different content, language or structure.
  • Does not treat scientific communication as one-size-fits-all.

Prior knowledge

Students can write and communicate scientific ideas and findings (AC9S7I08). They have not yet explicitly analysed communication choices for different purposes and audiences, or written scientific reports that include validity and reproducibility discussion as a required structural component.

Width

Minimum
Compare two scientific texts addressing similar topics for different audiences (e.g. a journal abstract and a magazine article on the same research) and identify at least two differences in content selection or language use that reflect the different audiences.
Typical
Analyse two or three texts across different purposes (report, media release, community communication) and audiences (specialist, general public, specific community), identifying how content selection, language and text features shift for each combination — and explaining the rationale for each choice.
Wider
  • Examine how culturally responsive communication differs from standard scientific communication — analysing specific texts that successfully navigate cultural sensitivity (e.g. communicating First Nations Australians' knowledges without offensive narratives or misattribution).
  • Compare scientific texts addressing similar topics for different purposes or audiences to build the analytical vocabulary for the communication choices students will make in their own texts.

Hinge

Students who do not understand that communication choices are purpose- and audience-driven produce texts that are appropriate for one context when another is required. This is the planning framework for all subsequent communication tasks. Without it, students default to a single text type regardless of the brief.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkCommunication analysis requires an explicit framework: 'Who is the audience? What do they already know? What is the purpose — to inform, persuade, report? What content does that purpose require? What language is appropriate for that audience?' Students need this framework as a checklist before they analyse or produce texts.
