Investigable vs non-investigable questions

AC9S7I01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 7–8 Science · Inquiry

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can distinguish investigable from non-investigable questions and explain what makes a question investigable

Success criteria

  • Correctly classifies at least 3 questions as investigable or non-investigable
  • States at least two features that make a question investigable
  • Does not confuse 'interesting' with 'investigable'

Prior knowledge

Students can pose investigable questions to identify patterns and test relationships and make predictions based on experiences (AC9S5I01). They have not yet developed hypotheses with explicit variable-outcome-reasoning structure or generated questions to explore scientific models.

Width

Minimum
Identify whether a given question is investigable or not; state at least two features that make a question investigable
Typical
Discuss features of investigable and non-investigable questions; evaluate their own and others' questions for investigability; refine a non-investigable question into an investigable one
Wider
  • Limited width — distinguishing investigable from non-investigable questions is a judgement skill that does not vary meaningfully across contexts. More question sets provide practice volume, not genuine breadth. Extensions such as evaluating questions from First Nations ecological knowledge contexts add cultural dimension but don't change the underlying criteria being practised.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Consolidation: more practise steps, short feedback loopsDistinguishing investigable from non-investigable questions is a judgement skill that students commonly get wrong in both directions — either accepting untestable questions or rejecting testable ones. The concept does not vary meaningfully across contexts; what helps is repeated practice classifying and refining questions until the criteria become automatic.
Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly apply the label 'investigable' or 'not investigable' without being able to articulate why. Scaffolding the reasoning — what feature of this question makes it testable or untestable, and what would need to change — builds the explicit criteria that consolidation practice then reinforces.
