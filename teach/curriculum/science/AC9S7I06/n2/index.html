Evaluating methods and assumptions

AC9S7I06 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Years 7–8 Science · Inquiry

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can evaluate an investigation method by identifying assumptions about controlled variables and assessing whether the method tests the hypothesis

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least one assumption about a controlled variable
  • Explains why the assumption matters for the investigation's validity
  • Assesses whether the method tests the stated hypothesis

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Identifying sources of error.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least one assumption in a given method about a variable that should be controlled; explain why the assumption matters
Typical
Evaluate a method by identifying assumptions about controlled variables; suggest improvements with reasoning; assess whether the method adequately tests the hypothesis
Wider
  • Evaluate a method where an assumption about a controlled variable is likely wrong and explain the consequences for results
  • Compare two methods for testing the same hypothesis and evaluate which is more rigorous
  • Analyse conclusions and claims to identify facts or premises taken for granted and evaluate their relevance

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents commonly evaluate methods by checking procedural steps rather than examining underlying assumptions. The misconception that a method is 'good' if the steps are followed correctly persists unless students see — across multiple investigation contexts — that hidden assumptions can invalidate even well-executed procedures. Different investigation types surface different categories of assumption.
Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkIdentifying an assumption requires students to reason backward from the method — 'for this method to work, what must be true about the controlled variables?' — which is a non-obvious reasoning move. Explicit scaffolding of this backward reasoning helps students find assumptions rather than just evaluate ones they're told about.
