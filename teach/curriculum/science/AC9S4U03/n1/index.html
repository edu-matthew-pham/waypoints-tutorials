Forces are exerted by one object on another

AC9S4U03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 5 · Year 4 Science · Physical · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain that a force is an interaction between two objects — one exerting, one receiving — and distinguish between contact forces (objects touching) and non-contact forces (acting at a distance).

Lessons on this site

  • Pushes and pulls

Success criteria

  • Identifies the two objects involved in a force interaction for a given example
  • Distinguishes between a contact force example and a non-contact force example with justification

Prior knowledge

Students can describe pushes and pulls in terms of strength and direction and predict their effects on motion and shape (AC9S1U03). They have not yet encountered named force types (friction, gravity, magnetism) or the concept that forces are exerted by one object on another, including at a distance.

Width

Minimum
Students identify the two objects involved in a force interaction for at least two examples, and distinguish between contact and non-contact examples.
Typical
Students explain that forces require an interaction between objects and that some forces (gravity, magnetism) act without physical contact, using examples from their experience.
Wider
  • Investigate traditional First Nations Australians' toys and games and identify the forces involved in each — connecting cultural artefacts to the force concept
  • Explore the idea that even 'stationary' objects have forces acting on them (a book on a table — gravity pulling down, table pushing up) — introducing force balance informally
  • Examine how force arrows are used to show direction and magnitude, and draw force arrow diagrams for simple scenarios

Hinge

The two-object interaction framing is the conceptual prerequisite for correctly identifying friction, gravity and magnetism in Nodes 2–4. Students who attribute force to one object ('the magnet pulls') without identifying the receiving object cannot accurately describe force pairs and will misidentify contact vs non-contact situations throughout the sequence.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe concept that force requires two objects — an exerter and a receiver — is non-obvious to Year 4 students who tend to think of force as a property of one object ('the magnet pulls'). Making the two-object interaction explicit, and distinguishing contact from non-contact, is a necessary conceptual scaffold before students can correctly identify friction, gravity and magnetism as force types.
