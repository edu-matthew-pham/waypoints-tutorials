← AC9S4U03 Forces and motion

Friction slows motion between surfaces

AC9S4U03 n2 · waypoint 2 of 5 · Year 4 Science · Physical

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain that friction is a force that acts between two surfaces in contact, describe its effect on motion (slowing or stopping), and identify factors that affect friction strength.

Success criteria

  • Explains that friction is a force between surfaces and describes its effect on motion
  • Gives one example where friction is helpful and one where it is a problem
  • Identifies at least one factor that affects how strong the friction force is

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Forces are exerted by one object on another.

Width

Minimum
Students explain that friction acts between surfaces and slows or stops moving objects, giving two everyday examples.
Typical
Students explain positive and negative effects of friction in everyday contexts (friction needed for gripping; friction causes wear and slows machines) and identify how surface texture affects friction strength.
Wider
  • Investigate how shoe sole design uses friction principles — examining patterns and materials for different surface conditions
  • Test friction between different surface combinations and represent results in a column graph (connects to AC9S4I04)
  • Research how friction reduction is used in engineering contexts (lubricants, ball bearings, aerodynamics) and examine trade-offs

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents readily identify friction as 'slowing things down' but struggle to see it as a force exerted by one surface on another. They also miss contexts where friction is desirable (braking, gripping, writing). Varied contexts — shoe grip, playground slides, bike brakes, wet roads — help students build a more complete model of friction as a surface interaction with both beneficial and detrimental effects.
