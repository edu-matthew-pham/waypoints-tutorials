← AC9S7U05 Particle theory and properties

Particle arrangement, motion and attraction in each state

AC9S7U05 n2 · waypoint 2 of 5 · Year 7 Science · Chemical · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can describe particle arrangement, motion and attraction for each state and explain observable properties using this model

Lessons on this site

  • Why does a gas fill its container?

Success criteria

  • Describes particle arrangement, motion and attraction for each state
  • Explains why solids hold shape and gases fill containers using particle theory
  • Does not treat particle diagrams as decorative — uses them to support reasoning

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Introducing the particle model.

Width

Minimum
Describe particle arrangement, motion and attraction for each state; explain why solids hold shape and gases fill containers
Typical
Compare particle diagrams for 6 substances — rank by particle spacing and predict relative density; check against data
Wider
  • Relate motion and energy of particles to distances between particles in different states of the same substance
  • Compare attractive forces in solid, liquid and gaseous states and relate to relative position and movement of particles
  • Investigate an anomalous case (e.g. ice floating on water) — explain using particle theory and evaluate whether the basic model needs refinement

Hinge

Students who treat particle diagrams as decorative rather than explanatory cannot apply the model to property explanation in Node 3. The pattern-recognition built across multiple substances in this node is what enables the explanatory move — without it, Node 3 collapses to labelling.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepParticle arrangement is the foundation of all property explanations. Students who treat particle diagrams as decorative rather than explanatory cannot use the model to reason. More substances and state comparisons force students to use diagrams as reasoning tools rather than illustrations — the misconception resolves through breadth of application, not repetition on the same substance.
