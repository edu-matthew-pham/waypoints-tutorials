← AC9S2U03 Physical changes to materials

Physical changes alter shape without changing composition

AC9S2U03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Year 2 Science · Chemical · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain that physically changing a material (bending, twisting, stretching, breaking) changes its shape or size but not what it is made of.

Success criteria

  • Applies at least two physical changes to a material and names each action
  • Explains that the material is still the same substance after a physical change — only its shape or size has changed

Prior knowledge

Students can observe and describe properties of materials (AC9SFU03, F band). They have not yet distinguished between changing the form of a material and changing its composition, or systematically explored multiple physical change actions.

Width

Minimum
Students demonstrate at least two physical changes to a material and explain that the material is still the same substance after the change.
Typical
Students manipulate materials (paper, fabric, playdough, wool); apply multiple physical changes (scrunching, twisting, bending, cutting, tearing, folding); explain that the material is still the same even though its shape has changed; and compare the properties of the original and changed material.
Wider
  • Crush a stick of chalk into powder — comparing properties of the stick and powder and discussing whether it is still the same material
  • Explore how First Nations Australians make physical changes to natural materials to produce objects such as bowls, baskets and fibre crafts — examining purposeful physical transformation
  • Create an 'odd one out' game using samples of the same material changed in different ways plus one different material — applying composition knowledge to a classification challenge

Hinge

N2 requires students to accept that physical action changes form but not composition before they can reason about why different materials respond differently to the same action. Without the composition-vs-form distinction, students conflate different physical outcomes with different materials — blocking the comparison at N2.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly believe that dramatically changing the shape of a material (crushing chalk, folding paper into a plane) creates a different material. Making the composition-vs-form distinction explicit — the shape changes, what it is made of does not — is the essential conceptual scaffold before students can correctly distinguish physical from chemical change in later years.
