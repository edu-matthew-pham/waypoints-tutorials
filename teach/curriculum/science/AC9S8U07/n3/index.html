Observable indicators of chemical change

AC9S8U07 n3 · waypoint 3 of 6 · Year 8 Science · Chemical

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify and explain four observable indicators of chemical change and explain why each suggests a new substance has formed.

Lessons on this site

  • How can you tell a reaction happened?

Success criteria

  • Names four observable indicators of chemical change
  • Explains why each indicator suggests a new substance has formed
  • Distinguishes indicators (observable evidence) from the definition (new substance)

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 2: Chemical change — new substance formation at the particle level.

Width

Minimum
Students name four indicators and describe one observation demonstrating each.
Typical
Students describe: gas production (bubbles, odour — a gaseous compound has formed); precipitate formation (solid appears in solution — an insoluble compound has formed); colour change (new compound has different light absorption); temperature change (energy released or absorbed as bonds break and form); perform or analyse simple reactions to identify indicators; explain that indicators are evidence of the criterion, not the definition — the indicator suggests a new substance may have formed.
Wider
  • Design a reaction investigation that uses multiple indicators to confirm chemical change — which combinations give the strongest evidence?
  • Investigate how chemical change indicators are used in practical testing contexts — soil pH kits, water quality tests, pregnancy tests — and identify which indicator each exploits

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkIndicators are evidence of chemical change, not its definition. Students commonly invert this — classifying a change as chemical because they see a colour change. Making the evidence-criterion relationship explicit (indicator suggests new substance may have formed → confirm by checking properties) prevents systematic misclassification.
