Reversible changes do not produce new substances

AC9S6U04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 6 Science · Chemical · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain that reversible changes — including dissolving and state changes — do not produce new substances, describe how dissolved substances can be recovered, and give examples of reversible changes used in recycling.

Success criteria

  • Explains that dissolving is reversible — the dissolved substance can be recovered
  • Describes at least one method for recovering a dissolved substance
  • Explains that reversible changes do not produce new substances

Prior knowledge

Students can explain changes of state using the particle model (AC9S5U04) and distinguish solids, liquids and gases (AC9S3U04). They have informally encountered dissolving. They have not yet formally compared reversible and irreversible changes or connected irreversibility to the production of new substances.

Width

Minimum
Students explain that dissolving and state changes are reversible, give one method for recovering a dissolved substance, and name one reversible change used in recycling.
Typical
Students explain why dissolving and state changes are reversible (same substances, no new matter produced); describe evaporation, filtration and distillation as methods for reclaiming dissolved substances; explain how state change reversibility enables recycling of glass and metals; and use particle-level reasoning to explain why these changes are reversible.
Wider
  • Investigate how dissolved substances are reclaimed from solutions — comparing evaporation, filtration and crystallisation as recovery methods
  • Examine First Nations Australians' knowledge of reversible processes such as the application of adhesives — exploring how reversibility is a deliberately selected property in traditional material use
  • Explore how reversible changes are exploited in industrial recycling — examining which materials can be returned to their original state and reformed

Hinge

The new-substance criterion is the definitional basis for the reversible/irreversible distinction at Node 3 — students who believe dissolving is irreversible because the substance visually disappears cannot correctly classify changes at N3, and cannot distinguish reversible from irreversible in ambiguous cases like dissolving. The misconception must be resolved here before systematic comparison is possible.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly believe dissolving is irreversible ('the salt is gone') because the change is visually dramatic. The reasoning chain — dissolved particles are still present in the solution → they can be recovered by removing the water → the original substance is unchanged → this is reversible — requires explicit scaffolding before students can consistently distinguish dissolving from chemical change.
