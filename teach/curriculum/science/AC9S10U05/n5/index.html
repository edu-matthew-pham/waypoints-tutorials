Newton's Third Law — action-reaction pairs

AC9S10U05 n5 · waypoint 5 of 8 · Year 10 Science · Physical

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify action-reaction force pairs in a scenario and explain why they do not cancel, despite being equal and opposite.

  • Push the wall, and it pushes you?

Success criteria

  • Identifies the action and reaction forces in a named scenario and states which object each acts on
  • Explains why action-reaction pairs do not cancel (they act on different objects)
  • Distinguishes Third Law pairs from balanced forces acting on the same object

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 4: Newton's Second Law — F = ma.

Width

Minimum
Students identify the action and reaction forces in one scenario (e.g. foot pushing on ground, ground pushing back on foot) and explain that they act on different objects.
Typical
Students identify action-reaction pairs across multiple scenarios (rocket thrust, swimming, jumping); explain why the pairs do not cancel using the 'different objects' principle; distinguish Third Law pairs from balanced forces acting on the same object.
Wider
  • Analyse propulsion in a rocket, jet engine or swimmer using Newton's Third Law — predict direction and relative magnitude of each force pair
  • Investigate a common Third Law misconception: 'the heavier object exerts more force' — design a demonstration that tests this prediction

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe persistent Third Law misconception — that equal and opposite forces cancel and therefore nothing should move — must be addressed by explicitly establishing that action-reaction pairs act on different objects. Scenarios where only one object is being analysed (e.g. a person jumping) help isolate the relevant force before students encounter multi-body analysis.
