Newton's laws of motion

AC9S10U05 · Year 10 Science · Physical

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Descriptor

investigate Newton's laws of motion and quantitatively analyse the relationship between force, mass and acceleration of objects

Prior knowledge

Students can represent and explain how balanced and unbalanced forces, including gravity, relate to changes in an object's motion in terms of mass, magnitude and direction (AC9S7U04). There is a two-year gap in the physical sciences strand (Years 8–9 address energy, not force/motion), so force and motion knowledge has had no curriculum reinforcement for three years. Students carry the Aristotelian misconception that moving objects require a continuous applied force to maintain motion. They have no prior exposure to Newton's named laws, acceleration as a defined quantity, F=ma as a quantitative relationship, or action-reaction pairs.

By the end

Students apply Newton's three laws of motion to analyse object behaviour and quantitatively solve problems using F=ma, including multi-force scenarios, free body diagrams, and real-world applications such as vehicle safety features and sport.

ACARA elaborations

Examples from ACARA that illustrate this standard. They are examples, not a checklist, and belong to the standard as a whole.

  • investigating a moving object to analyse relationships between distance, time, speed, force and acceleration
  • using mathematical representations to quantitatively relate force, speed, acceleration and mass
  • investigating how First Nations Australians achieve increased speed and impact force through spearthrowers and bows
  • modelling how change in net force affects motion and relating to safety features
  • investigating Newton's laws applications in sport
  • constructing a data-supported argument for lower speed limits
  • investigating how driverless vehicles apply Newton's laws to brake in time

Waypoints

  • 1. Force as a vector quantityStudents can represent forces as vectors with both magnitude and direction, and add or subtract forces acting along a single axis to find net force.
  • 2. Newton's First Law — inertia and the Aristotelian misconceptionHingeStudents can state Newton's First Law and explain why a moving object with no net force continues at constant velocity, identifying and correcting the misconception that motion requires a continuous force.
  • 3. Acceleration as a defined vector quantityHingeStudents can define acceleration as the rate of change of velocity, calculate acceleration from velocity and time data, and represent acceleration as a vector with direction.
  • 4. Newton's Second Law — F = maHingeStudents can apply F = ma to calculate force, mass or acceleration in single-force scenarios and explain qualitatively how changing mass or force affects acceleration.
  • 5. Newton's Third Law — action-reaction pairsStudents can identify action-reaction force pairs in a scenario and explain why they do not cancel, despite being equal and opposite.
  • 6. Multi-force analysis and free body diagramsStudents can draw a free body diagram for an object with multiple forces and calculate net force, then apply F = ma to determine acceleration.
  • 7. Quantitative problem solving — integrating all three lawsStudents can solve multi-step force and motion problems that require selecting and applying Newton's laws, using mathematical representations including graphs and algebraic formulas.
  • 8. Real-world applications — Newton's laws in contextStudents can apply Newton's laws to analyse motion in real-world contexts including vehicle safety, sport, and First Nations technologies, and explain design decisions in terms of force, mass and acceleration.
