Newton's First Law — inertia and the Aristotelian misconception

AC9S10U05 n2 · waypoint 2 of 8 · Year 10 Science · Physical · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can state Newton's First Law and explain why a moving object with no net force continues at constant velocity, identifying and correcting the misconception that motion requires a continuous force.

Lessons on this site

  • Does a moving thing need a push?

Success criteria

  • States Newton's First Law correctly
  • Explains why a moving object without net force continues at constant velocity (does not slow down)
  • Identifies friction as the hidden force responsible for observed deceleration in everyday scenarios
  • Corrects the claim that 'a moving object needs a force to keep moving'

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Force as a vector quantity.

Width

Minimum
Students state that an object continues at constant velocity (or remains at rest) when the net force is zero, and explain one everyday scenario where this is counterintuitive (e.g. a sliding hockey puck).
Typical
Students apply Newton's First Law to multiple scenarios including objects at rest and in constant motion; explain inertia as resistance to change in motion; describe how friction explains why objects appear to slow down without a visible opposing force; connect to balanced force concept from Year 7.
Wider
  • Analyse why inertia makes seatbelts necessary — trace what happens to an unrestrained passenger when a car stops suddenly
  • Investigate historical resistance to Galileo's and Newton's ideas — why was the Aristotelian view so persistent, and what evidence overturned it?

Hinge

The Aristotelian misconception — that motion requires a continuous force — directly undermines Newton's Second and Third Laws if unresolved. Students who hold this view interpret F=ma as 'force produces motion' rather than 'net force produces change in motion', causing systematic errors in N4 and N6. Students who cannot separate constant velocity from acceleration also cannot correctly define acceleration in N3.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe Aristotelian misconception is well-documented and persistent — it survives exposure to the correct definition without explicit confrontation. Students need scenarios where the intuitive 'force causes motion' framing produces a wrong prediction, followed by correction using Newton's First Law. Friction as the hidden force explains almost every everyday 'counterexample' students produce.
