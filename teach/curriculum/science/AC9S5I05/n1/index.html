← AC9S5I05 Evaluating

Errors are identified and their effect on findings is explained

AC9S5I05 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Years 5–6 Science · Inquiry · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify specific sources of error in an investigation method (uncontrolled variables, measurement errors, environmental changes) and explain how each error could affect the findings and conclusions.

Success criteria

  • Identifies at least two specific sources of error in an investigation method
  • Explains how each identified error could affect the findings or conclusion

Prior knowledge

Students can compare findings, evaluate fairness and draw data-grounded conclusions (AC9S3I05, 3-4 band). They now extend to recognising specific error sources, selecting evidence from data, and drawing reasoned conclusions — not just grounded ones.

Width

Minimum
Students identify at least two specific sources of error in a given investigation and explain how each could affect the findings.
Typical
Students identify error sources across types (uncontrolled variable, misreading of measurements, changed environmental factors); explain the specific effect of each error on the data; compare methods with peers to identify whether different methods produced different findings due to error differences; and distinguish error from natural variation.
Wider
  • Examine the difference between data and evidence — discussing how the same data can be interpreted differently depending on what counts as relevant evidence for a conclusion
  • Compare proposed reasons for findings in a group — explaining reasoning, identifying disagreements and posing further questions about what additional evidence would resolve them
  • Reflect on inferences made during analysis and evaluate whether the inference is supported by the data or goes beyond it

Hinge

Error identification is prerequisite for Node 2 evidence selection — students who cannot recognise what made their data unreliable cannot make principled decisions about which data counts as valid evidence for a conclusion. Thin treatment here produces conclusions that ignore data quality.
