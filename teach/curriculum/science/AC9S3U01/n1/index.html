← AC9S3U01 Life cycles

Living, once living and non-living things have distinct characteristics

AC9S3U01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 3 Science · Biological · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can classify objects as living, once living or non-living and explain their reasoning using observable characteristics (growth, reproduction, response to environment, need for nutrients).

Success criteria

  • Classifies at least five objects as living, once living or non-living with characteristic-based reasoning
  • Correctly classifies at least one ambiguous case (e.g. seed, dead leaf, fire) with explanation

Prior knowledge

Students can identify and describe features of living things and group them based on observable characteristics (AC9SFU01, F band; AC9S1U01, 1-2 band). They have not yet formally compared living and non-living things using a characteristic framework, or examined the different structural forms life cycles can take (metamorphic vs non-metamorphic).

Width

Minimum
Students classify a collection of objects as living, once living or non-living and explain the reasoning for at least three classifications using observable characteristics.
Typical
Students explain the characteristics that distinguish living from non-living things, correctly classify ambiguous cases (dead leaves, fire, crystals, seeds) with reasoning, and distinguish between 'once living' and 'never living'.
Wider
  • Investigate how First Nations Australians understand and classify the relationships between living things, Country and non-living elements — comparing with the living/non-living framework
  • Examine edge cases that challenge the classification (viruses, crystals that 'grow', fire that 'spreads') and discuss what characteristic evidence is used to decide
  • Research how scientists classify single-celled organisms and discuss whether the living/non-living distinction applies at the microscopic level

Hinge

The characteristic-based classification framework is the prerequisite for examining life cycles in Nodes 2 and 3. Students who classify by appearance or familiarity rather than characteristics cannot reason about what changes at each life stage, because they lack a principled definition of what being 'living' involves at any stage.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly classify by appearance or familiarity rather than by characteristics — dead wood is 'non-living' because it looks dead, seeds are 'non-living' because they aren't moving. Making the characteristic evidence explicit (what does living mean? → growth, reproduction, response, needs nutrients) is the scaffold that makes classification reliable across ambiguous cases.
