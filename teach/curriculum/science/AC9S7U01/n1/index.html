Purpose of classification

AC9S7U01 n1 · waypoint 1 of 5 · Year 7 Science · Biological

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain why classification is useful for organising and communicating about living things

Lessons on this site

  • Why sort living things?

Success criteria

  • Names at least two reasons why classification is useful (e.g. identification, communication)
  • Gives a correct everyday example of classification
  • Does not confuse classification with simple sorting or listing

Prior knowledge

Students can group living things by their external features (AC9SFU01), compare living and non-living things and the life cycles of plants and animals (AC9S3U01), and explain how structural features and behaviours help living things survive in their habitats (AC9S5U01). They have not studied formal taxonomy or constructed classification tools.

Width

Minimum
State at least two reasons why scientists classify living things (e.g. identification, communication); give one example of classification in everyday life
Typical
Compare two different classification systems; explain which is more useful for a given purpose and why
Wider
  • Investigate how Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples classify living things on Country and compare with Linnaean taxonomy — what does each system prioritise?
  • Research how classification systems have changed over time through improvements in microscopy; explain what this reveals about scientific knowledge
  • Consider how classification supports identification and communication in a real scientific or practical context (e.g. biosecurity, medicine)

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepThe wider tier offers genuine breadth through multiple distinct lenses — cultural classification systems (First Nations vs Linnaean), historical change through microscopy, and practical contexts (biosecurity, medicine). Each adds a different dimension to why classification exists and what any system reveals about the priorities of those who made it. More contexts here help students see classification as purposeful and constructed rather than natural and fixed.
