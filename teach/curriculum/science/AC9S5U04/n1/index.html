← AC9S5U04 Particle model of matter

Gases have observable properties distinct from solids and liquids

AC9S5U04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 5 Science · Chemical · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can describe observable properties of gases — including that gases have mass, can be compressed, and take the shape and volume of their container — and distinguish these from the properties of solids and liquids.

Success criteria

  • States at least two observable properties of gases that distinguish them from solids and liquids
  • Provides evidence that gases have mass

Prior knowledge

Students can classify materials as solids or liquids by observable properties and explain changes of state using heat energy (AC9S3U04). They have not yet used a particle model to explain why solids, liquids and gases behave as they do, or examined gases as a third state of matter with distinct properties.

Width

Minimum
Students describe at least two observable properties of gases and explain how these differ from the properties of solids or liquids.
Typical
Students classify substances as solids, liquids and gases; describe the characteristic properties of each state including compressibility, fixed vs variable shape and volume; demonstrate that gases have mass; and explain what is different about gases compared with the other two states.
Wider
  • Investigate examples demonstrating that gases have mass — using a balance to compare an empty balloon with one filled with air, or blowing air through a straw to move objects
  • Explore what is between gas particles — guided discussion about the nature of 'empty space' between particles and what this means for compression
  • Examine First Nations Australians' knowledge of gases and their applications — including the extraction of oils, medical therapies and cooking — connecting practical knowledge to particle-level understanding

Hinge

The empirical foundation for gas properties must precede the particle model — students who believe gases have no mass or cannot be compressed will reject the particle model explanation at Node 2 because it contradicts their existing framework. Thin treatment here produces a particle model that students apply by rote without understanding what it explains.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents commonly believe gases have no mass ('air is nothing') and cannot be compressed ('there is nothing to compress'). Encountering multiple demonstrations of gas mass and compressibility — and discussing what is between particles — builds an accurate empirical foundation before the particle model is introduced.
