← AC9S3U04 Solids, liquids and change of state

Solids and liquids have distinct observable properties

AC9S3U04 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Year 3 Science · Chemical · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Science: treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can distinguish solids from liquids using observable properties — solids hold their shape, liquids take the shape of their container and flow — and correctly classify materials including ambiguous cases.

Success criteria

  • Classifies at least five materials as solid or liquid using property evidence
  • States at least two observable properties that distinguish solids from liquids

Prior knowledge

Students can observe and describe properties of materials and sort them by observable features (AC9SFU03, F band). They have not yet formally defined solid and liquid using observable properties, investigated change of state, or connected heat energy addition/removal to state transitions.

Width

Minimum
Students classify at least five materials as solid or liquid using observable properties and explain the reasoning for each classification.
Typical
Students describe the observable properties that define solids (hold shape, fixed volume) and liquids (take shape of container, fixed volume, flow), correctly classify materials including ambiguous cases (toothpaste, slime, honey, cornflour mixture), and explain the evidence for each classification.
Wider
  • Investigate substances that are difficult to classify as solid or liquid — toothpaste, slime, hair gel, cornflour in water — and discuss what properties are used to decide and what limitations the binary classification has
  • Explore gases as a third state of matter — comparing their properties with solids and liquids and discussing what the three states have in common
  • Examine how properties of solids and liquids are used in everyday design decisions — why a container for liquid must be rigid and sealed, why a cutting tool must be solid

Hinge

The property-based definition of solid and liquid is the prerequisite for understanding change of state in Node 2. Students who classify by appearance rather than by the defining property criteria cannot correctly describe what changes when a solid melts — they see 'ice' becoming 'water' as a substance change rather than a state change.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly classify by appearance (sugar is a solid because it looks like sand; honey is a solid because it moves slowly) rather than by the defining property criteria. Making the property definitions explicit — does it hold its shape without a container? does it flow? — and applying them to ambiguous cases is the scaffold that makes classification reliable.
