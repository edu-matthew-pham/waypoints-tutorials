Identifying needs for known users

AC9TDI2P03 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Years 1–2 Digital Technologies (processes) · Processes and production skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Digital Technologies (processes): treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can describe a specific need that a known user has, distinct from a general want or preference

Lessons on this site

Success criteria

  • Names a specific user
  • Describes a need linked to a task or purpose, not just a preference

Prior knowledge

Students have explored digital systems in familiar contexts (AC9TDI2K01). They can identify systems and name their components. They have not yet evaluated systems against user needs — linking what a system does to whether it satisfies a specific user's requirement.

Width

Minimum
For one known user (themselves, a family member, a classmate), describe a specific need that a digital system could help with
Typical
Identify needs for multiple different users across home, school and community contexts; distinguish a need (something required to do a task) from a want (a preference)
Wider
  • Discuss how digital systems store and provide access to cultural stories authored and published by First Nations Australians — identify the need these systems satisfy for communities who want to preserve and share these stories
  • Compare what a student needs from a digital system (e.g. to watch videos, play games) with what a teacher needs from the same system (e.g. to share work with the class) — identify the different needs for the same device

Hinge

Without an identified need for a known user, Node 2 has nothing to connect system features to — discussing how a system satisfies a need requires a need to have been established first.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents at this level tend to describe all digital system use as want-based ('I want to play games') rather than identifying genuine needs. Multiple users across contrasting contexts — student vs teacher, home vs community — help students see that the same system can address different, specific needs depending on the user.
