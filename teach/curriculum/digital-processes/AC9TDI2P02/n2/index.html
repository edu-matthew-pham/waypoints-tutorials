Identifying and following branching (decisions)

AC9TDI2P02 n2 · waypoint 2 of 4 · Years 1–2 Digital Technologies (processes) · Processes and production skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Digital Technologies (processes): treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can identify decision points in an algorithm and follow the correct path based on the condition

Success criteria

  • Identifies a decision point and states the condition being checked
  • Follows the correct path for a given condition and can explain both possible outcomes

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Following and describing sequences.

Width

Minimum
Given an algorithm with one decision point (if/otherwise), follow the correct path based on the condition; explain what decision is being made and what each outcome means
Typical
Follow and describe algorithms with multiple decision points across familiar contexts (weather → what to wear, if raining → raincoat, otherwise → hat); draw or explain the decision structure; identify the condition and both possible outcomes
Wider
  • Create an algorithm for a classroom decision (e.g. if the book is in the library, borrow it, otherwise put it on the wish list) — identify the condition, the two paths, and the outcome of each
  • Apply decision-making to a recipe or process — identify where choices are made and what each choice leads to

Hinge

Branching is the hardest structural element and the reason explicitly describes systematic errors (describing only one path). Students who cannot hold both paths simultaneously cannot attempt Node 4's integration of all three structures.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepBranching is the hardest structural element to grasp at this level — students often describe only one path of a decision (the if, not the otherwise) or confuse the condition with the action. Encountering decisions in multiple, distinct contexts (weather, library, recipe) where both paths are equally plausible helps students see that both outcomes must always be specified.
Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkFollowing branching requires students to hold two possible paths in mind simultaneously and apply the condition to select one. Explicitly scaffolding the if/otherwise structure — reading it aloud, drawing both paths — prevents students from treating every decision as always taking the same path.
