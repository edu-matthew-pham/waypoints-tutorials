Following and describing sequences

AC9TDI2P02 n1 · waypoint 1 of 4 · Years 1–2 Digital Technologies (processes) · Processes and production skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Digital Technologies (processes): treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can follow a short ordered sequence of steps and describe the steps in correct order

Success criteria

  • Follows a sequence of steps in the correct order without skipping or repeating
  • Describes steps in order using sequencing language (first, then, next, last)

Prior knowledge

This is the first algorithmic thinking descriptor in the DT curriculum (no Foundation predecessor). Students bring general sequencing experience from everyday tasks — they can order familiar actions. They have not yet used computational vocabulary (sequence, branching, iteration) or applied algorithmic structure to a problem.

Width

Minimum
Follow a provided 4–6 step sequence to complete a simple task; describe the steps in order using first/then/next/last
Typical
Follow and describe sequences for multiple familiar tasks (making a sandwich, getting ready for school, feeding a pet); rearrange mixed-up steps into the correct order; explain why order matters
Wider
  • Rearrange a mixed-up sequence of pictures from a story authored and published by First Nations Australians — describe the correct order and explain how each step leads to the next
  • Compare two tasks where step order matters (socks before shoes) and two where it does not (jumper before or after shoes) — explain the difference

Hinge

Sequence fluency is the foundation for all subsequent structural types — the reason explicitly states that rushing to decisions before sequence is automatic leads to confused algorithms. Students who cannot hold step order reliably cannot add branching or iteration without losing the sequence structure.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Consolidation: more practise steps, short feedback loopsCorrect sequencing requires precision — students at this age need repeated practice ordering steps across varied contexts before branching and iteration are introduced. Rushing to decisions before sequence is automatic leads to confused algorithms that mix step types.
