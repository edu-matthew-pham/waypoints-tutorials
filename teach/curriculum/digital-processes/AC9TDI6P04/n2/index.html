← AC9TDI6P04 Generate, modify and evaluate designs

Evaluating designs against criteria and user stories

AC9TDI6P04 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Years 5–6 Digital Technologies (processes) · Processes and production skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Digital Technologies (processes): treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can evaluate design ideas against design criteria and user stories and identify which idea best meets them

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • References the design criteria and user stories when selecting a preferred design
  • Gives at least one criterion-based reason for the preference

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: Generating a range of design ideas.

Width

Minimum
Compare at least two design ideas against the design criteria and user stories; state which idea better satisfies them and give at least one reason
Typical
Use the design criteria to systematically evaluate each idea (e.g. rate each against each criterion); identify a preferred design and explain which criteria it best satisfies and where it falls short
Wider
  • Use a weighted decision matrix to evaluate designs where some criteria matter more than others — e.g. safety-critical criteria are non-negotiable, others are preferences
  • Include a user in the evaluation — explain each design to a peer acting as the user and record their responses as part of the evaluation

Hinge

Criterion-based evaluation in N2 is prerequisite for N3's modification task — students who evaluate by preference rather than criteria cannot identify which criterion is unmet, so N3's 'modify to address the gap' has no gap to address.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents commonly select a preferred design based on personal preference and then justify it post-hoc rather than genuinely evaluating against criteria. The reasoning scaffold — apply criterion 1 to idea A and idea B, apply criterion 2, then decide — prevents preference-then-justify errors.
