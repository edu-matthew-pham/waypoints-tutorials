Evaluating effectiveness against criteria and user stories

AC9TDI6P06 n1 · waypoint 1 of 3 · Years 5–6 Digital Technologies (processes) · Processes and production skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Digital Technologies (processes): treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can evaluate how well a solution addresses the identified problem by testing it against the design criteria and user stories

Success criteria

  • References at least two design criteria or user story requirements in the evaluation
  • Provides evidence for whether each criterion is met

Prior knowledge

Students can discuss how existing and student solutions satisfy the design criteria and user stories (AC9TDI4P05). They have made comparisons between solutions and criteria. They have not yet evaluated broader community impact — the new demand is assessing solutions beyond their immediate function to consider effects on the wider community.

Width

Minimum
Identify at least two criteria or user story requirements and state whether the solution meets each — with evidence
Typical
Systematically evaluate a solution against all design criteria and user stories; for each criterion, provide evidence of whether it is met and explain what would need to change if it is not
Wider
  • Evaluate the effectiveness of a student solution for a local interactive history walk — checking whether information is accurate, relevant to council needs, and accessible to expected visitors
  • Evaluate an existing solution (e.g. a community transport timetable system) against user stories for a specific group (e.g. elderly users, non-English speakers) rather than a general audience

Hinge

The evaluation reasoning scaffold established in N1 (criterion → evidence → verdict) is applied in N2 (safety features) and N3 (community impact) — students who evaluate by description in N1 will describe rather than evaluate in the subsequent nodes.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkStudents typically describe a solution positively rather than testing it against criteria. The evaluation reasoning scaffold — criterion / evidence / met or not met / what would fix it — prevents description-as-evaluation and makes the claim-evidence structure explicit.
