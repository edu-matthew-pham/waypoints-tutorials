Why passphrases are stronger than passwords

AC9TDI6P09 n1 · waypoint 1 of 2 · Years 5–6 Digital Technologies (processes) · Processes and production skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Digital Technologies (processes): treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain what makes a passphrase strong and why passphrases are more secure than short passwords

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Explains at least two characteristics that make a passphrase strong
  • Contrasts a strong passphrase with a weak password example

Prior knowledge

Students can access their school account using a memorised password and explain why it should be easy to remember but hard for others to guess (AC9TDI4P08). They have a single-account security practice. They have not yet managed multiple accounts with different credentials, or understood the specific risk cascade that password reuse creates.

Width

Minimum
Explain at least two characteristics of a strong passphrase (length, unpredictability) and contrast with a weak password
Typical
Explain that passphrases are longer sequences of words that are hard to guess but easier to remember than short complex passwords; describe what makes a passphrase strong (length, not a famous phrase, not personal info) and what makes it weak
Wider
  • Compare how a brute-force attack works against a short complex password versus a long passphrase — explaining why length is more protective than character complexity alone
  • Investigate how password managers store multiple unique passphrases securely — and why using a password manager is better than reusing a memorable password

Hinge

Students who do not understand what makes a credential strong cannot reason about the reuse risk in N2 — the cascade argument requires knowing that a compromised credential is now known to an attacker, and that requires understanding what 'compromised' means relative to credential strength.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe passphrase strength reasoning (length beats complexity; unpredictability beats memorability) is counter-intuitive. Students need the reasoning made explicit — 'a longer random phrase takes exponentially longer to guess than a short complex one' — before they can apply it to evaluate their own credentials.
