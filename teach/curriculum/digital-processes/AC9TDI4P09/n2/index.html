Digital identity from online accounts

AC9TDI4P09 n2 · waypoint 2 of 3 · Years 3–4 Digital Technologies (processes) · Processes and production skills · Hinge

Draft, from Waypoints Kit v0.2. Generated from Kit's node data and not yet reviewed by a specialist in Digital Technologies (processes): treat it as a starting suggestion and check it before use.

Checkpoint

Students can explain how personal data stored across accounts can reveal information about who they are and how they live

Lessons on this site

None yet.

Success criteria

  • Explains how a piece of stored data reveals something beyond just the data itself
  • Does not limit 'personal data' only to name and address

Prior knowledge

Waypoint 1: What personal data is stored in accounts.

Width

Minimum
Give one example of how personal data stored in an account could tell someone something about the account owner beyond just their name
Typical
Explain how personal data in accounts (photos, location, habits, nickname, posted content) collectively forms a digital identity that reveals details about a person's life, location and interests
Wider
  • Analyse photographs of themselves, friends or fictional characters and identify what details (location, home, routine) could be inferred from the image — discuss how images are personal data with hidden detail
  • Discuss how a gaming nickname that seems anonymous can still reveal information about the player through patterns of play, team membership or in-game communication

Hinge

Students who do not understand how data accumulates into a digital identity cannot name specific risks in N3 — 'what could go wrong' requires knowing what information is actually revealed, not just that data is stored.

Response modes

Where extra effort goes at this waypoint (width), not what kind of lesson it is.

Context breadth: more contexts in the vary or modify stepStudents readily understand that their name and address are personal data but underestimate how combinations of seemingly neutral data (nickname + playing time + region) build a detailed profile. Multiple examples across different data types build awareness of cumulative identity disclosure.
Structured reasoning: a worked chain of reasons, then a write-and-compare (reasoning) checkThe move from 'this data is stored' to 'this data reveals something about me' requires explicit inference reasoning. Students benefit from a scaffold: 'if someone only had this information, what could they work out about me?' — applied across several examples.
