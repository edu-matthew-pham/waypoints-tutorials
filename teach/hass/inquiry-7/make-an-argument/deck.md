# Make an argument

Investigate like a humanities expert · Sharing what you found

<!-- Speaker note: Students take a position for an audience, back it with two different points that each have named evidence and a reason, and fairly state a reasonable alternative view. ## Today

**We are learning to** argue a position with two supported points and a fair alternative view.

- I can take a clear position for an audience.
- I can support two different points with named evidence and reasoning.
- I can acknowledge a reasonable alternative.

## Why it matters

> Saying 'I strongly believe it' tells us your view. What would give a reader a reason to agree?

<!-- Speaker note: An **argument** takes a position on a question people could fairly disagree about. Give supporting points. Name your evidence and explain how it supports your position. ## Think first

**Which plan gives readers reasons to agree?**

- Give a position, two points with evidence, and another view
- Say the position three times, more strongly each time
- Give a position and the one best piece of evidence
- List everything you found, and let readers choose

## The idea: support a position

**Match each part of the Mill Road argument to its job.**

| | |
|---|---|
| `Ask the council to trial a closure at arrival time, then review it.` | A. Evidence that a timed trial could avoid many delivery periods |
| `Count C2 records 40 vehicles crossing the entrance during arrival time.` | B. Evidence for examining conflicts at the entrance |
| `Shop survey C3 says 6 of 8 shops receive deliveries later.` | C. Counter-consideration: an issue to address |
| `Two shops may still need access; check their arrangements.` | D. Position: what the audience is asked to accept |

## Vary: argue about a display

**History model:** *The display about change should include the mirror. It is an artefact that shows how a surface can change. First, catalogue source M1 records a polished face, so it was once shiny. Second, conservation source M2 says wear may have dulled it, which helps explain the change. Neither record tells us exactly how it first looked. So the display should call this a possible explanation, not the full story of life in Han times.*

For a real historical topic, your teacher supplies or approves real sources and checks historical knowledge.

## Apply: plan your argument

Choose the civic case (address the council) or the historical case (address museum visitors), or use your class topic with verified sources. You may disagree with the models if the evidence supports your position. For real class work, use your teacher's sources.

| Part | What to put in your plan |
|---|---|
| Question and audience | What is being debated, and who needs to understand it? |
| Position | One clear answer |
| Point 1 | A reason, named evidence and its connection to the position |
| Point 2 | A different reason, named evidence and its connection |
| Alternative | A fair objection or interpretation and your response |
| Close | Return to the position within the evidence's limits |

## Apply: write and check

Turn your plan into a short argument. Use your subject’s checklist:

- **Civics:** a clear claim, **two distinct supporting points** with referenced evidence and at least two accurate civic terms, such as *consultation* and *participation*.
- **History:** a clear position supported by **at least two pieces of evidence**, at least two historical terms, such as *artefact*, *source*, *change* or *interpretation*, and a counter-consideration.
- **Both:** evidence connected to reasons, language suitable for the audience and uncertainty preserved.

Writing stays in your own book or document. A partner or teacher reads it; the site does not collect or mark it.
