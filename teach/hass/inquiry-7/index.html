Teacher guide: Investigate like a humanities expert

Nine short lessons on asking questions, finding and organising information, and sharing explanations and arguments. Dip into the skill your class needs; you do not have to finish the course before starting an inquiry. Use your current subject's task, with other cases and width steps as optional practice on the same idea. Four invented cases support practice; real inquiries remain class work. Preview, not yet tried with classes.

Open the student course · How the ideas build

Each lesson works two ways. Students can work through the online tutorial at their own pace (it checks their answers and saves their progress in their browser), or you can teach it to the whole class from the slides, then send students into the tutorial for the Modify and Make steps.

Every lesson guide has plain-language notes, the answers to every check, a solution for every task, and slides and a worksheet for Waypoints Prepare. These guide pages are not linked from student pages, but they are not secret either: keep printed Teacher editions for answers you do not want students to find.

Year 7 Economics and Business inquiry (Economics and Business · 5 stages)

Printable sheet: Open in Print · Markdown · Student page

Use this outline to plan and carry out a class investigation into an economic or business issue chosen with your teacher (for example, how a local business, school service or council allocates limited resources). Write your topic, question, notes, data tables and final explanation in your own book or document, not on this page.

Hand in:

  • Your key inquiry question and a short note on what evidence will answer it.
  • Your selected notes and data from at least two sources, organised in a table or chart.
  • A comparison of at least two options for responding to the issue, showing opportunity cost, costs and benefits.
  • A written description and explanation that uses economics and business terms and references your sources.

1. Ask an investigable question

Students will: Write a focused question about your class's economic or business issue that uses an economics concept (such as scarcity, opportunity cost, market or business purpose) and can be answered with evidence.

Evidences (2 waypoints):

  • What makes a question investigable (AC9HE7S01 node 1): Identifies a question as investigable or not-investigable and gives a reason; Explains at least two features of a good investigable question
  • Using economics concepts in question construction (AC9HE7S01 node 2): Develops at least one investigable question that correctly uses an economics or business concept; The question is answerable using accessible data or sources

Need help? Try… Can this be investigated? · Build a question from a big idea · Right-sized, and what would answer it

Stage note: Supported on the site by the three Asking questions lessons. Help students narrow broad 'Should...' questions to a specific local decision or business case with accessible data.

2. Find and organise information

Students will: Locate at least two relevant sources or datasets, keep only the facts and figures that answer your question, and organise them into a clear digital table or graph.

Evidences (3 waypoints):

  • Locating sources using targeted search (AC9HE7S02 node 1): Locates at least two relevant sources for an economic topic; Describes the search terms or strategy used and explains why they were chosen
  • Selecting relevant information (AC9HE7S02 node 2): Selects at least two pieces of information from a source with a stated reason for relevance; Discards or sets aside information identified as not relevant to the investigation question
  • Organising data using appropriate formats (AC9HE7S02 node 3): Organises selected information into an appropriate format using a digital tool; Explains why the chosen format suits the data type

Need help? Try… Where to look, and how to search · Keep what answers your question · Organise what you found

Stage note: Supported on the site by the three Finding information lessons (and ICT's Working with data if students use a spreadsheet). Supply a small starter pack of 2 to 3 local sources or datasets so class time goes into selecting and organising rather than open-ended web searching.

3. Make sense of the data

Students will: Read your table or graph to spot a pattern, trend or cause-and-effect relationship, and point to the exact figures that show it.

Evidences (3 waypoints):

  • Identifying economic issues from information (AC9HE7S03 node 1): Identifies an economic or business issue from provided information; Explains the inference made — what in the information pointed to the issue
  • Identifying trends using multi-variable data (AC9HE7S03 node 2): Identifies and describes a trend in a multi-variable dataset using a digital tool; Answers a directed question about the trend using evidence from the data
  • Cause-and-effect relationships (AC9HE7S03 node 3): Identifies a cause-and-effect relationship in a visual data display; Explains the direction of the relationship using evidence from the data; Uses economic terms (trend, cause, effect, relationship, variable) accurately

Need help? Try… Economics skills: Read economic data for trends and cause and effect (Coming later)

Stage note: Class work (Economics data skills course is planned). Give students one multi-variable table or chart related to your topic and model the difference between describing a number and inferring an economic trend or cause-and-effect link.

4. Weigh costs and benefits to choose a response

Students will: Propose a response to the issue, list both financial and non-financial costs and benefits of your option and an alternative, and explain why your choice comes out ahead.

Evidences (2 waypoints):

  • What counts as a cost and a benefit (AC9HE7S04 node 1): Identifies at least one financial and one non-financial cost and benefit for a given decision; Explains why each factor counts as a cost or benefit
  • Reasoning from cost-benefit to a response (AC9HE7S04 node 2): Develops a response to an economic issue that identifies costs and benefits of the chosen option; Explains why one alternative was selected over another using cost-benefit reasoning

Need help? Try… Economics and Business › Scarcity and opportunity cost: Putting it together - scarcity, choice and the future (applies opportunity cost to a decision) · Economics skills: Costs, benefits and choosing a response (Coming later)

Stage note: Class work. Lesson 4 of Scarcity and opportunity cost (`economics/scarcity-7/putting-together`) applies opportunity cost to a council decision; a dedicated skill lesson on cost-benefit tables is planned (`Coming later`). Check that students include at least one non-financial cost or benefit (such as time, fairness or environmental impact) alongside dollar figures, name the opportunity cost of their chosen option, and note that in economics weighing costs and benefits across different groups involves a value judgement.

5. Explain what you found

Students will: Write a clear opening for your audience and an explanation that uses economics and business terms accurately and references your data and sources.

Evidences (2 waypoints):

  • Orienting an audience to an economic issue (AC9HE7S05 node 1): Writes an opening that names the economic issue and its relevance to the audience; Uses at least two economic or business terms accurately in the opening
  • Explaining with data and appropriate formats (AC9HE7S05 node 2): Explains an idea or decision using at least one piece of referenced data or information; Presents data or information in a format appropriate to the explanation and audience; Uses economic and business terms (market, income, goods and services, costs and benefits) accurately throughout

Need help? Try… Use the subject's words · Explain with evidence

Stage note: Supported on the site by Sharing what you found (Lessons 7 and 8; Lesson 9 is optional if students are writing a persuasive recommendation). Students write in their own book or document and use the 'Good looks like' criteria below for self- and peer-checking.

When to use this skeleton: In the second half of a Year 7 Economics and Business unit (after Scarcity and opportunity cost or Types of businesses), over 2 to 4 lessons of class inquiry time.

What is on the site vs class work: Stages 1, 2 and 5 are supported directly by the shared Investigate like a humanities expert lessons (hass/inquiry-7). Stages 3 and 4 are class-led using your local case and data (economics/scarcity-7/putting-together applies opportunity cost to a decision; the dedicated cost-benefit skill lesson is planned as Coming later).

First Nations note: Where your class inquiry investigates First Nations Australian businesses, exchange networks or sustainable resource management (AC9HE7S02, AC9HE7K01–AC9HE7K03), start from ACARA's elaborations and use local, community-approved material or a First Nations business's own published words.

Year 7 Civics and Citizenship inquiry (Civics and Citizenship · 5 stages)

Printable sheet: Open in Print · Markdown · Student page

Use this outline to plan and carry out a class investigation into a civic, legal or community issue chosen with your teacher (for example, how a council rule, parliamentary check or community campaign works in practice). Write your topic, question, source notes, comparison of options and final argument in your own book or document, not on this page.

Hand in:

  • Your focused civic inquiry question and sub-questions.
  • Your organised source notes showing who made each source and what perspective it represents.
  • A comparison of at least two lawful ways citizens or institutions could respond, noting strengths and trade-offs.
  • A written explanation and civic argument that uses civics terms accurately and references your sources.

1. Ask a focused civic question

Students will: Write an open, testable question about a civic institution, rule or current issue that uses civics concepts (such as representation, rights, responsibility or fairness).

Evidences (3 waypoints):

  • Characteristics of effective civic inquiry questions (AC9HC7S01 node 1): Identify at least two characteristics of an effective civic inquiry question; Distinguish between a too-broad question and a focused, investigable question and explain the difference
  • Developing a key inquiry question (AC9HC7S01 node 2): Develop a focused inquiry question about Australia's political or legal system; Explain what the question is designed to investigate and identify what sources would be useful
  • Using current events to generate civic questions (AC9HC7S01 node 3): Identify a current event related to a civic issue and develop a focused question connecting it to a broader civic concept; Explain what makes this question more useful for civic investigation than simply asking 'what happened'

Need help? Try… Can this be investigated? · Build a question from a big idea · Right-sized, and what would answer it

Stage note: Supported on the site by the three Asking questions lessons (Lesson 2's wider step practices turning a current news event into a neutral civic question for `AC9HC7S01 n3`). Help students turn a slogan into a question that evidence can test.

2. Find and group civic information

Students will: Locate relevant civic sources (such as official factsheets, committee submissions or news reports), keep what answers your sub-questions, and group your notes by idea.

Evidences (3 waypoints):

  • Identifying and using civics-relevant sources (AC9HC7S02 node 1): Identify at least three source types relevant to civic investigations and explain what each provides; Match a given inquiry question to the most appropriate source type and explain the choice
  • Locating information using search strategies (AC9HC7S02 node 2): Use at least one targeted search strategy beyond basic keywords to locate relevant civic information; Identify an appropriate online source for a given civic research question
  • Selecting and organising information (AC9HC7S02 node 3): Select information from at least two sources that is relevant to a civic inquiry question; Organise selected information so that similar ideas from different sources are grouped, not just listed by source

Need help? Try… Where to look, and how to search · Keep what answers your question · Organise what you found

Stage note: Supported on the site by the three Finding information lessons. Provide a small curated pack of 2 to 3 contrasting civic sources so students spend their time selecting and grouping rather than cold-searching.

3. Compare perspectives on the issue

Students will: Identify the different perspectives in your sources, explain why groups hold different views on the issue, and note any challenges or disagreements they raise.

Evidences (3 waypoints):

  • Identifying perspectives in civic sources (AC9HC7S03 node 1): Identify the perspective represented by a civic source and explain how that perspective might influence the information it contains; Identify at least one perspective that might be absent from a given source
  • Comparing and explaining perspective differences (AC9HC7S03 node 2): Compare the perspectives of two sources on the same civic issue and identify at least one point of agreement and one of disagreement; Explain at least one reason why the perspectives differ
  • Identifying potential challenges (AC9HC7S03 node 3): Identify at least one potential challenge arising from competing perspectives on a civic issue; Explain what makes the challenge difficult to resolve with reference to the perspectives involved

Need help? Try… Civics skills: Analysing perspectives and challenges in civic sources (Coming later)

Stage note: Class work (Civics perspectives skill lesson is planned). Ask students to identify what each group values or stands to gain or lose, rather than simply labelling a source 'biased'.

4. Choose a way to take part

Students will: Compare at least two democratic participation methods for your issue, weigh their strengths and trade-offs, and choose a strategy that fits your civic goal.

Evidences (3 waypoints):

  • Features of civic participation methods (AC9HC7S04 node 1): Identify at least three civic participation methods and describe the key features of each; Explain what makes a particular method appropriate for a specific type of civic goal
  • Responsibilities of active citizenship (AC9HC7S04 node 2): Identify at least two responsibilities of active citizenship and explain how they support democratic life; Give an example of active citizenship contributing to social cohesion and explain the connection
  • Choosing participation strategies for civic goals (AC9HC7S04 node 3): Explain why a particular civic participation method would be chosen for a specific civic goal; Identify the connection between participation strategy and intended democratic or just outcome

Need help? Try… Civics and Citizenship › Freedoms and taking part: Ways to take part in democracy (compares voting, petitions, submissions, community groups and peaceful protest) · Civics and Citizenship › Freedoms and taking part: Formal and active citizenship (distinguishes legal citizenship status from active participation) · Civics skills: Choosing a participation strategy (Coming later)

Stage note: Supported by Lessons 3 and 4 of Freedoms and taking part (`civics/democracy-7`), with a dedicated strategy-selection lesson planned (`Coming later`). Discuss how matching a method to the right level of government and audience matters more than picking the loudest action.

5. Explain and argue your position

Students will: Write a clear explanation and civic argument that states your position, supports it with at least two distinct evidence-backed points, and uses civics terms accurately.

Evidences (3 waypoints):

  • Using civics concepts and terms accurately (AC9HC7S05 node 1): Use at least three civics concepts or terms correctly in a piece of writing about a civic topic; Demonstrate that terms are used to communicate genuine understanding (not superficially) by explaining what each term means in context
  • Developing evidence-referenced explanations (AC9HC7S05 node 2): Write an explanation that references at least one piece of specific evidence; Connect the evidence to the explanation — showing why the evidence supports the claim rather than just listing it
  • Constructing civic arguments (AC9HC7S05 node 3): Construct a structured civic argument with a clear claim, at least two supporting points, and referenced evidence; Use at least two civics concepts or terms accurately and present ideas appropriate to the intended audience

Need help? Try… Use the subject's words · Explain with evidence · Make an argument

Stage note: Supported on the site by all three Sharing what you found lessons. Check that each student names the specific institution or level of government that has the power to act on their proposal.

When to use this skeleton: Alongside How Australia is governed (civics/government-7) or Freedoms and taking part (civics/democracy-7), over 2 to 4 lessons of class inquiry time.

What is on the site vs class work: Stages 1, 2 and 5 are supported by the shared Investigate like a humanities expert lessons, and Stage 4 connects directly to civics/democracy-7. Stage 3 is class-led with your chosen local or national case.

First Nations note: Where a class topic involves First Nations Australians' perspectives on civic, legal or constitutional questions (AC9HC7S02, AC9HC7S03), follow ACARA's elaborations on culturally responsive protocols and use local, community-approved sources rather than asking students to cold-contact community organisations.

Year 7 Geography inquiry (Geography · 6 stages)

Printable sheet: Open in Print · Markdown · Student page

Use this outline to plan and carry out a geographical investigation into a place, water resource or liveability challenge chosen with your teacher. Write your topic, questions, fieldwork and secondary data tables, maps and final explanation in your own book or document, not on this page.

Hand in:

  • Your geographical inquiry questions and plan for collecting data.
  • Your recorded primary and secondary data and a geographical representation (map, graph or table).
  • Your analysis of spatial patterns, impacts and proposed strategies for action.
  • A written geographical explanation that uses geographical concepts, terms and evidence.

1. Ask and plan a geographical inquiry

Students will: Write at least two focused geographical questions about the same phenomenon or challenge. Plan at least two types of evidence and a collection method for each, explaining how each will help.

Evidences (3 waypoints):

  • Geographical questions vs general questions (AC9HG7S01 node 1): Identifies at least two features that make a question geographical and applies these to classify example questions.; Transforms at least one general question into a geographical inquiry question, explaining what geographical dimension has been added.
  • Developing questions for a specific phenomenon or challenge (AC9HG7S01 node 2): Develops at least two geographical inquiry questions about a specific phenomenon or challenge that are specific and investigable with real evidence.; Explains what geographical phenomenon, process or concept each question targets.
  • Planning a geographical investigation (AC9HG7S01 node 3): Produces an investigation plan that specifies the inquiry question, at least two types of evidence needed, and a collection method for each.; Explains how the planned evidence would allow the inquiry question to be answered.

Need help? Try… Can this be investigated? · Build a question from a big idea · Right-sized, and what would answer it

Stage note: Supported on the site by the three Asking questions lessons. Check that students' questions name a specific place, spatial distribution or environmental/human connection.

2. Collect and represent data (fieldwork and secondary)

Students will: Collect primary fieldwork observations and secondary data (such as maps, climate tables or ABS statistics) and represent them in an appropriate geographical format.

Evidences (4 waypoints):

  • Primary fieldwork methods for geographical data (AC9HG7S02 node 1 · teacher guide): Identifies at least two primary research methods appropriate to a specific geographical inquiry question and explains what data each produces.; Uses at least one digital tool to support primary data collection and explains why it was selected.
  • Secondary research: finding and selecting relevant materials (AC9HG7S02 node 2 · printed on student sheet): Locates at least two secondary sources relevant to a geographical inquiry question and explains the criteria used to assess each source's relevance.; Uses at least one advanced search method to find sources and reflects on how it improved search results.
  • Representing data in appropriate geographical formats (AC9HG7S02 node 3 · printed on student sheet): Represents geographical data in at least one format (map, table, graph) that is appropriate to the data type and inquiry question.; Applies cartographic conventions (title, scale, north arrow, legend) to any map produced.
  • Integration: collect, organise and represent in an inquiry context (AC9HG7S02 node 4 · printed on student sheet): Produces an evidence base using at least one primary and one secondary source, represented in appropriate formats for the inquiry question.; Reflects on one limitation or gap in the evidence base and explains what additional evidence would address it.

Need help? Try… Where to look, and how to search · Keep what answers your question · Organise what you found · Geography skills: Fieldwork and maps (Coming later)

Stage note: The student sheet prints the 3 culminating nodes (`n2`–`n4`, 6 tick-boxes) so the six-stage sheet fits on two pages; check primary fieldwork methods (`AC9HG7S02 n1`) alongside them when your class collects primary data. Supported for secondary research and tables by Finding information (`hass/inquiry-7`); fieldwork and mapping conventions (BOLTSS) are class work (`Coming later`).

3. Analyse patterns, trends and relationships

Students will: Compare your data and maps to identify similarities and differences, describe spatial patterns and trends, and infer geographical relationships.

Evidences (4 waypoints):

  • Identifying similarities and differences in geographical data (AC9HG7S03 node 1 · teacher guide): Identifies at least two similarities and two differences between geographical datasets or places, citing specific evidence for each.; Avoids vague generalisations — each comparison is grounded in a specific data point, map feature or image detail.
  • Explaining geographical patterns and trends (AC9HG7S03 node 2 · printed on student sheet): Identifies at least one geographical pattern or trend in data and explains it by reference to a geographical process.; Distinguishes between describing a pattern (what is observed) and explaining it (why it occurs).
  • Inferring geographical relationships (AC9HG7S03 node 3 · printed on student sheet): Infers at least one relationship from geographical data and identifies the evidence that supports the inference.; Notes at least one limitation of the inference or an alternative explanation that the data cannot rule out.
  • Integration: interpret and analyse geographical data (AC9HG7S03 node 4 · printed on student sheet): Produces a data analysis that demonstrates at least two of: identifying similarities/differences, explaining patterns/trends, inferring relationships.; Grounds each analytical claim in specific data evidence.

Need help? Try… Geography skills: Interpreting geographical data and patterns (Coming later)

Stage note: The student sheet prints `n2`–`n4` (patterns, relationships and integrated analysis); check basic comparison of similarities and differences (`AC9HG7S03 n1`) as students read their tables and maps. Model naming where a pattern holds and any exception (anomaly).

4. Draw geographical conclusions

Students will: Use geographical concepts (such as place, interconnection, environment or sustainability) to organise your findings and explain impacts on people, places and environments.

Evidences (3 waypoints):

  • Geographical concepts as conclusion organisers (AC9HG7S04 node 1): Names the geographical concept used to organise the conclusion and explains how it frames the analytical finding.; Produces a conclusion that is structured by a geographical concept, not just a summary of data findings.
  • Explaining impacts on people, places and environments (AC9HG7S04 node 2): Explains at least one impact on people and one impact on places or environments, connecting each to the geographical phenomenon studied.; Uses analysis evidence to support each impact claim.
  • Conclusions as answers to the inquiry question (AC9HG7S04 node 3): Produces a conclusion that directly responds to the inquiry question.; Supports the conclusion with at least two analytical findings, using geographical terminology.

Need help? Try… Geography › Water as a resource: Putting it together: classify, connect, change (joins resource classification, spatial connection and environmental/human effects) · Geography skills: Drawing conclusions about impacts (Coming later)

Stage note: Lesson 5 of Water as a resource (`geography/water-7/classify-connect-change`) models joining classification, connection and change into a conclusion. Check that students' conclusions directly answer their Stage 1 question.

5. Propose strategies for action

Students will: Propose an individual or collective strategy to respond to the geographical challenge, explain its environmental, economic or social impacts, and note how values shape the choice.

Evidences (4 waypoints):

  • Individual action in response to geographical phenomena (AC9HG7S05 node 1 · teacher guide): Proposes at least one individual action in response to a geographical phenomenon or challenge.; Supports the proposal with geographical reasoning that connects the action to the identified problem.
  • Collective action and geographical change (AC9HG7S05 node 2 · printed on student sheet): Proposes at least one collective action and explains how it would address a geographical challenge.; Identifies the scale at which the collective action operates and explains why that scale is appropriate.
  • Personal values and explanation of potential impacts (AC9HG7S05 node 3 · printed on student sheet): Identifies at least one personal value or attitude that influences their explanation of a potential impact.; Explains how a different value perspective would produce a different assessment of the same impact.
  • Integration: strategy, impact and values (AC9HG7S05 node 4 · printed on student sheet): Produces a strategy proposal with at least two potential impacts explained.; Reflects on at least one value dimension that shapes the impact explanation.

Need help? Try… Geography skills: Strategies for action and weighing values (Coming later)

Stage note: The student sheet prints `n2`–`n4` (collective action, values/impacts and integrated strategy); check individual action options (`AC9HG7S05 n1`) alongside them. Remind students that environmental, economic and social priorities can pull in different directions, so choosing a 'best' strategy depends on how a community weights those values.

6. Communicate your geographical explanation

Students will: Write a geographical description and explanation that uses geographical concepts, terms, research evidence and representations to answer your question.

Evidences (4 waypoints):

  • Geographical descriptions with concepts and terminology (AC9HG7S06 node 1 · teacher guide): Produces a geographical description that uses at least three geographical terms accurately.; References at least one geographical concept explicitly in the description.
  • Geographical explanations supported by research findings (AC9HG7S06 node 2 · printed on student sheet): Produces a geographical explanation that integrates at least two research findings as evidence within the explanation (not just appended).; Explains how each piece of evidence supports the geographical idea being argued.
  • Geographical representations as communication tools (AC9HG7S06 node 3 · printed on student sheet): Includes at least one geographical representation in a response and explains how it supports the argument.; Integrates the representation into the text of the response rather than presenting it as a separate appendix.
  • Integration: create complete geographical responses (AC9HG7S06 node 4 · printed on student sheet): Produces a geographical response that includes description, explanation supported by evidence, and at least one geographical representation.; Uses geographical terminology accurately and consistently throughout.

Need help? Try… Use the subject's words · Explain with evidence · Geography skills: Communicating with maps and representations (Coming later)

Stage note: The student sheet prints `n2`–`n4` (evidence-backed explanations, representations and complete response); check accurate geographical terminology and concepts (`AC9HG7S06 n1`, taught in `use-the-subjects-words`) throughout.

When to use this skeleton: Alongside Water as a resource (geography/water-7) or a Year 7 Place and liveability unit, over 3 to 5 lessons of class/field inquiry time.

What is on the site vs class work: Stages 1, 2 (secondary research and organising) and 6 (terminology and evidence-based explanation) are supported by hass/inquiry-7, and Stage 4 connects to geography/water-7/classify-connect-change. Fieldwork, mapping conventions, spatial pattern analysis and action strategies are class-led (Coming later on the site).

First Nations note: Where your class inquiry examines First Nations Australians' Custodianship of Country/Place, water management or liveability (AC9HG7S02, AC9HG7K04, AC9HG7K06), start from ACARA's elaborations and use local, community-approved sources and protocols selected with your school or state curriculum authority.

Year 7 History inquiry (History · 5 stages)

Printable sheet: Open in Print · Markdown · Student page

Use this outline to plan and carry out a historical investigation into an ancient society, event, site or historical mystery from the depth study you are doing with your class. Write your topic, questions, source notes, usefulness checks and final historical argument in your own book or document, not on this page.

Hand in:

  • Your historical inquiry questions and a list of the primary and secondary sources you located.
  • Your source analysis notes covering origin, purpose, accuracy, usefulness and perspectives.
  • Your notes on causes, continuity and change, or competing historical interpretations.
  • A written historical description, explanation or argument that uses historical terms and references your sources.

1. Ask historical questions

Students will: Write at least two historical inquiry questions about the same topic using different concept frames, such as cause and change. Explain what evidence each needs and keep the scope manageable.

Evidences (3 waypoints):

  • What makes a question historical? (AC9HH7S01 node 1): Correctly distinguishes a historical inquiry question from a recall or opinion question.; Identifies at least two characteristics of a good historical inquiry question (investigable, focused on causation/change/significance/perspective).; Can sort a given set of questions into 'suitable for historical inquiry' and 'not suitable', with reasons.
  • Developing questions using historical concepts (AC9HH7S01 node 2): Develops at least two historical inquiry questions using different concept frames.; Questions are genuinely investigable using historical evidence — not answerable by simple recall.; Correctly identifies which historical concept (causation, change, perspective, significance) each question draws on.
  • Questions that inform inquiry — scope and focus (AC9HH7S01 node 3): Develops a historical inquiry question that is appropriately scoped — investigable, not a simple recall or overly broad question.; Identifies what types of evidence would be needed to investigate the question.; Explains how the question would guide the investigation — what it would require the student to look for.

Need help? Try… Can this be investigated? · Build a question from a big idea · Right-sized, and what would answer it

Stage note: Supported on the site by the three Asking questions lessons. Help students move from simple fact-lookup questions ('When was X built?') to historical questions about purpose, change, significance or evidence.

2. Locate and sort primary and secondary sources

Students will: Find relevant primary sources (from the time being investigated) and secondary sources (later interpretations of that past), keep what answers your question, and organise your source notes clearly.

Evidences (3 waypoints):

  • Primary and secondary sources — the disciplinary distinction (AC9HH7S02 node 1): States the correct disciplinary definition of primary and secondary sources (primary = from the time of the event).; Correctly classifies at least four sources, including at least one non-obvious case.; Does not classify based on age alone — recognises that a modern document about a modern event is still a primary source.
  • Locating sources — repositories and search strategies (AC9HH7S02 node 2): Successfully locates at least one primary and one secondary source relevant to a given inquiry question.; Identifies the repository or database used and explains why it was appropriate for the source type sought.; Uses subject-specific search terms rather than general keyword searches.
  • Sorting and organising sources for inquiry (AC9HH7S02 node 3): Correctly sorts a set of sources into primary and secondary categories.; Identifies at least two sub-categories within primary sources (e.g. artefact, written, visual).; Explains why different source types serve different functions in historical inquiry.

Need help? Try… History › Source origin and purpose: Primary or secondary? It is about when, not how old (distinguishes primary from secondary sources for your question) · Where to look, and how to search · Organise what you found

Stage note: Supported on the site by `history/sources-7/primary-or-secondary` and the Finding information lessons. Provide a curated source booklet (artefact photos, translated extracts and a modern historian's summary) suited to your depth study.

3. Question the sources (origin, purpose, usefulness and perspectives)

Students will: Interrogate each source's origin, context and purpose, test its accuracy and usefulness for your specific question, and explain whose perspective is shown or missing.

Evidences (9 waypoints):

  • Origin and content — the basic analytical questions (AC9HH7S03 node 1 · teacher guide): Correctly identifies the producer (who), period (when), and location (where) of a source's origin.; Accurately describes the content — what the source shows, contains, or records — without yet interpreting what it means.; Distinguishes origin from content — keeps the 'who made it' question separate from the 'what does it show' question.
  • Context and purpose — the interpretive questions (AC9HH7S03 node 2 · teacher guide): Correctly identifies the historical context of a source's production — not just 'it's from ancient times' but specific circumstances.; Identifies the purpose of the source — ceremonial, propagandistic, administrative, commemorative — with reasoning.; Explains how context and purpose affect what the source reveals and what it omits.
  • Integrating OCCP — what can this source tell us? (AC9HH7S03 node 3 · printed on student sheet): Applies all four dimensions (origin, content, context, purpose) to a source systematically.; Draws a reasoned conclusion about what the source can and cannot tell us about a specific historical question.; Does not treat OCCP as four separate boxes — synthesises the dimensions into an integrated interpretive conclusion.
  • Accuracy — fact, opinion, and source reliability (AC9HH7S04 node 1 · teacher guide): Correctly distinguishes at least two factual claims from two opinions in a given source.; Applies at least two accuracy questions to the source and draws a reasoned conclusion about reliability.; Identifies at least one factor (bias, limited information, purpose) that affects the source's accuracy.
  • Usefulness — what a source can tell us for a specific inquiry (AC9HH7S04 node 2 · teacher guide): Correctly defines accuracy and usefulness as separate concepts.; Identifies information in a source that is useful as evidence for a specific inquiry question.; Explains at least one case where a source with limited accuracy is still useful — and why.
  • Integrating accuracy and usefulness — evidence assessment (AC9HH7S04 node 3 · printed on student sheet): Assesses both accuracy and usefulness of a source for a specific inquiry question.; Synthesises both dimensions into a reasoned conclusion — not two separate boxes but an integrated assessment.; Explicitly states what the source can and cannot contribute to answering the inquiry question.
  • What is a perspective in a historical source? (AC9HH7S06 node 1 · teacher guide): Identifies whose perspective is represented in a source and describes it as a viewpoint, not a fact.; Distinguishes between what the source says (content) and what perspective it expresses.; Explains how the producer's social position shapes the perspective in the source.
  • Attitudes and values of the past — reading the world behind the source (AC9HH7S06 node 2 · printed on student sheet): Identifies at least two attitudes or values of the past expressed in a source.; Explains how the source expresses these values — not just states they exist but shows how they appear in the source.; Does not simply judge past attitudes by contemporary standards — demonstrates some attempt to understand them in historical context.
  • Absent perspectives — who is missing from the historical record? (AC9HH7S06 node 3 · teacher guide): Names at least two groups underrepresented in historical sources and explains why.; Explains what limited evidence about underrepresented groups can still reveal — does not conclude that absence of evidence means absence of significance.; Identifies specific types of evidence that can partially recover absent perspectives.

4. Weigh causes, continuity and interpretations

Students will: Place events in chronological sequence, explain causes, effects and what changed or stayed the same, and compare different historical interpretations of the evidence.

Evidences (6 waypoints):

  • Chronological reasoning and dating conventions (AC9HH7S05 node 1 · teacher guide): Correctly defines BC/BCE and AD/CE and converts between systems without error.; Correctly sequences at least five events in chronological order using a mix of BCE and CE dates.; Defines BP and explains when and why it is used (deep-time events before written records).
  • Causes and effects — multi-causal historical reasoning (AC9HH7S05 node 2 · printed on student sheet): Identifies at least two causes of a historical event and distinguishes between them (e.g. long-term vs short-term).; Describes at least two effects — short-term and long-term, or immediate and broader consequences.; Does not treat historical events as having a single, obvious cause.
  • Continuity and change — explaining what persisted and what shifted (AC9HH7S05 node 3 · printed on student sheet): Identifies at least one continuity and one change within a period and supports each with evidence.; Explains that continuity and change coexist — does not treat them as mutually exclusive.; Uses chronological evidence (dated sources, timelines) to support continuity/change claims rather than asserting them without evidence.
  • What is a historical interpretation? (AC9HH7S07 node 1 · teacher guide): Correctly defines historical interpretation as a constructed argument about the past, not a statement of fact.; Distinguishes between a historical fact and a historical interpretation, with an example of each.; Explains that interpretations are based on evidence — they are reasoned arguments, not opinions.
  • Different interpretations of the same event or individual (AC9HH7S07 node 2 · teacher guide): Describes at least two different interpretations of the same event or individual.; Identifies at least two reasons why the interpretations differ (not just 'they had different opinions').; Does not treat one interpretation as simply 'right' — recognises that multiple interpretations can coexist legitimately.
  • Developing a historical argument about interpretation (AC9HH7S07 node 3 · printed on student sheet): Develops a reasoned argument — not just a description — about why a historical event or individual is interpreted in different ways.; Identifies at least two interpretations and at least two factors producing the difference.; The argument uses evidence to support its claims, not just assertion.

5. Build a historical argument

Students will: Write a historical description, explanation or argument that uses chronological and historical terms accurately, cites primary and secondary sources, and addresses a counter-consideration.

Evidences (3 waypoints):

  • Historical description — communicating knowledge with evidence (AC9HH7S08 node 1): Uses at least two subject-specific historical terms correctly in context.; References at least one piece of evidence from a source — naming the source or evidence type.; The description is accurate — it correctly represents historical knowledge without factual errors.
  • Historical explanation — reasoning with evidence (AC9HH7S08 node 2): Addresses a 'why' or 'how' question — the text explains, not just describes.; Uses at least one historical concept explicitly in the explanation.; References evidence from sources to support reasoning — evidence is integrated into the argument, not listed separately.
  • Historical argument — taking and supporting a position with evidence (AC9HH7S08 node 3): Develops a clear position — an argument, not just a description or explanation — supported by at least two pieces of evidence from sources.; Uses historical knowledge, concepts, and terms accurately throughout.; Acknowledges at least one counter-consideration or alternative view.

Need help? Try… Use the subject's words · Explain with evidence · Make an argument
