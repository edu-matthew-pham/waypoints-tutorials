# Where to look, and how to search

Investigate like a humanities expert · Finding information

<!-- Speaker note: Students choose where to look from what the question needs (a count, a rule, a view), name four kinds of source (government, news, specialist report, statistics), and search with a few strong words, an exact phrase and a narrower place. ## Today

- I can say what would answer a question and who would have it.
- I can name four kinds of source and what each is good for.
- I can use a targeted search to locate two relevant sources and record how I found them.

## Why it matters

> You need to know how many students buy from the Parkside tuckshop each day. You could search the internet, ask a friend, or look at the till records. Which first?

<!-- Speaker note: Once you have a question, ask **what would answer it, and who would have it**. A count may be in records or need collecting. A rule needs the relevant authority’s current documents. A view needs the people who hold it; a news report may quote some of them, but cannot represent everyone. Then you search, and a search works best when it is a few strong words. All practice cases, records and addresses in this lesson are invented. Addresses end in `.example`; do not try to visit them. For this case, Parkside keeps anonymised daily buyer counts in its sales records. Ordinary transaction totals do not necessarily count distinct students. -->

**Nib is looking for the daily count. Click the line where Nib's thinking goes wrong.**

```

 4  Nib writes down what the first result says and starts the report.
```

* Answer: line 3

## In each subject

Every subject starts with: what would answer it, and who would have it. The places differ:

| | Usually starts with |
|---|---|
| **Economics** | business and shop records, prices, reports and statistics |
| **Civics** | government websites (rules, decisions), news, specialist reports and statistics |
| **Geography** | maps, statistics, reports and your own fieldwork (planned in Geography's own course) |
| **History** | archives, museum collections and libraries; what counts as a primary or a secondary source is History's own lesson |

## your own

Use your real class topic and teacher-approved search tools or collections. Try an exact phrase, a site limit or a catalogue filter, and note whether it improved the results. For History, locate one primary and one secondary source; for Geography, two secondary sources. In Civics, also name three useful source types and what each provides.

For example, a source note needs: **title; maker; publication date (or “not given”); address or catalogue ID; why it helps**. A search result is a route to a source: open the source itself before recording it. Your teacher can supply an approved collection to search; the `.example` cards above are for practice only.

Write in your own book or document. Your partner or teacher reads it.
