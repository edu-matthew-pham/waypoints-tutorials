# Where to look, and how to search

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** You need to know how many students buy from the Parkside tuckshop each day. You could search the internet, ask a friend, or look at the till records. Which first?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students choose where to look from what the question needs (a count, a rule, a view), name four kinds of source (government, news, specialist report, statistics), and search with a few strong words, an exact phrase and a narrower place. History's primary and secondary sources and Geography's fieldwork are not here; they are in those subjects.

**Students should be able to say:** I can say what would answer a question and who would have it. I can name four kinds of source and what each is good for. I can use a targeted search to locate two relevant sources and record how I found them.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## The idea: strong words, not the first result


:::keep-together

### A strong search

**You want the rules for closing a road. Which search is strongest?**

☐ "road closure" council rules

☐ can you explain to me what a council is and what it does and what rules there are about roads

☐ road

☐ tell me about roads

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** "road closure" council rules

Quote marks keep "road closure" together as a phrase. The other words are the thing (council rules). Your search tool may use different symbols, so look at its help page.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find Nib's mistake

**Nib is looking for the daily count. Click the line where Nib's thinking goes wrong.**

```
 1  Nib needs to know how many students buy from the Parkside tuckshop each day.
 2  Nib types: tuckshops.
 3  Nib says: "The first result is always the best place to look, so I will stop there."
 4  Nib writes down what the first result says and starts the report.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: Nib thinks the first result is always the best place. The right place depends on the question. The count is in the tuckshop's own records, so the first search result is the wrong place to start.

:::


:::keep-together

### Narrowing down

**A search for the Kilby River's water level gives too many pages. Which change helps most?**

☐ Add the water authority's name, or limit the search to its site

☐ Use a shorter word

☐ Click the first result

☐ Add 'please'

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Add the water authority's name, or limit the search to its site

Naming the maker or limiting to one site narrows it to the place that would have the records. Many search tools use site: followed by an address for this.

## In each subject

Every subject starts with: what would answer it, and who would have it. The places differ:

| | Usually starts with |
|---|---|
| **Economics** | business and shop records, prices, reports and statistics |
| **Civics** | government websites (rules, decisions), news, specialist reports and statistics |
| **Geography** | maps, statistics, reports and your own fieldwork (planned in Geography's own course) |
| **History** | archives, museum collections and libraries; what counts as a primary or a secondary source is History's own lesson |

Next: Keep what answers your question.

## your own

Use your real class topic and teacher-approved search tools or collections. Try an exact phrase, a site limit or a catalogue filter, and note whether it improved the results. For History, locate one primary and one secondary source; for Geography, two secondary sources. In Civics, also name three useful source types and what each provides.

For example, a source note needs: **title; maker; publication date (or “not given”); address or catalogue ID; why it helps**. A search result is a route to a source: open the source itself before recording it. Your teacher can supply an approved collection to search; the `.example` cards above are for practice only.

Write in your own book or document. Your partner or teacher reads it.
