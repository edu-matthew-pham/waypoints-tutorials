## 1. Ask a focused civic question

**You will:** Write an open, testable question about a civic institution, rule or current issue that uses civics concepts (such as representation, rights, responsibility or fairness).

### Good looks like

**Characteristics of effective civic inquiry questions**

☐ Identify at least two characteristics of an effective civic inquiry question

☐ Distinguish between a too-broad question and a focused, investigable question and explain the difference

**Developing a key inquiry question**

☐ Develop a focused inquiry question about Australia's political or legal system

☐ Explain what the question is designed to investigate and identify what sources would be useful

**Using current events to generate civic questions**

☐ Identify a current event related to a civic issue and develop a focused question connecting it to a broader civic concept

☐ Explain what makes this question more useful for civic investigation than simply asking 'what happened'

**Need help? Try…**

- Can this be investigated?
- Build a question from a big idea
- Right-sized, and what would answer it

:::teacher-note

Supported on the site by the three Asking questions lessons (Lesson 2's wider step practices turning a current news event into a neutral civic question for `AC9HC7S01 n3`). Help students turn a slogan into a question that evidence can test.

:::


## 2. Find and group civic information

**You will:** Locate relevant civic sources (such as official factsheets, committee submissions or news reports), keep what answers your sub-questions, and group your notes by idea.

### Good looks like

**Identifying and using civics-relevant sources**

☐ Identify at least three source types relevant to civic investigations and explain what each provides

☐ Match a given inquiry question to the most appropriate source type and explain the choice

**Locating information using search strategies**

☐ Use at least one targeted search strategy beyond basic keywords to locate relevant civic information

☐ Identify an appropriate online source for a given civic research question

**Selecting and organising information**

☐ Select information from at least two sources that is relevant to a civic inquiry question

☐ Organise selected information so that similar ideas from different sources are grouped, not just listed by source

**Need help? Try…**

- Where to look, and how to search
- Keep what answers your question
- Organise what you found

:::teacher-note

Supported on the site by the three Finding information lessons. Provide a small curated pack of 2 to 3 contrasting civic sources so students spend their time selecting and grouping rather than cold-searching.

:::


## 3. Compare perspectives on the issue

**You will:** Identify the different perspectives in your sources, explain why groups hold different views on the issue, and note any challenges or disagreements they raise.

### Good looks like

**Identifying perspectives in civic sources**

☐ Identify the perspective represented by a civic source and explain how that perspective might influence the information it contains

☐ Identify at least one perspective that might be absent from a given source

**Comparing and explaining perspective differences**

☐ Compare the perspectives of two sources on the same civic issue and identify at least one point of agreement and one of disagreement

☐ Explain at least one reason why the perspectives differ

**Identifying potential challenges**

☐ Identify at least one potential challenge arising from competing perspectives on a civic issue

☐ Explain what makes the challenge difficult to resolve with reference to the perspectives involved

**Need help? Try…**

- Civics skills: Analysing perspectives and challenges in civic sources *(Coming later)*

:::teacher-note

Class work (Civics perspectives skill lesson is planned). Ask students to identify what each group values or stands to gain or lose, rather than simply labelling a source 'biased'.

:::


## 4. Choose a way to take part

**You will:** Compare at least two democratic participation methods for your issue, weigh their strengths and trade-offs, and choose a strategy that fits your civic goal.

### Good looks like

**Features of civic participation methods**

☐ Identify at least three civic participation methods and describe the key features of each

☐ Explain what makes a particular method appropriate for a specific type of civic goal

**Responsibilities of active citizenship**

☐ Identify at least two responsibilities of active citizenship and explain how they support democratic life

☐ Give an example of active citizenship contributing to social cohesion and explain the connection

**Choosing participation strategies for civic goals**

☐ Explain why a particular civic participation method would be chosen for a specific civic goal

☐ Identify the connection between participation strategy and intended democratic or just outcome

**Need help? Try…**

- Civics and Citizenship › Freedoms and taking part: Ways to take part in democracy *(compares voting, petitions, submissions, community groups and peaceful protest)*
- Civics and Citizenship › Freedoms and taking part: Formal and active citizenship *(distinguishes legal citizenship status from active participation)*
- Civics skills: Choosing a participation strategy *(Coming later)*

:::teacher-note

Supported by Lessons 3 and 4 of Freedoms and taking part (`civics/democracy-7`), with a dedicated strategy-selection lesson planned (`Coming later`). Discuss how matching a method to the right level of government and audience matters more than picking the loudest action.

:::


## 5. Explain and argue your position

**You will:** Write a clear explanation and civic argument that states your position, supports it with at least two distinct evidence-backed points, and uses civics terms accurately.

### Good looks like

**Using civics concepts and terms accurately**

☐ Use at least three civics concepts or terms correctly in a piece of writing about a civic topic

☐ Demonstrate that terms are used to communicate genuine understanding (not superficially) by explaining what each term means in context

**Developing evidence-referenced explanations**

☐ Write an explanation that references at least one piece of specific evidence

☐ Connect the evidence to the explanation — showing why the evidence supports the claim rather than just listing it

**Constructing civic arguments**

☐ Construct a structured civic argument with a clear claim, at least two supporting points, and referenced evidence

☐ Use at least two civics concepts or terms accurately and present ideas appropriate to the intended audience

**Need help? Try…**

- Use the subject's words
- Explain with evidence
- Make an argument

:::teacher-note

Supported on the site by all three Sharing what you found lessons. Check that each student names the specific institution or level of government that has the power to act on their proposal.

:::
