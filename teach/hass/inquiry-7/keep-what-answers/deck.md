# Keep what answers your question

Investigate like a humanities expert · Finding information

<!-- Speaker note: Students keep only what helps answer their question, give a reason that points back to the question, and set the rest aside. Interesting is not relevant. About 40 minutes (Think first and Try 10 min · The idea 10 min · Vary 6 min · Check 5 min · Make 8 min.). -->

---

## Today

- I can decide what to keep from a source by asking whether it helps answer my question.
- I can give a reason for keeping a fact that points back to the question.
- I can set aside facts that are interesting but not relevant.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Economics hinge node: select at least two pieces with a stated reason and set aside what is not relevant. Covered by the Try, The idea and Make steps. The daily counts describe only Monday and Tuesday, not a typical week. Vehicle counts are not counts of customers or people. Keep provenance with notes so claims can be traced later. Civics Make draws on two sources. Reliability of a source is not checked here; that is in Geography's and History's own courses and the Can you trust this source? catch-up. Teacher: Think first: show all five kinds of information in the prompt before collecting predictions. Ask which two answer the question. Teacher: The idea: ask for a reason out loud, then check it points to the question. Teacher: Vary: show the same page, new question. -->

## Why it matters

> You find a page full of facts about school canteens. Some are fascinating. How many of them go in your notes?

<!-- Speaker note: A good source gives you a lot. Your question decides what to keep. For each piece, ask: **does this help answer my question?** If yes, keep it and write why. If not, set it aside, even if it is interesting. All sources here are invented. Addresses end in `.example`; use the supplied cards for practice, then teacher-approved sources for your class topic. Relevance and reliability are different: something can answer your question yet still need checking. -->

## Think first

**Question: How many students buy from the Parkside tuckshop each day? A record lists Monday’s buyer count, Tuesday’s buyer count, the opening year, the most popular item and the number of volunteers. How many directly help?**

- All five, they are all about the tuckshop
- Two
- Whichever is most surprising
- None

<!-- Speaker note: What happens: Two: the daily buyer counts. Only facts that help answer the question about the daily number belong in your notes. The rest may be true and interesting but do not answer it. Take two or three predictions before anyone runs it. -->

## Find Nib's mistake

**Click the line where Nib's thinking goes wrong.**

```
 1  Nib's question: How many students buy from the Parkside tuckshop each day?
 2  Nib finds a page about the history of school canteens, with a lovely photo.
 3  Nib says: "This is interesting, so it must be relevant. I will keep all of it."
 4  Nib pastes the whole page into the notes.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: interesting is not the same as relevant. A history of canteens does not tell Nib how many students buy each day. Keep only what answers the question. -->

## A reason that points back

**You keep 'On Monday 212 students bought something.' Which reason is best?**

- It looks official
- It was near the top of the page
- It is a number
- It is a daily count of students, which is what my question asks

* Answer: It is a daily count of students, which is what my question asks

## In each subject

| | What "relevant" depends on |
|---|---|
| **Economics** | the economic question: costs, choices, who gains or loses (hinge) |
| **Civics** | the civic question: who decides, what rules, whose views |
| **Geography** | the place and the question; reliability is also checked (see Geography's course) |
| **History** | the historical question; checking a source's maker and time is History's own lesson |
