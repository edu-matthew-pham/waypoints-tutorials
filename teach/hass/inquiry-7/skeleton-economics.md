## 1. Ask an investigable question

**You will:** Write a focused question about your class's economic or business issue that uses an economics concept (such as scarcity, opportunity cost, market or business purpose) and can be answered with evidence.

### Good looks like

**What makes a question investigable**

☐ Identifies a question as investigable or not-investigable and gives a reason

☐ Explains at least two features of a good investigable question

**Using economics concepts in question construction**

☐ Develops at least one investigable question that correctly uses an economics or business concept

☐ The question is answerable using accessible data or sources

**Need help? Try…**

- Can this be investigated?
- Build a question from a big idea
- Right-sized, and what would answer it

:::teacher-note

Supported on the site by the three Asking questions lessons. Help students narrow broad 'Should...' questions to a specific local decision or business case with accessible data.

:::


## 2. Find and organise information

**You will:** Locate at least two relevant sources or datasets, keep only the facts and figures that answer your question, and organise them into a clear digital table or graph.

### Good looks like

**Locating sources using targeted search**

☐ Locates at least two relevant sources for an economic topic

☐ Describes the search terms or strategy used and explains why they were chosen

**Selecting relevant information**

☐ Selects at least two pieces of information from a source with a stated reason for relevance

☐ Discards or sets aside information identified as not relevant to the investigation question

**Organising data using appropriate formats**

☐ Organises selected information into an appropriate format using a digital tool

☐ Explains why the chosen format suits the data type

**Need help? Try…**

- Where to look, and how to search
- Keep what answers your question
- Organise what you found

:::teacher-note

Supported on the site by the three Finding information lessons (and ICT's Working with data if students use a spreadsheet). Supply a small starter pack of 2 to 3 local sources or datasets so class time goes into selecting and organising rather than open-ended web searching.

:::


## 3. Make sense of the data

**You will:** Read your table or graph to spot a pattern, trend or cause-and-effect relationship, and point to the exact figures that show it.

### Good looks like

**Identifying economic issues from information**

☐ Identifies an economic or business issue from provided information

☐ Explains the inference made — what in the information pointed to the issue

**Identifying trends using multi-variable data**

☐ Identifies and describes a trend in a multi-variable dataset using a digital tool

☐ Answers a directed question about the trend using evidence from the data

**Cause-and-effect relationships**

☐ Identifies a cause-and-effect relationship in a visual data display

☐ Explains the direction of the relationship using evidence from the data

☐ Uses economic terms (trend, cause, effect, relationship, variable) accurately

**Need help? Try…**

- Economics skills: Read economic data for trends and cause and effect *(Coming later)*

:::teacher-note

Class work (Economics data skills course is planned). Give students one multi-variable table or chart related to your topic and model the difference between describing a number and inferring an economic trend or cause-and-effect link.

:::


## 4. Weigh costs and benefits to choose a response

**You will:** Propose a response to the issue, list both financial and non-financial costs and benefits of your option and an alternative, and explain why your choice comes out ahead.

### Good looks like

**What counts as a cost and a benefit**

☐ Identifies at least one financial and one non-financial cost and benefit for a given decision

☐ Explains why each factor counts as a cost or benefit

**Reasoning from cost-benefit to a response**

☐ Develops a response to an economic issue that identifies costs and benefits of the chosen option

☐ Explains why one alternative was selected over another using cost-benefit reasoning

**Need help? Try…**

- Economics and Business › Scarcity and opportunity cost: Putting it together - scarcity, choice and the future *(applies opportunity cost to a decision)*
- Economics skills: Costs, benefits and choosing a response *(Coming later)*

:::teacher-note

Class work. Lesson 4 of Scarcity and opportunity cost (`economics/scarcity-7/putting-together`) applies opportunity cost to a council decision; a dedicated skill lesson on cost-benefit tables is planned (`Coming later`). Check that students include at least one non-financial cost or benefit (such as time, fairness or environmental impact) alongside dollar figures, name the opportunity cost of their chosen option, and note that in economics weighing costs and benefits across different groups involves a value judgement.

:::


## 5. Explain what you found

**You will:** Write a clear opening for your audience and an explanation that uses economics and business terms accurately and references your data and sources.

### Good looks like

**Orienting an audience to an economic issue**

☐ Writes an opening that names the economic issue and its relevance to the audience

☐ Uses at least two economic or business terms accurately in the opening

**Explaining with data and appropriate formats**

☐ Explains an idea or decision using at least one piece of referenced data or information

☐ Presents data or information in a format appropriate to the explanation and audience

☐ Uses economic and business terms (market, income, goods and services, costs and benefits) accurately throughout

**Need help? Try…**

- Use the subject's words
- Explain with evidence

:::teacher-note

Supported on the site by Sharing what you found (Lessons 7 and 8; Lesson 9 is optional if students are writing a persuasive recommendation). Students write in their own book or document and use the 'Good looks like' criteria below for self- and peer-checking.

:::

☐ A written description and explanation that uses economics and business terms and references your sources.
