# Use the subject's words

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** A paragraph can contain five specialist words and still tell the reader almost nothing.


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students use their subject's terms to describe a case so a reader understands what the issue is and why it matters, rather than listing terms. The cases are invented.

**Students should be able to say:** I can introduce an issue for an audience. I can use subject terms accurately in context. I can name evidence in a historical description.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## The idea: make the words do work

Compare these models with a plain list of words such as 'scarcity, opportunity cost'. Each model tells the reader something about the case.

| Subject | Words used to describe the case |
|---|---|
| Economics | The tuckshop's choice of equipment will change our menu. Because of scarcity, it can't buy everything. If it picks a fridge, the opportunity cost is the oven it gives up. |
| Civics | Riverbend Council is thinking about closing Mill Road. In a consultation, it asks for views before it decides. Parents can participate by sending in comments. |
| Geography | Wattle Flat is downstream of a dam in the Kilby catchment. Because of this interconnection, water kept upstream can change how much reaches the town. |
| History | Catalogue card M1 describes the bronze mirror as an artefact with a polished face. As a source, it tells us about the surface. It can't tell us how everyone lived. |

The History model names its source, which is made up. For real class work, use sources your teacher gives you or approves.

## Check: introduce the economic issue


:::keep-together

**Which opening best introduces the equipment choice to students buying lunch?**

☐ Scarcity means limited resources. Opportunity cost is the next best thing given up.

☐ The money can't buy both machines, so a new fridge means no new oven.

☐ Our menu depends on this choice. Due to scarcity, a fridge costs us the oven.

☐ The tuckshop faces scarcity and opportunity cost when it buys equipment.

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Our menu depends on this choice. Due to scarcity, a fridge costs us the oven.

The best opening says why students should care (the menu), names the choice, and uses both terms.

## Apply: write for a reader

Choose **your current subject’s** case or class topic and write one short opening. Meet that subject’s checklist below; the other cases show the same move and are available for optional practice.

- **Economics:** address Parkside students, name the menu issue and its relevance, and use *scarcity* and *opportunity cost* accurately.
- **Civics:** describe Mill Road using *council*, *consultation* and *participation*, showing what each means here.
- **Geography:** describe Wattle Flat using *catchment*, *upstream* and *downstream*. Explicitly name the concept of *interconnection*.
- **History:** describe the mirror using *artefact* and *source*, name card M1 and what it records. Do not turn an invented record into a fact about Han society.

For your class topic, use the vocabulary and real evidence your teacher has taught. The site does not collect or mark your writing.

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?
