# Build a question from a big idea

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Nib's class is told to write a question using a big idea. Nib writes one word: 'Scarcity.' Is that a question?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students see that a subject's big ideas (scarcity, power, change, cause) are lenses for investigating topics, and practise building a question by naming a case and asking something about the idea that evidence can answer. Geography's and History's hinge nodes (using their concepts as question frames) are covered here in general; each subject's own lesson goes further.

**Students should be able to say:** I can name a big idea from my subject. I can tell a topic from a question. I can build an investigable question from a big idea and a case.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::


## Think first


:::keep-together

**Which of these is a question that uses the big idea of opportunity cost?**

☐ Is the tuckshop a good use of the school's money?

☐ How much is the tuckshop's budget this year?

☐ Opportunity cost at the Parkside tuckshop

☐ What did Parkside's tuckshop miss out on with its new equipment?

My prediction, and why:

{{lines-3}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**What happens:** The question about what the tuckshop missed out on. It names the case and asks what was given up, and you would need evidence to find out. 'Opportunity cost at the Parkside tuckshop' names the idea and the case but asks nothing. The budget is a look-up. 'A good use of money' needs criteria for good.

:::

## The idea: a question, not a topic


:::keep-together

### Make the idea a question

**Which one turns the big idea 'cause' into a question about the bronze mirror?**

☐ What might have caused the mirror's surface to change?

☐ Cause and the bronze mirror

☐ Is the bronze mirror more beautiful than others?

☐ Where in the museum is the mirror kept now?

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** What might have caused the mirror's surface to change?

It names the case, asks why and how, and needs evidence to answer.

:::

 4  Nib says: "That uses the big idea, so it is done."

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: Nib writes the big idea and calls it the question. 'Scarcity' is the topic. A question asks something about the case, such as: what does the tuckshop do when it cannot stock everything?

## In each subject

The move is the same: take a big idea, name a case, ask something about it. The big ideas are what differ.

| | Ask about | A starter that works |
|---|---|---|
| **Economics** | what is given up, who gets what | "When [group] couldn't have everything, what did they give up, and why?" |
| **Civics** | who decides, and by what rules | "Who decides [this], and how?" |
| **Geography** | places, links and changes | "How has [this place] changed, and what changed with it?" |
| **History** | why, what changed, what mattered | "Why did [this] happen, and what changed?" |

History's and Geography's own lessons come back to how to use their ideas well.
