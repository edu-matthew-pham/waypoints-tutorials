## 1. Ask historical questions

**You will:** Write at least two historical inquiry questions about the same topic using different concept frames, such as cause and change. Explain what evidence each needs and keep the scope manageable.

### Good looks like

**What makes a question historical?**

☐ Correctly distinguishes a historical inquiry question from a recall or opinion question.

☐ Identifies at least two characteristics of a good historical inquiry question (investigable, focused on causation/change/significance/perspective).

☐ Can sort a given set of questions into 'suitable for historical inquiry' and 'not suitable', with reasons.

**Developing questions using historical concepts**

☐ Develops at least two historical inquiry questions using different concept frames.

☐ Questions are genuinely investigable using historical evidence — not answerable by simple recall.

☐ Correctly identifies which historical concept (causation, change, perspective, significance) each question draws on.

**Questions that inform inquiry — scope and focus**

☐ Develops a historical inquiry question that is appropriately scoped — investigable, not a simple recall or overly broad question.

☐ Identifies what types of evidence would be needed to investigate the question.

☐ Explains how the question would guide the investigation — what it would require the student to look for.

**Need help? Try…**

- Can this be investigated?
- Build a question from a big idea
- Right-sized, and what would answer it

:::teacher-note

Supported on the site by the three Asking questions lessons. Help students move from simple fact-lookup questions ('When was X built?') to historical questions about purpose, change, significance or evidence.

:::


## 2. Locate and sort primary and secondary sources

**You will:** Find relevant primary sources (from the time being investigated) and secondary sources (later interpretations of that past), keep what answers your question, and organise your source notes clearly.

### Good looks like

**Primary and secondary sources — the disciplinary distinction**

☐ States the correct disciplinary definition of primary and secondary sources (primary = from the time of the event).

☐ Correctly classifies at least four sources, including at least one non-obvious case.

☐ Does not classify based on age alone — recognises that a modern document about a modern event is still a primary source.

**Locating sources — repositories and search strategies**

☐ Successfully locates at least one primary and one secondary source relevant to a given inquiry question.

☐ Identifies the repository or database used and explains why it was appropriate for the source type sought.

☐ Uses subject-specific search terms rather than general keyword searches.

**Sorting and organising sources for inquiry**

☐ Correctly sorts a set of sources into primary and secondary categories.

☐ Identifies at least two sub-categories within primary sources (e.g. artefact, written, visual).

☐ Explains why different source types serve different functions in historical inquiry.

**Need help? Try…**

- History › Source origin and purpose: Primary or secondary? It is about when, not how old *(distinguishes primary from secondary sources for your question)*
- Where to look, and how to search
- Organise what you found

:::teacher-note

Supported on the site by `history/sources-7/primary-or-secondary` and the Finding information lessons. Provide a curated source booklet (artefact photos, translated extracts and a modern historian's summary) suited to your depth study.

:::


## 3. Question the sources (origin, purpose, usefulness and perspectives)

**You will:** Interrogate each source's origin, context and purpose, test its accuracy and usefulness for your specific question, and explain whose perspective is shown or missing.

### Good looks like

**Integrating OCCP — what can this source tell us?**

☐ Applies all four dimensions (origin, content, context, purpose) to a source systematically.

☐ Draws a reasoned conclusion about what the source can and cannot tell us about a specific historical question.

☐ Does not treat OCCP as four separate boxes — synthesises the dimensions into an integrated interpretive conclusion.

**Integrating accuracy and usefulness — evidence assessment**

☐ Assesses both accuracy and usefulness of a source for a specific inquiry question.

☐ Synthesises both dimensions into a reasoned conclusion — not two separate boxes but an integrated assessment.

☐ Explicitly states what the source can and cannot contribute to answering the inquiry question.

**Attitudes and values of the past — reading the world behind the source**

☐ Identifies at least two attitudes or values of the past expressed in a source.

☐ Explains how the source expresses these values — not just states they exist but shows how they appear in the source.

☐ Does not simply judge past attitudes by contemporary standards — demonstrates some attempt to understand them in historical context.

**Need help? Try…**

- History › Source origin and purpose: Who made it, and what does it show?
- History › Source origin and purpose: Why was it made, and what was going on?
- History › Source origin and purpose: What can this source tell us?
- History › Source usefulness: Fact, opinion and accuracy
- History › Source usefulness: Accurate versus useful
- History › Source usefulness: Weighing a source as evidence
- History skills: Past perspectives in sources *(Coming later)*

## 4. Weigh causes, continuity and interpretations

**You will:** Place events in chronological sequence, explain causes, effects and what changed or stayed the same, and compare different historical interpretations of the evidence.

### Good looks like

**Causes and effects — multi-causal historical reasoning**

☐ Identifies at least two causes of a historical event and distinguishes between them (e.g. long-term vs short-term).

☐ Describes at least two effects — short-term and long-term, or immediate and broader consequences.

☐ Does not treat historical events as having a single, obvious cause.

**Continuity and change — explaining what persisted and what shifted**

☐ Identifies at least one continuity and one change within a period and supports each with evidence.

☐ Explains that continuity and change coexist — does not treat them as mutually exclusive.

☐ Uses chronological evidence (dated sources, timelines) to support continuity/change claims rather than asserting them without evidence.

**Developing a historical argument about interpretation**

☐ Develops a reasoned argument — not just a description — about why a historical event or individual is interpreted in different ways.

☐ Identifies at least two interpretations and at least two factors producing the difference.

☐ The argument uses evidence to support its claims, not just assertion.

**Need help? Try…**

- History › How we know: Reading the layers *(uses stratigraphy and physical layers to sequence the past)*
- History › How we know: DNA and dating answer different questions *(checks how dating methods establish chronology)*
- History skills: Causes, continuity and historical interpretations *(Coming later)*

## 5. Build a historical argument

**You will:** Write a historical description, explanation or argument that uses chronological and historical terms accurately, cites primary and secondary sources, and addresses a counter-consideration.

### Good looks like

**Historical description — communicating knowledge with evidence**

☐ Uses at least two subject-specific historical terms correctly in context.

☐ References at least one piece of evidence from a source — naming the source or evidence type.

☐ The description is accurate — it correctly represents historical knowledge without factual errors.

**Historical explanation — reasoning with evidence**

☐ Addresses a 'why' or 'how' question — the text explains, not just describes.

☐ Uses at least one historical concept explicitly in the explanation.

☐ References evidence from sources to support reasoning — evidence is integrated into the argument, not listed separately.

**Historical argument — taking and supporting a position with evidence**

☐ Develops a clear position — an argument, not just a description or explanation — supported by at least two pieces of evidence from sources.

☐ Uses historical knowledge, concepts, and terms accurately throughout.

☐ Acknowledges at least one counter-consideration or alternative view.

**Need help? Try…**

- Use the subject's words
- Explain with evidence
- Make an argument

:::teacher-note

Supported on the site by all three Sharing what you found lessons (`hass/inquiry-7`). Check that students quote or cite specific details from their Stage 3 source evaluations and acknowledge where the surviving evidence is incomplete.

:::
