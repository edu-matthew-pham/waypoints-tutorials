:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students size a question (too big, too small, right) and say what evidence would answer it and where to get it. It also covers starting from a news story (Civics' node, and how Economics investigations often begin). Geography's planning node, with a collection method, is the Make step's table.

**Students should be able to say:** I can tell a question that is too big, too small or the right size. I can say what evidence would answer a question and where to get it. I can turn a news story into a question about a bigger idea.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

**Which question about Mill Road is the right size for a week?**

☐ Is the council good?

☐ How many cars use Mill Road at 8:30 on Monday?

☐ What might closing Mill Road change for students, drivers and shops?

☐ How has every democracy changed throughout history?

My prediction, and why:

{{lines-3}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**What happens:** The question about effects on students, drivers and shops. It names the case and you could gather evidence (a survey, traffic counts, council papers) and weigh it. 'How has every democracy changed throughout history?' covers too many places and periods. One traffic count is a useful supporting measurement, but does not by itself explain the effects. 'Is the council good?' needs criteria and a clearer focus.

:::

## Check: before you move on

A plan should say how the evidence helps, not just name a source. For the made-up river case:

| Evidence needed | Where and how to collect it | How it helps |
|---|---|---|
| Rainfall for the dry summer and a comparison summer | Download monthly records from the water authority | Check whether less rain could help explain lower river levels |
| River levels for the same months | Download readings for the same measuring station | Check when levels fell and compare them with rainfall |

These records could support an explanation; they would not rule out water extraction or dam operations. Plan fieldwork with your teacher; you do not need to visit a river for this task.

:::keep-together

**Which is the best one-line plan for the question 'Why did the Kilby River run low last summer?'**

☐ Ask one student what they think

☐ Look at river height records and rainfall figures for last summer, and a map of what draws water from the river

☐ Read everything about rivers

☐ Look up the river's length

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Look at river height records and rainfall figures for last summer, and a map of what draws water from the river

These records could test possible causes. Include a comparison period and check other influences before concluding why it ran low.

:::


## In each subject

Every subject sizes a question the same way. What each one asks you to plan is a little different:

| | Plan to name | Starts well from |
|---|---|---|
| **Economics** | the data and views you would need (prices, sales, choices) | a news story about a choice or a price |
| **Civics** | the rules, records and views you would need | a news story about a decision or a law |
| **Geography** | the evidence and **how you would collect it** (fieldwork or secondary sources, and the tool you would use) | a map, a place or a change |
| **History** | the kinds of evidence you would need (what was made, what was written) | an object or a source |
