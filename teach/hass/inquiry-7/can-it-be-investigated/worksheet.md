# Can this be investigated?

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** The school tuckshop has room for six items. Which of these questions would give a class something to investigate for a week?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn the test for an investigable question (answering it means gathering evidence and thinking about it) and sort questions from all four Year 7 humanities subjects. The four hinge nodes (one per subject) are the same move, so one lesson serves a History, Geography, Civics or Economics class.

**Students should be able to say:** I can say whether a question can be investigated and give a reason. I can distinguish a main inquiry question from a supporting fact or measurement and a question needing clearer scope or criteria. I can write one investigable question and one that is not, for a topic my class is studying.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::


## Think first


:::keep-together

**Which question about the Parkside tuckshop could a class investigate for a week?**

☐ What is the tuckshop's phone number?

☐ How does the tuckshop decide which six items to stock, and what do students miss out on?

☐ Should schools have tuckshops?

☐ Is the tuckshop good?

My prediction, and why:

{{lines-3}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**What happens:** The question about choosing stock. It asks how and why, so you would gather evidence such as sales records and student views and weigh it. The phone number is one fact. 'Is it good?' needs criteria; 'Should schools have them?' needs a narrower setting and evidence about benefits and costs. Neither is ready as written.

:::

## The idea: what makes it investigable


:::keep-together

### The best reason

**Which of these is the best reason a question is investigable?**

☐ Answering it means gathering evidence and thinking about it

☐ Lots of people have asked it

☐ It has a short answer

☐ It is about the topic

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Answering it means gathering evidence and thinking about it

If you can only answer it by finding evidence and weighing it, it is investigable.

:::

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: Nib thinks a question is great because it is about history and has a clear answer. This particular question asks for one established date; it does not yet ask why, how things changed or how we know. That date could support a larger inquiry.

### Not investigable

**Which question needs clearer criteria and scope before it can guide an inquiry?**

☐ How does the council decide when to close a road?

☐ Which is the best river in the country?

☐ What did people in Han times use bronze mirrors for?

☐ Why did the Kilby River run low last summer?

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Which is the best river in the country?

'Best' for what purpose, and for whom? Criteria such as water quality or access would turn this into a question evidence could help answer.

## In each subject

Every subject wants an investigable question. What each one watches for is a little different:

| | The question usually goes wrong by being | A good one asks |
|---|---|---|
| **Economics** | closed, too broad, or unclear ("Will everyone have enough?") | how people or businesses choose, and what is given up |
| **Civics** | too broad ("What is democracy?") | who decides, how, and with what rules |
| **Geography** | missing a spatial process or relationship to investigate | where, why there, and what changes |
| **History** | plain recall ("When did X happen?") | why it happened, how it changed, how we know |

Next: Build a question from a big idea.
