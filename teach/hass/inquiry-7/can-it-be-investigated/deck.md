# Can this be investigated?

Investigate like a humanities expert · Asking questions

<!-- Speaker note: Students learn the test for an investigable question (answering it means gathering evidence and thinking about it) and sort questions from all four Year 7 humanities subjects. The four hinge nodes (one per subject) are the same move, so one lesson serves a History, Geography, Civics or Economics class. About 40 minutes (Think first and Try 10 min · The idea 10 min · Vary 6 min · Check 5 min · Make 6 min. Typical and wider steps are extras and not counted in this.). -->

## Today

- I can say whether a question can be investigated and give a reason.
- I can distinguish a main inquiry question from a supporting fact or measurement and a question needing clearer scope or criteria.
- I can write one investigable question and one that is not, for a topic my class is studying.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: This is a hinge lesson in every subject: the next lessons (building and sizing a question) only work if students can already tell a look-up from an investigation. Spend time here. The cases are made up (Parkside School tuckshop, Riverbend Council and Mill Road, Wattle Flat and the Kilby River, the Hartley Museum's bronze mirror). The sort mixes all four on purpose, so students see the same four kinds in each subject. The Han mirror is a stand-in for any museum object; the lesson claims nothing about real objects. The sorting categories are teaching tools, not rigid definitions. Counts are investigable data-collection questions; a definition may be a useful starting point. Evidence-informed judgements about fairness or value are legitimate if students state criteria and recognise different perspectives. Do not imply that inquiry requires agreement or that future questions cannot use evidence. Use it before or at the start of any unit that ends in an investigation. Students who have done the Year 6 catch-up Questions that guide an inquiry (in Before Year 7: HASS) will find it easier. For Geography, use the Make prompt to transform a general question and explain its spatial focus. For History, ask students to explain how their question investigates cause, change, significance or perspective. The source-node tags identify practice opportunities; an objective selection is not evidence that a student can formulate their own question. Teacher: Think first: ask for votes before the answer. Many students pick 'Is the tuckshop good?' because it feels like the real question. Teacher: Try: stop after the sort and ask which subject each investigable question belongs to. ## Why it matters

> The school tuckshop has room for six items. Which of these questions would give a class something to investigate for a week?

<!-- Speaker note: A useful **inquiry question** is focused enough to investigate with available evidence and gives you something to work out. A quick look-up can help, but usually cannot carry the whole inquiry. Counts and measurements are investigable supporting questions: you may need to collect new data to answer them. Judgements can use evidence too. “Is the tuckshop good?” needs clearer criteria: good for whom, and in what way? “How well does the menu meet students’ needs within its budget?” gives you evidence to gather and a judgement to explain. People may still weigh that evidence differently. The cases in this lesson are made up: the Parkside School tuckshop, Riverbend Council and Mill Road, the town of Wattle Flat on the Kilby River, and a bronze mirror in the Hartley Museum. -->

## Think first

**Which question about the Parkside tuckshop could a class investigate for a week?**

- How does the tuckshop decide which six items to stock, and what do students miss out on?
- Should schools have tuckshops?
- Is the tuckshop good?

## Not investigable

**Which question needs clearer criteria and scope before it can guide an inquiry?**

- How does the council decide when to close a road?
- Which is the best river in the country?
- What did people in Han times use bronze mirrors for?
- Why did the Kilby River run low last summer?

* Answer: Which is the best river in the country?

<!-- Speaker note: 'Best' for what purpose, and for whom? Criteria such as water quality or access would turn this into a question evidence could help answer. -->

## The test

**Which question is investigable?**

- Why did the Kilby River run low last summer?
- How long is the Kilby River?
- Is the Kilby River nice?
- What is a river?

* Answer: Why did the Kilby River run low last summer?

<!-- Speaker note: It asks why and needs evidence to answer. The others are a supporting measurement, a preference needing criteria and a definition; each can be developed or contribute to a larger inquiry. Ask on mini whiteboards or by show of hands before the reveal. -->

## In each subject

Every subject wants an investigable question. What each one watches for is a little different:

| | The question usually goes wrong by being | A good one asks |
|---|---|---|
| **Economics** | closed, too broad, or unclear ("Will everyone have enough?") | how people or businesses choose, and what is given up |
| **Civics** | too broad ("What is democracy?") | who decides, how, and with what rules |
| **Geography** | missing a spatial process or relationship to investigate | where, why there, and what changes |
| **History** | plain recall ("When did X happen?") | why it happened, how it changed, how we know |

## Exit ticket

**How can you tell if a question can be investigated?**

* Answering it means gathering evidence and thinking about it. A useful main question has focus and accessible evidence. Supporting facts and measurements help; a judgement needs explicit criteria.
