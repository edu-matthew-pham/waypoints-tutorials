Responding to an economic issue · Reading economic data

## Today

**We are learning to** identify a cause-and-effect relationship in data, naming the cause, the effect and the economic reason, and say why a pattern alone doesn't prove it.

- I can read a pattern in a graph and use it to predict.
- I can name the cause, the effect and the because.
- I can explain why two things going together doesn't prove one causes the other.

## Why it matters

> In Riverbend, households that earn more also tend to save more. Does earning more cause the saving, or does saving cause the earning?

<!-- Speaker note: Riverbend is a made-up town, and this is made-up data. When two things change together, there may be a **cause-and-effect relationship**: a change in one (the **cause**) leads to a change in the other (the **effect**). But two things going together doesn't prove one causes the other. To explain a cause-and-effect relationship, give all three parts: - **The cause:** what changes first. - **The effect:** what changes because of it. - **The because:** the economic reason that links them. For example: "When a household's income rises, its saving can rise, **because** there is more left over after paying for needs." This isn't only for economics: in science, in the news and in persuasive writing, two things going together doesn't prove one causes the other. -->

## Cause, effect and because

**Which sentence explains the relationship with a cause, an effect and a because?**

- Income and saving both go up together across the graph
- More income can lead to more saving, because more is left after needs
- Saving goes up, so income goes up, because the dots go up
- Some households save more than others, so income varies

* Answer: More income can lead to more saving, because more is left after needs
