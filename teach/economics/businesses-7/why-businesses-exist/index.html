Goods, services and why businesses exist

Students distinguish goods from services and learn that businesses exist for several reasons — providing goods and services, making a profit, creating employment and meeting community needs. This is the hinge lesson for AC9HE7K02: if students think profit is the only reason a business exists, cooperatives and not-for-profits in lesson 2 will not make sense.

Students will be able to

  • I can explain the difference between a good and a service, with an example of each.
  • I can give at least two reasons why businesses exist beyond making a profit.
  • I can explain why a community organisation that sells goods or services counts as a business.

Timing

Predict and Try 8 min · Worked example 12 min · Vary 8 min · Practise 6 min · Make 6 min. Typical and wider steps are extras and not counted in this.

Before class

This is a hinge lesson. Lesson 2 introduces cooperatives and not-for-profit organisations alongside sole proprietorships and corporations. Students who hold onto the idea that every business exists only for owner profit will misclassify those structures.

All businesses in the lesson (Corner Bakery, Pedal Fix Workshop, Riverbend Pantry) are made up so nothing dates and no real brand is advertised. When students write their own examples in the Make step, either real local types (a chemist, a hairdresser) or made-up ones are fine.

Running the lesson

  1. Predict: take a quick vote before locking in. Many students will pick the profit-only option.
  2. Try: pause on the bike workshop and the cafe. Ask what you can touch and take home (the good) and what is done for you (the service).
  3. Worked example: let students read the three town businesses before looking at Nib's lines. Use the Build up to it check if anyone says Riverbend Pantry isn't a real business.
  4. Vary: ask pairs to name one business in town that has at least three reasons for existing.
  5. Make: students write in their own book and check with a partner.

In class

On the slides at these points. Students can do the whole lesson on their own; these are for the room. In the deck, the Run, Modify and Make slides have the live playground in them (the tutorial link is the backup), and the notes above are in each slide's speaker notes.

  • Think, pair, share (start)
    A community pantry sells groceries, rents a shop and pays two workers, but any spare money at the end of the year is used to lower food prices rather than paid to an owner. One student says it is a business; another says it isn't because nobody takes a profit. Who do you agree with, and why?
    • What does the pantry do each day that looks just like the bakery next door?
    • If profit were the only reason to start a business, why would anyone set up a cooperative or a community bus?

    Keep the focus on the multiple reasons businesses exist: providing goods and services, creating jobs, meeting community needs, and making a profit.

  • Good, service or both? (run, 4 min)

    Call out familiar places (a dentist, a bookshop, a pizzeria, a lawn-mowing round, an optometrist). Students hold up one finger for a good, two for a service, or both hands for both. Ask one student from 'both' to separate the physical item from the work done.

    The 'both' cases stop students thinking every business is purely one or the other.

  • More than one reason (make, 4 min)

    Ask pairs to share one business from their book and name at least two different reasons it exists (one for the owner or workers, one for customers or the town).

    Check that students name at least two distinct reasons beyond profit.

  • Beyond profit (investigate)
    Which of these is a reason a business exists, other than making a profit?
    Providing employment and meeting a community need
    Businesses also exist to provide goods and services, create jobs and serve their community.

The hard part (on purpose)

Students often treat 'business' as a word for 'making money for an owner'. Keep pointing back to what the organisation actually does every day: does it provide goods or services, employ people, and pay its costs?

When students get stuck

What you will seeA question to askThe fix
A student says a hairdresser or vet is not a business because nothing is made in a factory.What are you paying for when you go there?You are paying for their skill and time — a service. Businesses provide goods, services, or both.
A student says a not-for-profit or cooperative cannot pay wages.Is paying a worker for their work the same thing as handing profit to an owner?No. Wages are a cost of running the business. 'Not-for-profit' means any surplus left after costs is put back into the mission, not paid out to private owners.
A student lists 'making money' and 'making a profit' as two different reasons.Besides money for the owner, what does the business do for customers or workers?Prompt for providing goods and services, creating jobs (employment), or meeting a community need.

Answers to the checks

  1. why do they exist?: Which of these is true about why businesses exist?
    Businesses exist for several reasons: providing goods and services, making a profit, creating employment, and meeting community needs. Some do not aim to make a profit for an owner at all.
  2. Try: goods, services or both?: What is each business mainly providing: a good, a service, or both?
    A bakery selling loaves of bread → Good; A dog-walking business that takes pets to the park → Service; A bike workshop that fits a new tyre and tunes the brakes → Both; A shoe shop selling school shoes → Good; A swimming instructor teaching a Saturday class → Service; A cafe making hot lunches and serving them at tables → Both
  3. Three businesses in Mia's town: In Mia's town, Riverbend Pantry sells fresh food at low prices, pays two shop workers, and puts any spare money back into keeping prices down. Which two reasons explain why it exists?
    To provide goods to the community and create employment
    Riverbend Pantry provides goods a community needs and employs staff, even though it does not run for an owner's profit.
  4. Find Nib's mistake: Nib is explaining the businesses in Mia's town. Click the line where Nib's thinking goes wrong.
    line 3
    Line 3: Nib says a business only exists to make a profit for its owner. Businesses also exist to provide goods and services, create jobs and meet community needs. Not-for-profits and cooperatives are still businesses when they provide goods or services.
  5. Why the reason matters: Why does it matter for the rest of this course that businesses can exist for reasons beyond profit?
    Because some business types, such as cooperatives and not-for-profits, are set up around those other reasons
    Once you see that purpose varies, the different business structures in the next lesson make sense.
  6. Match each reason: Match each reason a business exists to the example that shows it best.
    Provide goods and services → A. A hardware store stocks timber, screws and paint so residents can repair their homes; Make a profit → D. An owner invests savings into a cafe hoping to earn more than the costs of running it; Provide employment → B. A town bakery trains and pays four apprentices from the local high school; Meet a community need → C. A community-owned bus service runs a Tuesday route so older residents can reach the clinic
  7. Dev's repair stall: Dev opens a mobile phone repair stall. He wants to earn a living, give his cousin a Saturday job, and stop broken phones going to landfill. How many reasons for existing does Dev's business have?
    Several: earning a profit, providing a repair service, creating a job, and helping the community
    Most real businesses combine several reasons at once.
  8. What it provides and why: Sort each statement into whether it describes what the business provides (Good or Service) or why it exists (Purpose).
    Fresh apples boxed at the orchard → Good; Delivering boxes to customers' doors → Service; Giving steady work to six local pickers → Purpose; Earning a return for the orchard owners → Purpose
  9. Sam's rule about factories: Sam says: 'The local vet clinic doesn't make anything in a factory, so it isn't a real business.' What is the best reply?
    It is a business that provides a service — checking and treating animals — and goods are not the only thing businesses sell
    Businesses provide goods, services, or both. Treating animals is a service.
  10. Typical: how goods and services reach people: Match each way of providing goods and services to what makes it useful.
    Saturday growers' market → D. Growers sell fresh produce straight to locals once a week with low stall costs; Suburban shopping centre → A. Many stores share one roof and car park so customers can buy lots of things in one trip; Online store → B. Customers order from home at any hour and goods are posted or downloaded; Small independent store → C. A single local shop offers specialist advice and knows its regular customers
  11. Wider: a remote community store: A store in a remote town 500 km from the nearest city is the only place to buy fresh fruit, medicine and fuel, and roads flood in the wet season. How does its purpose differ from one of twenty supermarkets in a big city?
    Keeping essentials available and affordable for the community is vital, because residents cannot simply walk to another shop
    Where a store is the community's lifeline, reliable supply of essentials and community wellbeing sit at the centre of why it exists.

Stretch and support

Stretch: Think of a business that provides both a good and a service (such as a bike shop or an optometrist). Which half — the good or the service — would be harder for an online-only store to replace, and why?

Support: Two prompt questions on the board: '1. Can I touch it and take it home (good), or is someone doing work for me (service)? 2. Who benefits from this business existing — owners, workers, customers, or the wider community?'

Exit ticket

Give two reasons why businesses exist other than making a profit, and name one business that provides a service.

Reasons include providing goods and services, creating employment (jobs), and meeting a community need. Service examples include a hairdresser, vet clinic, bike repairer or bus service.

What each step is for

  • why do they exist?: Committing first surfaces the common belief that a business only counts if someone is trying to make a profit.
  • Try: goods, services or both?: Sorting everyday examples separates physical goods from actions done for a customer.
  • Worked example: more than profit: Looking at three made-up businesses in Mia's town shows that profit is one reason a business exists, not the only one. Nib's profit-only rule is planted as the mistake.
  • Vary: match the reason: A single business often has more than one reason for existing, and the main reason shapes what it does.
  • goods, services and reasons: Applying both checks together — what it provides and why it exists — builds fluency.
  • two businesses near you: Naming one goods-providing and one service-providing business and explaining why each exists turns the terms into your own.
  • Typical: how goods and services reach people: Businesses reach customers in different ways — markets, shopping centres, independent shops, online and remote stores — and each way suits different needs.
  • Wider: a remote community store: In a remote community, the local store is often the only source of fresh food and everyday essentials for hundreds of kilometres, so community health and steady supply matter alongside covering costs.
