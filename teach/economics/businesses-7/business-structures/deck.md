## Today

**We are learning to** distinguish six business structures by who owns them, who decides, and where any surplus goes.

- I can name at least three business structures and state the feature that sets each apart.
- I can match a realistic local business to its ownership structure.
- I can explain why the number of staff in a shop does not tell you its ownership structure.

## Why it matters

> In Mia's town, one baker owns the corner bakery alone, two vets own the animal clinic together, and twenty apple growers own the packing shed together. Why didn't they all set up the same way?

<!-- Speaker note: Last lesson showed that businesses exist for different reasons. They also have different **business structures**: the legal and practical way a business is owned and run. Six structures come up across Australia (all businesses named in this lesson — Rosa's Car Yard, Priya & Tom Vets, Valley Growers' Shed, Made-Up Grocers Corp and Burger Barn — are made up): - **Sole proprietorship:** owned and run by one person (a **sole proprietor**), who makes the decisions and keeps any profit (and carries the losses). - **Partnership:** owned by two or more people who share the skills, decisions, profits and responsibilities. - **Cooperative:** owned and run by its members (such as growers, workers or local shoppers) to meet their shared needs; members each get a say. - **Corporation:** a separate legal body owned by **shareholders**, often used by larger businesses such as a national supermarket chain. - **Franchise:** an independent business owner (**franchisee**) pays to use an established brand's name, products and system (**franchisor**). - **Not-for-profit organisation:** exists for a community, charitable or social purpose; any surplus left after costs goes back into that purpose, not to private owners. -->

## Cooperative or corporation?

**Which feature belongs to a cooperative rather than a corporation?**

- It is owned by the members who use or work in it, with each member having an equal say
- It is owned by one person alone who keeps all the profit
- It is owned by outside shareholders who vote according to how many shares they hold
- It pays a franchise fee to copy a national chain's brand and menu

* Answer: It is owned by the members who use or work in it, with each member having an equal say

## Why form a partnership?

**Why might a plumber choose to join a partnership with an electrician rather than stay a sole proprietor?**

- Because in a partnership, one partner can make every big decision without consulting the other
- Because a sole proprietor is not allowed to employ staff or apprentices
- They can combine two sets of skills, share the cost of tools, and cover for each other
- Because a partnership does not have to pay for tools or materials

* Answer: They can combine two sets of skills, share the cost of tools, and cover for each other

## Where the surplus goes

**What is the key difference between a corporation and a not-for-profit organisation when money is left over after paying all bills and wages?**

- A corporation can pay profits to shareholders, while a not-for-profit must reinvest its surplus into its community purpose
- A corporation must reinvest all surplus into charity, while a not-for-profit pays dividends to shareholders
- A corporation can pay wages to staff, while a not-for-profit can only use unpaid volunteers
- A corporation can sell physical goods, while a not-for-profit can only provide services

* Answer: A corporation can pay profits to shareholders, while a not-for-profit must reinvest its surplus into its community purpose

<!-- Speaker note: The difference is what happens to the surplus: shareholder return versus community mission. -->
