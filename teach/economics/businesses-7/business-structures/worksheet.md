# Ways a business can be set up

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** In Mia's town, one baker owns the corner bakery alone, two vets own the animal clinic together, and twenty apple growers own the packing shed together. Why didn't they all set up the same way?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn the distinguishing features of six business types — sole proprietorship, partnership, cooperative, corporation, franchise and not-for-profit — and match local examples to each.

**Students should be able to say:** I can name at least three business structures and state the feature that sets each apart. I can match a realistic local business to its ownership structure. I can explain why the number of staff in a shop does not tell you its ownership structure.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: matching town businesses


:::keep-together

### How ownership works in town

1. $\text{Rosa's Car Yard}$ — One owner (Rosa) who employs six staff -> Sole proprietorship.
2. $\text{Priya & Tom Vets}$ — Two owners sharing skills, costs and profits -> Partnership.
3. $\text{Valley Growers' Shed}$ — Owned by 20 member farms for shared packing -> Cooperative.
4. $\text{Made-Up Grocers Corp}$ — Owned by thousands of shareholders across Australia -> Corporation.

Now: how do you tell a partnership from a sole proprietorship or a cooperative?

**Two qualified vets, Priya and Tom, pool their savings to open a clinic together and share all decisions and profits. What type of business is their clinic?**

☐ A partnership

☐ A sole proprietorship

☐ A franchise

☐ A not-for-profit organisation

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** A partnership

Two or more people sharing ownership, decisions and profits is a partnership.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find Nib's mistake

**Nib is classifying two businesses in Mia's town. Click the line where Nib's thinking goes wrong.**

```
 1  Rosa owns the town car yard by herself and pays six salespeople to help on the lot.
 2  Across the road, twenty dairy farmers co-own the Valley Growers' Shed, and two staff work inside it.
 3  Nib says: "You can tell the business structure just by counting how many people work in the building."
 4  Nib says: "So Rosa's car yard must be a corporation because seven people work there, and the shed is a partnership of two."
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: Nib judges the structure by counting workers in the room instead of asking who owns the business. The car yard has one owner (sole proprietor) who employs staff; the growers' shed is owned by twenty member farms (a cooperative).

:::

### The valley farmers' shed

**Twenty dairy farmers in a valley each put in money to own a milk-chilling shed together so every member farm can store its milk and each member gets one vote. Which structure is this?**

☐ A franchise

☐ A cooperative

☐ A sole proprietorship

☐ A corporation owned by outside shareholders

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** A cooperative

A business owned and run by its members to serve their shared need, with one vote per member, is a cooperative.

## Compare two structures


:::keep-together

### Why form a partnership?

**Why might a plumber choose to join a partnership with an electrician rather than stay a sole proprietor?**

☐ Because in a partnership, one partner can make every big decision without consulting the other

☐ Because a sole proprietor is not allowed to employ staff or apprentices

☐ They can combine two sets of skills, share the cost of tools, and cover for each other

☐ Because a partnership does not have to pay for tools or materials

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** They can combine two sets of skills, share the cost of tools, and cover for each other

A partnership brings extra skills and shared costs, in exchange for sharing decisions and profits.

:::


:::keep-together

### Where the surplus goes

**What is the key difference between a corporation and a not-for-profit organisation when money is left over after paying all bills and wages?**

☐ A corporation can pay profits to shareholders, while a not-for-profit must reinvest its surplus into its community purpose

☐ A corporation must reinvest all surplus into charity, while a not-for-profit pays dividends to shareholders

☐ A corporation can pay wages to staff, while a not-for-profit can only use unpaid volunteers

☐ A corporation can sell physical goods, while a not-for-profit can only provide services

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** A corporation can pay profits to shareholders, while a not-for-profit must reinvest its surplus into its community purpose

The difference is what happens to the surplus: shareholder return versus community mission.
