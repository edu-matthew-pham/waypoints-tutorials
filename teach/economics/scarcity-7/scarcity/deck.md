# Scarcity - not enough for everything

Scarcity and opportunity cost · Scarcity

<!-- Speaker note: Students learn that scarcity is the lasting gap between unlimited wants and limited land, labour and capital, and that a shortage is a short-term run-out that more stock fixes. They then use the three allocation questions (what, how, for whom) on made-up places. This is the second hinge lesson: if students leave thinking scarcity means 'it ran out', opportunity cost and sustainability will not make sense. ## Today

**We are learning to** explain economic scarcity, tell it apart from a temporary shortage, and apply the three allocation questions.

- I can explain scarcity and say how it differs from a shortage.
- I can name land, labour and capital in an example.
- I can sort a decision under what, how or for whom.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: This is a hinge lesson. The idea students must leave with is that scarcity is permanent and comes from limited resources meeting unlimited wants. Do not ask students what their own families can or cannot afford. The Make step asks about scarcity at school, not at home. Water counts as land (a natural resource) in this lesson. ## Why it matters

> The school tuckshop has one oven, two helpers and a Friday lunch rush. Everyone wants a hot pie. What happens, and is that the same as 'running out'?

<!-- Speaker note: Last lesson showed that wants never run out. This lesson asks what happens when those wants meet a limited amount of stuff. **Scarcity** is the problem that there are not enough resources to make everything people want. **Resources** are the things used to make goods and services: land and natural things (**land**), people's work (**labour**) and tools and equipment (**capital**). Scarcity is always there, even when the shelves are full. That is the difference from a **shortage**, which is a shop running out of something for a short time. -->

## Shortage or scarcity

Now: what is the difference between a shortage and scarcity?

$$\text{Wants}$$

1) $\text{Resources}$ — One oven (capital), two helpers (labour), a limited amount of ingredients (land).
2) $\text{Compare}$ — The wants are larger than what the resources can make.
3) $\text{So}$ — That gap is scarcity. Running out for a day is only how it can show up.

## Shortage or scarcity

**The tuckshop runs out of pies today and gets a new batch tomorrow. What is the best way to describe that?**

- Scarcity, because pies ran out
- A shortage: it is short-term and more stock fixes it
- Neither, because the pies sold well
- A want that was met

* Answer: A shortage: it is short-term and more stock fixes it

<!-- Speaker note: It is a shortage. A shortage is a short-term run-out that can be fixed by getting more. Scarcity is bigger: it is there on the days the shelves are full as well. -->

## Find Nib's mistake

**Nib is explaining the pies. Click the line where Nib's thinking goes wrong.**

```
 1  The tuckshop ran out of pies at 11:10 today. A new batch comes in the morning.
 2  Dev says: "There's never enough pies. That's scarcity."
 3  Nib says: "Scarcity just means something ran out, so the tuckshop only needs to order more."
 4  Nib says: "Once they order more, scarcity is gone for good."
```

* Answer: line 4

<!-- Speaker note: Line 4: ordering more pies fixes the shortage, but not scarcity. The oven can still bake only so many pies in a day, and the helpers can only serve so many. Scarcity remains because the resources are limited. -->

## Decide: what, how and for whom

Because there is not enough for everything, the tuckshop has to decide three things. These are the **allocation questions**.

- **What** to make: pies, wraps or salads?
- **How** to make it: using more helpers, or a bigger oven?
- **For whom**: who gets the pies first, and who waits?

## A town with full shelves

**A rich town has shops with full shelves every day. Is there scarcity there?**

- Only in poor towns
- Yes: the land, workers and equipment are still limited, and people still want more than can be made
- Only on days when a shop sells out
- No, because the shelves are full

* Answer: Yes: the land, workers and equipment are still limited, and people still want more than can be made

## Selling out

**A bakery has a big sale and sells out by noon. Next morning it is full again. This is...**

- Proof there is no scarcity
- A shortage that was fixed with more stock
- Scarcity that has gone away
- A want that never existed

* Answer: A shortage that was fixed with more stock

<!-- Speaker note: The run-out is a shortage. The bakery is still limited by its ovens and workers, which is scarcity. -->

## Exit ticket

**The tuckshop runs out of pies, then orders more. Is scarcity gone? Why?**
