# Scarcity - not enough for everything

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** The school tuckshop has one oven, two helpers and a Friday lunch rush. Everyone wants a hot pie. What happens, and is that the same as 'running out'?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn that scarcity is the lasting gap between unlimited wants and limited land, labour and capital, and that a shortage is a short-term run-out that more stock fixes. They then use the three allocation questions (what, how, for whom) on made-up places. This is the second hinge lesson: if students leave thinking scarcity means 'it ran out', opportunity cost and sustainability will not make sense.

**Students should be able to say:** I can explain scarcity and say how it differs from a shortage. I can name land, labour and capital in an example. I can sort a decision under what, how or for whom.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: shortage or scarcity


:::keep-together

### Shortage or scarcity

1. $\text{Wants}$ — Every student wants a hot pie on Friday, and next week they will want something more.
2. $\text{Resources}$ — One oven (capital), two helpers (labour), a limited amount of ingredients (land).
3. $\text{Compare}$ — The wants are larger than what the resources can make.
4. $\text{So}$ — That gap is scarcity. Running out for a day is only how it can show up.

Now: what is the difference between a shortage and scarcity?

**The tuckshop runs out of pies today and gets a new batch tomorrow. What is the best way to describe that?**

☐ Scarcity, because pies ran out

☐ A shortage: it is short-term and more stock fixes it

☐ Neither, because the pies sold well

☐ A want that was met

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** A shortage: it is short-term and more stock fixes it

It is a shortage. A shortage is a short-term run-out that can be fixed by getting more. Scarcity is bigger: it is there on the days the shelves are full as well.

:::


:::keep-together

### Why scarcity stays

**Which best explains why scarcity is always there?**

☐ Prices are too high

☐ Shops forget to order enough

☐ People waste money

☐ Wants are unlimited, but resources are limited

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Wants are unlimited, but resources are limited

Unlimited wants meet limited land, labour and capital, so there is never enough for everything.

## Decide: what, how and for whom

Because there is not enough for everything, the tuckshop has to decide three things. These are the **allocation questions**.

- **What** to make: pies, wraps or salads?
- **How** to make it: using more helpers, or a bigger oven?
- **For whom**: who gets the pies first, and who waits?

## Vary: a new place


:::keep-together

### A town with full shelves

**A rich town has shops with full shelves every day. Is there scarcity there?**

☐ Only in poor towns

☐ Yes: the land, workers and equipment are still limited, and people still want more than can be made

☐ Only on days when a shop sells out

☐ No, because the shelves are full

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Yes: the land, workers and equipment are still limited, and people still want more than can be made

Every town has limited land, labour and capital, and wants keep growing. Full shelves show that this week's shortage is fixed, not that scarcity is gone.

:::


:::keep-together

### Selling out

**A bakery has a big sale and sells out by noon. Next morning it is full again. This is...**

☐ Proof there is no scarcity

☐ A shortage that was fixed with more stock

☐ Scarcity that has gone away

☐ A want that never existed

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** A shortage that was fixed with more stock

The run-out is a shortage. The bakery is still limited by its ovens and workers, which is scarcity.
