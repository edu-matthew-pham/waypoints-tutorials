# Opportunity cost - what you give up

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Mia has a free Saturday and $20. She could go to the pool, buy a book or save the money. She picks the pool. What did that choice cost her, apart from the ticket?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn that opportunity cost is the next best alternative given up, and not the price paid or everything else. They find it for time, money and land choices. Width is the point here: the same idea in several settings is what makes it transfer.

**Students should be able to say:** I can say what opportunity cost is in my own words. I can find the next best alternative in a choice. I can explain why the price is not the opportunity cost.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

☐ The $8 ticket

☐ The book and the savings

☐ Nothing, because she got what she chose

My prediction, and why:

{{lines-3}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**What happens:** The book. It was her next best choice, and she gave it up. The $8 is the price, not the opportunity cost, and the savings was a worse option than the book.

## Worked example: price or opportunity cost


:::keep-together

### Mia's choice

1. $\text{List the options}$ — Pool, book, saving.
2. $\text{Rank them}$ — Pool first (chosen), book second, saving third.
3. $\text{Find the next best}$ — The book.
4. $\text{So}$ — The opportunity cost of the pool is the book, not the $8 and not everything else.

Now: how do you find the opportunity cost of a choice?

**Mia picks the pool. Her ranking was pool, then book, then saving. What did she give up?**

☐ Nothing

☐ The book, her next best option

☐ The $8 ticket price

☐ Everything else she could have done

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** The book, her next best option

The opportunity cost is the next best alternative, the book.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find Nib's mistake

**Nib is working out Mia's opportunity cost. Click the line where Nib's thinking goes wrong.**

```
 1  Mia has $20 and a free Saturday. She can buy a book, go to the pool, or save the money.
 2  She picks the pool, which costs $8.
 3  Nib says: "So Mia's opportunity cost is the $8 she paid."
 4  Nib says: "Or maybe it's everything she didn't do: the book, the savings and the sleep-in."
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: the $8 is the price Mia paid. Opportunity cost is what she gave up, the book. Line 4 makes a second mistake: it counts everything she did not do, when only the next best option counts.

:::


:::keep-together

### Only the next best

**Why is the opportunity cost not 'the book, the savings and the sleep-in'?**

☐ Only the next best option counts, because you could only have chosen one

☐ Because opportunity cost is always money

☐ Because those are cheap

☐ Because Mia did not want them

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Only the next best option counts, because you could only have chosen one

You could only have made one different choice, so the cost is the best of the ones you did not pick.
