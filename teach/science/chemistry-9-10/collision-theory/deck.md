# Why does heat speed up reactions?

Years 9 and 10 Chemistry · Reaction rates

## Today

**We are learning to** particles react only when they collide with enough energy (the activation energy). Collision theory explains why temperature, concentration, surface area and catalysts change the rate of a reaction.

- I can explain that particles react only when they collide with at least the activation energy.
- I can explain why more frequent or more energetic collisions make a reaction faster.
- I can name and explain at least two ways to increase the rate.

## Why it matters

> Milk goes off in days on the bench but lasts a week in the fridge. A glow stick in hot water glows brighter but fades sooner. Same idea: how often, and how hard, particles collide.

## Why most collisions fail

**Most collisions on the board did not make a reaction. Why not?**

- The particles were too far apart
- They didn't have enough energy: they were below the activation energy
- Only the first collision of each particle counts
- The particles were the wrong colour

* Answer: They didn't have enough energy: they were below the activation energy

<!-- Speaker note: A collision reacts only if it has at least the activation energy. The rest bounce off. -->

## What heating does

**What happens when the temperature goes up?**

Groups: True · False

## The bigger effect

**A 100 °C rise doesn't make the particles collide 10 times more often, yet the reaction can go 10 times faster. What explains the rest?**

- The container gets bigger
- The particles stick together for longer
- Many more collisions have enough energy to react

* Answer: Many more collisions have enough energy to react

<!-- Speaker note: Temperature changes how hard particles hit far more than how often. That is why heat has such a big effect. -->

## A fair test

**You want to test how temperature changes the rate of marble chips reacting with acid. Which plan is a fair test?**

- Heat the acid and use stronger acid too, so the difference is bigger
- Change only the temperature, keep the acid and the chips the same, time the fizzing, and repeat each temperature
- Do one run at each temperature: one result is enough

* Answer: Change only the temperature, keep the acid and the chips the same, time the fizzing, and repeat each temperature

## Four factors

**Each change speeds up a reaction. Match it to the reason, in collision terms.**

| | |
|---|---|
| `Higher temperature` | A. It lowers the activation energy, so a larger share of collisions succeed |
| `Higher concentration` | B. Particles move faster: they collide more often and more of the collisions have enough energy |
| `Larger surface area (smaller pieces)` | C. More reacting particles in the same space, so they collide more often |
| `A catalyst` | D. More particles of the solid are exposed, so more collisions can happen at once |

* Answer: Higher temperature → B. Particles move faster: they collide more often and more of the collisions have enough energy; Higher concentration → C. More reacting particles in the same space, so they collide more often; Larger surface area (smaller pieces) → D. More particles of the solid are exposed, so more collisions can happen at once; A catalyst → A. It lowers the activation energy, so a larger share of collisions succeed

<!-- Speaker note: Every factor works through collisions: either more of them each second, or a bigger share with enough energy. -->

## Why faster

**The concentrated reaction reached the target faster. Why?**

- More particles in the same space collide more often
- Each collision had more energy
- The activation energy was lower

* Answer: More particles in the same space collide more often

## Surface area

**A lump of chalk reacts slowly with acid; chalk powder fizzes fast. Which explanation fits collision theory?**

- Powder exposes far more particles to the acid, so there are more collisions each second
- Powder is a different substance
- Powder particles have more energy

* Answer: Powder exposes far more particles to the acid, so there are more collisions each second

<!-- Speaker note: Surface area, like concentration, changes collision frequency. -->

## How a catalyst works

**With the catalyst, the reaction sped up but the temperature didn't change. How?**

- The catalyst lowered the activation energy, so more collisions had enough energy
- The catalyst reacted and was used up
- The catalyst gave the particles extra energy

* Answer: The catalyst lowered the activation energy, so more collisions had enough energy

<!-- Speaker note: A catalyst provides a route with a lower activation energy. (In the model it simply halves the threshold; real catalysts often work on their surface.) -->

## Evaluate: look back

Write one sentence: "At first I thought… Now I think… because…"

**Revision summary**

- Particles react only when they **collide** with at least the **activation energy**. Most collisions just bounce.
- **Temperature:** faster particles collide more often and, much more importantly, harder.
- **Concentration** and **surface area:** more collisions each second.
- **Catalyst:** a lower activation energy, so more collisions succeed. It is not used up.

## Exit ticket

**Why does crushing a tablet make it dissolve and react faster in water?**

* Crushing increases the surface area, so more particles are exposed and collisions happen more often.
