# Same element, different atoms?

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Every living thing contains a tiny amount of carbon that is slowly turning into nitrogen. That carbon is how scientists date bones and charcoal tens of thousands of years old.


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students define isotopes as atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons, explain why isotopes react alike, and recognise that some isotopes are unstable (AC9S9U06, node 2).

**Students should be able to say:** I can define isotopes. I can say what is the same and what is different about two isotopes of an element. I can name isotopes (carbon-14) and recognise that some are unstable.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (Engage, Explore, Explain, Evaluate). Typical and Wider sections add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::


## Engage


:::keep-together

**Carbon-12 and carbon-14 both have 6 protons. What is different about a carbon-14 atom?**

☐ It has 2 more neutrons

☐ It has 2 more protons

☐ It has 2 more electrons

☐ It is a different element

My prediction, and why:

{{lines-3}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**What happens:** 2 more neutrons. Both are carbon because both have 6 protons. The mass number counts protons and neutrons, so 14 − 6 = 8 neutrons, against 6 in carbon-12. Carbon-12 and carbon-14 are isotopes of carbon.

:::

## Explain: isotopes


:::keep-together

### What isotopes are

**What are isotopes?**

☐ Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons

☐ Atoms that have gained or lost electrons

☐ Atoms of different elements with the same mass number

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons

Same protons (so the same element), different neutrons (so a different mass number).

:::


:::keep-together

### Same or different

**Carbon-12 and carbon-14 atoms: same or different?**

Groups: Same, Different

- Number of protons: {{field-15}}
- Number of electrons (neutral atoms): {{field-15}}
- Number of neutrons: {{field-15}}
- Mass number: {{field-15}}
- How they react with oxygen: {{field-15}}
- Whether the nucleus is stable: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Number of protons → Same; Number of electrons (neutral atoms) → Same; Number of neutrons → Different; Mass number → Different; How they react with oxygen → Same; Whether the nucleus is stable → Different

:::


:::keep-together

### Chlorine

**Chlorine has two common isotopes, chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 (17 protons). How many neutrons are in chlorine-37?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 20

37 − 17 = 20 neutrons. Chlorine-35 has 18.

:::


:::keep-together

### Same reactions

**Why does carbon-14 burn in oxygen just like carbon-12?**

☐ The neutrons do the reacting

☐ Reactions depend on the electrons, and both have 6 arranged the same way

☐ It doesn't: carbon-14 does not burn

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Reactions depend on the electrons, and both have 6 arranged the same way

Isotopes of an element have the same number of electrons, so they react in the same way. Only the nucleus differs.

:::


:::keep-together

### Unstable isotopes

**Some isotopes are unstable. What does that mean?**

☐ The nucleus will decay, giving out radiation and often becoming a different element

☐ The electrons keep leaving the atom

☐ The atom will react violently with water

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** The nucleus will decay, giving out radiation and often becoming a different element

An unstable (radioactive) nucleus has a mix of protons and neutrons that does not last. It decays, giving out radiation. Most elements have both stable and unstable isotopes.

:::


:::typical

## Elaborate: where carbon-14 comes from


:::keep-together

### Living things

**Plants take in carbon dioxide, including a little made with carbon-14. Animals eat the plants. Why does that matter for dating old bones?**

☐ Every living thing has the same small share of carbon-14; after death no new carbon-14 comes in, and what is there slowly decays

☐ Bones make carbon-14 after death

☐ Carbon-14 turns into carbon-12 as bones age

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Every living thing has the same small share of carbon-14; after death no new carbon-14 comes in, and what is there slowly decays

While alive, an animal or plant keeps topping up its carbon-14. After death the share falls steadily. Measuring how much is left tells how long ago it died (you will see how in the half-life lesson).

## Elaborate: chlorine's mass of 35.5


:::keep-together

### The average

**About 3 in every 4 chlorine atoms are chlorine-35, and 1 in 4 is chlorine-37. What is the average mass number? (to 1 decimal place)**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 35.5

(3 × 35 + 1 × 37) ÷ 4 = 142 ÷ 4 = 35.5. That is why the periodic table gives chlorine a relative atomic mass of 35.5.

## Evaluate: look back

Write one sentence: "At first I thought… Now I think… because…"

**Revision summary**

- **Isotopes** are atoms of the same element (same number of protons) with different numbers of neutrons, so different mass numbers. We name them by mass number: carbon-12, carbon-13, carbon-14.
- Isotopes have the same number of electrons, so they react in the same way.
- Some isotopes are **stable**. Others are **unstable** (radioactive): their nuclei decay, giving out radiation.
- Carbon-14 is made in the air from nitrogen-14 and taken in by living things, which is why it can date things that were once alive.
