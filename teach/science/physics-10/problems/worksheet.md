# Solving force and motion problems

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** A car travelling at 25 m/s brakes. How long does it take to stop, and how big is the force? The answer takes three steps, and the hardest part is knowing which one comes first.


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students solve multi-step force and motion problems using F = ma and a = Δv ÷ t with correct units, use a graph to support a quantitative argument, and check answers for reasonableness (AC9S10U05, node 7, a practise node).

**Students should be able to say:** I can solve a problem that needs two or more steps. I can keep the units straight. I can tell whether an answer is reasonable.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (Engage, Explore, Explain, Evaluate). Typical and Wider sections add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::


## Engage


:::keep-together

**A 1500 kg car has an engine force of 4000 N forwards and a resistance of 1000 N backwards. You are asked how fast its speed is changing. What do you work out first?**

☐ The speed, from the mass

☐ Nothing: you need the time first

☐ The net force: 4000 − 1000 = 3000 N

☐ The acceleration, using 4000 N

My prediction, and why:

{{lines-3}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**What happens:** The net force, 3000 N. Acceleration depends on the net (unbalanced) force, not the engine force alone. Then a = F ÷ m = 3000 ÷ 1500 = 2 m/s².

:::


## Explain: practise the routine


:::keep-together

### Net force

**A 1500 kg car has an engine force of 4000 N and a resistance of 1000 N. What is its acceleration (m/s²)?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 2

Net force = 4000 − 1000 = 3000 N. a = F ÷ m = 3000 ÷ 1500 = 2 m/s².

:::


:::keep-together

### Speed after 6 s

**It accelerates at 2 m/s² from rest. What is its speed after 6 s (m/s)?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 12

Δv = a × t = 2 × 6 = 12 m/s.

:::


:::keep-together

### Braking

**A 1200 kg car travelling at 20 m/s brakes to a stop in 8 s. What is its acceleration (m/s²)? Use a negative sign for slowing down.**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** -2.5

a = Δv ÷ t = (0 − 20) ÷ 8 = −2.5 m/s².

:::


:::keep-together

### Braking force

**What braking force does the 1200 kg car need (N)? Give the size only.**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 3000

F = ma = 1200 × 2.5 = 3000 N.

:::


:::keep-together

### Is it reasonable

**A student calculates the braking force of a car as 3 000 000 N. What should they do?**

☐ Check the working: a force that large is not reasonable for a family car

☐ Divide by 10 to make it look sensible

☐ Report it: the calculation was careful

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Check the working: a force that large is not reasonable for a family car

Compare the answer with what you know: a family car's braking force is a few thousand newtons. A huge number suggests a unit slip (grams for kilograms, for example).

:::


:::keep-together

### The graph

**A speed–time graph for a trolley is a straight line rising from 0 m/s at 0 s to 6 m/s at 3 s. What is the acceleration, and what does the gradient show?**

☐ 2 m/s²: the gradient of a speed–time graph is the acceleration

☐ 6 m/s²

☐ 3 m/s²: the gradient is the time

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 2 m/s²: the gradient of a speed–time graph is the acceleration

Gradient = change in speed ÷ change in time = acceleration. Steeper means a bigger acceleration.

:::


:::typical

## Elaborate: two-step problem


:::keep-together

**A 60 kg sprinter reaches 9 m/s from rest in 3 s. What net force acts on the sprinter (N)?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 180

a = 9 ÷ 3 = 3 m/s². F = ma = 60 × 3 = 180 N.

## Elaborate: school zones


:::keep-together

**A car's stopping distance at 60 km/h is about 36 m, and at 40 km/h about 20 m. What is the best argument for a 40 km/h school-zone limit?**

☐ At the lower speed the car stops in much less distance, and the force on anyone it hits is smaller

☐ Stopping distance goes down in proportion to speed, so the lower limit saves only a third of the distance

☐ Cars go slowly so it is quieter

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** At the lower speed the car stops in much less distance, and the force on anyone it hits is smaller

A quantitative argument uses numbers: going 50% faster (60 instead of 40 km/h) takes nearly twice the distance to stop, and a faster car has more energy to lose.

## Evaluate: look back

Write one sentence: "At first I thought… Now I think… because…"

**Revision summary**

- A routine: **list what you know, find the net force, use F = ma for acceleration, use a = Δv ÷ t for speed and time, then check** the answer is reasonable.
- Keep **units** with every number: N, kg, m/s², m/s, s.
- On a **speed–time graph**, the gradient is the acceleration.
- A negative acceleration means slowing down.

## Exit ticket

:::keep-together

**A 50 kg skateboarder is pushed with a net force of 100 N from rest. How fast is she moving after 4 s?**

{{lines-3}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** a = 100 ÷ 50 = 2 m/s². After 4 s: 2 × 4 = 8 m/s.
