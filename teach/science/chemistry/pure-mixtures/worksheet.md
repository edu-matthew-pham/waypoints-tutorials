# Pure substance or mixture?

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** In the supermarket, "100% pure" orange juice means nothing has been added. In science, pure means something much stricter, and you can only check it by looking at the particles.


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students distinguish pure substances from mixtures at the particle level and classify familiar materials with a particle reason (AC9S7U06, node 1, a hinge node).

**Students should be able to say:** I can tell a pure substance from a mixture using a particle diagram. I can classify familiar materials and give a particle reason. I can explain why a solution like salt water is a mixture even though it looks the same all through.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (Engage, Explore, Explain, Evaluate). Typical and Wider sections add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Explain: pure means one kind of particle


:::keep-together

### Classify

**Pure substance or mixture?**

Groups: Pure substance, Mixture

- Distilled water: {{field-15}}
- Salt water: {{field-15}}
- Sandy water: {{field-15}}
- Air: {{field-15}}
- Copper wire: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Distilled water → Pure substance; Salt water → Mixture; Sandy water → Mixture; Air → Mixture; Copper wire → Pure substance

Count the kinds of particles, not how the material looks.

:::


:::keep-together

### Salt water

**Salt water looks exactly the same all the way through. Why is it still a mixture?**

☐ It contains two kinds of particles, even though they are evenly spread

☐ Because it is slightly cloudy

☐ Because people added the salt

☐ It isn't: the salt has disappeared, so it is pure water

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** It contains two kinds of particles, even though they are evenly spread

Evenly spread or not, two kinds of particles make a mixture.

:::


:::keep-together

### "Pure" juice

**So is "100% pure" orange juice a pure substance?**

☐ No: it contains water, sugars, acids and many other kinds of particles

☐ Yes, nothing has been added

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** No: it contains water, sugars, acids and many other kinds of particles

"Pure" on the label only means nothing was added.

:::


## Mix it up


:::keep-together

**Looking back at the particle model: if you could zoom into a drop of pure water, what would you see?**

☐ Only water particles, with empty space between them, always moving

☐ Water particles with a little air between them

☐ Smooth water, all the way down

☐ Water particles and salt particles evenly mixed

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Only water particles, with empty space between them, always moving

Pure means one kind of particle. Those particles still have empty space between them and never stop moving.

## Elaborate: solvents and solutes

A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture. The **solute** dissolves; the **solvent** does the dissolving.

:::keep-together

### Solvent

**In salt water, what is the solvent?**

☐ Water

☐ Salt

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Water

The solvent does the dissolving: water.

:::


:::keep-together

### Solutes

**In a soft drink, what are the solutes?**

☐ Sugar and carbon dioxide gas

☐ Water

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Sugar and carbon dioxide gas

A gas can be a solute too: that's the fizz.

## Elaborate: borderline cases


:::keep-together

### Stainless steel

**Stainless steel looks like a single shiny metal. It is mostly iron, with chromium and nickel particles spread evenly through it. Pure substance or mixture?**

☐ Mixture

☐ Pure substance

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Mixture

Alloys are mixtures, even though they look like one metal.

:::


:::keep-together

### Seawater

**Seawater can contain more or less salt depending on where it is collected. Is seawater with extra salt still the same mixture?**

☐ No: it becomes a new pure substance

☐ Yes: the same mixture with different amounts

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Yes: the same mixture with different amounts

Mixtures can vary in how much of each part they contain.

:::


:::keep-together

### The definition

**Which statement is true of every pure substance?**

☐ It looks shiny and even

☐ It always has the same make-up, with one kind of particle

☐ It occurs naturally

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** It always has the same make-up, with one kind of particle

That is what makes the definition useful: a pure substance's make-up can't vary.

## Evaluate: look back

Which did you think was pure, and what made you think so? Write one sentence: "At first I thought… Now I think… because…"

**Revision summary**

- A pure substance contains only one kind of particle. A mixture contains two or more.
- You can't tell by looking: salt water and air look the same all the way through, but both are mixtures.
- "Pure" on a food label means nothing added. It does not mean a pure substance.
