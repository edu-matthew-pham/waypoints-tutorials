# Would you weigh less on the Moon?

Year 7 Physics · Forces and motion

## Which is which

**Mass or weight?**

Groups: Mass · Weight

Measured in kilograms (kg) · Measured in newtons (N) · The same on Earth and on the Moon · A force, pulling towards the planet · How much matter an object has · Changes if g changes

* Answer: Measured in kilograms (kg) → Mass; Measured in newtons (N) → Weight; The same on Earth and on the Moon → Mass; A force, pulling towards the planet → Weight; How much matter an object has → Mass; Changes if g changes → Weight

## Weight on Earth

**A 5 kg bag on Earth (g = 9.8 N/kg). What is its weight, in N?**

* Answer: 49

<!-- Speaker note: Weight is mass × g, so it's 5 × 9.8 = 49 N. -->

**The same 5 kg bag on Mars (g = 3.7 N/kg). Its weight, in N?**

* Answer: 18.5

## Why the Moon pulls less

**Why is the astronaut's weight smaller on the Moon?**

- She has less mass there
- The Moon is further from the Sun
- The Moon has much less mass than Earth, so its gravity pulls less (smaller g)
- There is no air on the Moon

* Answer: The Moon has much less mass than Earth, so its gravity pulls less (smaller g)

<!-- Speaker note: Gravity depends on mass, and the Moon has only about 1/80 of Earth's mass and is also much smaller, which together make its g just 1.6 N/kg. -->

## Mix it up

**Looking back at balanced forces: a 2 kg bag hangs still from a spring balance. Its weight is about 20 N. What force does the spring pull up with?**

- About 2 N
- 0 N, because the bag is not moving
- About 20 N: the forces are balanced
- About 40 N, so that it can lift the bag

* Answer: About 20 N: the forces are balanced

<!-- Speaker note: The bag is still, so the forces are balanced and the spring pulls up with a force equal to the weight, about 20 N. What this step is for: Weight is a force, so it follows the same rule as any other force: if nothing is changing, something else balances it. -->

## Built for Mars

**A rover's wheels are designed for its weight on Mars. It is sent to Jupiter instead (if it could land). What is the problem?**

- Its weight would be over six times bigger, so its wheels and legs might collapse
- Nothing: weight is the same everywhere
- Its mass would be bigger, so it would need more fuel

* Answer: Its weight would be over six times bigger, so its wheels and legs might collapse

## Elaborate: do heavy things fall faster?

**On the Moon (no air), an astronaut dropped a hammer and a feather together (Apollo 15, 1971). What happened?**

- They hit the ground together
- The feather floated away
- The hammer landed first

* Answer: They hit the ground together

<!-- Speaker note: The heavier hammer is pulled harder, but it also has more mass to get moving, so both speed up at the same rate. Air resistance is what makes feathers fall slowly on Earth. What this step is for: Refuting the 'heavier falls faster' misconception uses weight and mass together: double the mass, double the pull, but double the mass to move. -->

## Evaluate: look back

Write one sentence: "At first I thought… Now I think… because…"

**Revision summary**

- **Mass** (kg): how much matter. The same everywhere.
- **Weight** (N): the force of gravity on an object. **Weight = mass × g.**
- **g** is the gravitational field strength: 9.8 N/kg on Earth (about 10), 1.6 on the Moon, 3.7 on Mars, 24.8 on Jupiter.
- Gravity depends on mass: the Moon has far less mass than Earth, so it pulls less (smaller g, smaller weight).
- Without air, all objects fall together, whatever their mass.

## Exit ticket

**A 2 kg rock on Earth (g = 9.8 N/kg). What are its mass and its weight on the Moon (g = 1.6 N/kg)?**

* Mass 2 kg (unchanged); weight 2 × 1.6 = 3.2 N.
