# How does current flow?

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** A copper wire and a plastic ruler both contain millions of electrons. Only one of them can light a bulb. What is different?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students explain electric current as a directed flow of free electrons driven by voltage, and distinguish conductors from insulators using free and bound electrons (AC9S9U04, node 3).

**Students should be able to say:** I can explain what an electric current is. I can say why copper conducts and plastic doesn't. I can say what the battery does.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (Engage, Explore, Explain, Evaluate). Typical and Wider sections add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::


## Engage


:::keep-together

**A battery is joined to a bulb by copper wire and the bulb lights. What is flowing round the circuit?**

☐ Electrons that were already in the wire, pushed round by the battery

☐ Electrons that come out of the battery and are used up in the bulb

☐ Nothing flows: the bulb makes the electricity

☐ Protons, pushed round by the battery

My prediction, and why:

{{lines-3}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**What happens:** Electrons that were already in the wire. A metal is full of free electrons; the battery pushes them round the loop. They are not used up: as many go back into the battery as come out. What the bulb uses is the energy they carry.

:::

## Explain: the particle model of current


:::keep-together

### Current

**What is an electric current in a metal wire?**

☐ A flow of free electrons

☐ A flow of atoms

☐ A flow of protons

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** A flow of free electrons

The atoms are fixed; the free electrons between them drift along when pushed.

:::


:::keep-together

### Conductor and insulator

**Why does copper conduct while plastic doesn't?**

☐ Copper has free electrons that can move; in plastic they are tied to their atoms

☐ Copper has more protons

☐ Plastic has no electrons

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Copper has free electrons that can move; in plastic they are tied to their atoms

Conductors have many free electrons; in insulators almost all the electrons are held tightly by their atoms.

:::


:::keep-together

### The battery

**What does the battery do?**

☐ Stores electrons and releases them until it is empty

☐ Makes the electrons

☐ Pushes the free electrons round the circuit, giving them energy

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Pushes the free electrons round the circuit, giving them energy

The battery's voltage is the push. A bigger voltage pushes harder, so the electrons drift faster and more energy is delivered each second.

:::


:::keep-together

### Used up?

**Are the electrons used up in the bulb?**

☐ Only some of them

☐ Yes: that is where the light comes from

☐ No: the same number go in as come out; the bulb transfers the energy they carry as light and heat

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** No: the same number go in as come out; the bulb transfers the energy they carry as light and heat

Current is the same at every point in the loop; the energy, not the electrons, is what the bulb gets.

:::


:::keep-together

### Which way

**On the board, electrons drift round one way. By convention the current is drawn the other way. Why?**

☐ The convention was chosen before electrons were discovered, and has stuck

☐ The board has a mistake

☐ Current is the flow of protons

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** The convention was chosen before electrons were discovered, and has stuck

Conventional current is drawn from + to −, a choice made before electrons were known. Electrons actually flow the other way.

:::


:::typical

## Elaborate: how bright?


:::keep-together

### What changes

**What happens to the electrons and the bulb when the voltage doubles?**

☐ The electrons get bigger

☐ The electrons drift faster, the current is bigger and the bulb is brighter

☐ Nothing changes: the bulb has a fixed brightness

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** The electrons drift faster, the current is bigger and the bulb is brighter

More volts means a stronger push. The electrons drift faster, so more charge passes each second and more energy reaches the bulb.

:::

:::

## Elaborate: in between


:::keep-together

**Silicon has only a few free electrons, far fewer than copper and far more than plastic. It is used in computer chips. Why is that useful?**

☐ It is a perfect insulator

☐ It is cheap

☐ Its conduction can be controlled (by adding other atoms, or a voltage), so it can act as a switch

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Its conduction can be controlled (by adding other atoms, or a voltage), so it can act as a switch

A material that sits between conductors and insulators can be switched on and off, which is how transistors in chips work.

:::

## Evaluate: look back

Write one sentence: "At first I thought… Now I think… because…"

**Revision summary**

- A **conductor** has **free electrons**; an **insulator** has almost none: its electrons are held tightly by their atoms.
- A **current** is a flow of free electrons. The **battery** pushes them round; more volts means a stronger push.
- The electrons are **not used up**; the bulb transfers the energy they carry.
- **Conventional current** is drawn the opposite way to the electrons, by a historical choice.

**A student says 'the bulb uses up the electrons so the wire after it is empty'. What would you say?**

{{lines-3}}
