# How does sound travel?

Year 9 Physics · Wave and particle models

## Today

**We are learning to** sound is a longitudinal wave: a vibrating source makes compressions (particles squashed together) and rarefactions (particles spread out) that travel through a medium, with each particle only vibrating to and fro in place. Frequency sets the pitch and amplitude sets the loudness. Without particles, as in a vacuum, there is nothing to carry the wave.

- I can describe a sound wave with compressions and rarefactions.
- I can say what changes when a sound gets higher or louder.
- I can explain why sound cannot travel through a vacuum.

## Engage: predict

**A speaker plays a note. As the sound passes through the air, what does one particle of air do?**

- It travels from the speaker all the way to your ear
- It vibrates to and fro in about the same place
- It speeds up, then stops
- It doesn't move: only the sound does

## Compression

**What is a compression in a sound wave?**

- The sound getting quieter
- A place where the particles are spread further apart
- A place where the particles are squashed closer together

* Answer: A place where the particles are squashed closer together

<!-- Speaker note: A compression is a squashed patch; a rarefaction is a stretched, spread-out patch. A sound wave is a line of them, one after the other. -->

## Longitudinal

**In sound, the particles vibrate backwards and forwards along the direction the wave travels. What do we call this kind of wave?**

- Transverse
- Stationary
- Longitudinal

* Answer: Longitudinal

<!-- Speaker note: Longitudinal: the vibration is along the direction of travel. That is why sound is squashes and stretches. -->

## Pitch

**You raise the pitch. What has increased?**

- The frequency: more vibrations each second, so shorter waves
- The speed of every particle
- The amplitude: bigger vibrations

* Answer: The frequency: more vibrations each second, so shorter waves

<!-- Speaker note: Higher pitch means higher frequency (more compressions pass each second). Loudness depends on amplitude, the size of the vibration. -->

## Matching

**Match the change to the effect on the sound.**

| | |
|---|---|
| `Bigger vibrations (larger amplitude)` | A. Higher pitch |
| `More vibrations each second (higher frequency)` | B. Lower pitch |
| `Fewer vibrations each second` | C. Louder |

* Answer: Bigger vibrations (larger amplitude) → C. Louder; More vibrations each second (higher frequency) → A. Higher pitch; Fewer vibrations each second → B. Lower pitch

## Vacuum

**Why can't sound travel through a vacuum?**

- Sound is too slow in a vacuum
- A vacuum absorbs the sound
- There are no particles to vibrate and pass the energy on

* Answer: There are no particles to vibrate and pass the energy on

<!-- Speaker note: Sound is energy passed from particle to particle. With no particles, there is nothing to pass it along. -->

## Why faster

**Sound travels at about 340 m/s in air, 1500 m/s in water and 5000 m/s in steel. Why is it fastest in steel?**

- Steel is warmer
- The particles are closer together and tightly bonded, so each passes the vibration on sooner
- Steel particles are heavier

* Answer: The particles are closer together and tightly bonded, so each passes the vibration on sooner

<!-- Speaker note: In a solid the particles are packed close together and joined to their neighbours, so a vibration is passed on very quickly. In a gas they are far apart, so it takes longer. -->

## Elaborate: an instrument

**A yidaki (didjeridu) is a hollow wooden tube. The player's lips vibrate the air inside it. A longer tube makes a lower note. Using the wave model, which is the best explanation?**

- A longer tube makes the sound louder, so it sounds lower
- Sound travels slower in longer tubes
- The longer column of air vibrates more slowly, so the frequency is lower and the pitch is lower

* Answer: The longer column of air vibrates more slowly, so the frequency is lower and the pitch is lower

## Evaluate: look back

Write one sentence: "At first I thought… Now I think… because…"

**Revision summary**

- Sound is a **longitudinal wave**: particles vibrate to and fro along the direction the wave travels.
- A wave is a line of **compressions** (squashed) and **rarefactions** (spread out). The pattern travels; the particles stay in place.
- **Frequency** (vibrations per second) sets the **pitch**; **amplitude** (size of the vibration) sets the **loudness**.
- Sound needs a **medium**: it cannot travel through a vacuum. It is faster in solids than in gases because the particles are closer together and more tightly bonded.

## Exit ticket

**A loud, high note is played. Say what is large or fast about the wave, and what the particles of air in between are doing.**

* Large amplitude (loud) and high frequency (high pitch). The particles vibrate to and fro in place while the squashed and spread-out patches travel.
