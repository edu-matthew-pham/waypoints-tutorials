# Where does the heat go?

Year 7 Chemistry · Particle theory

## Today

- I can say whether energy goes in or out when a substance melts, boils, freezes or condenses.
- I can explain a change of state using particles.
- I can explain why the temperature stays the same while a substance changes state.

## Why it matters

> A pot of water on the stove climbs to 100 °C and then stops, even with the gas still on full. Where is all that heat going?

<!-- Speaker note: You know how particles are arranged in solids, liquids and gases, so the next question is what the energy is doing while one turns into another. -->

## In or out

**Does the substance take energy in, or give energy out?**

Groups: Energy in · Energy out

Melting (solid to liquid) · Boiling or evaporating (liquid to gas) · Freezing (liquid to solid) · Condensing (gas to liquid)

* Answer: Melting (solid to liquid) → Energy in; Boiling or evaporating (liquid to gas) → Energy in; Freezing (liquid to solid) → Energy out; Condensing (gas to liquid) → Energy out

## The flat part

**On the heating curve, the line is flat from 1 to 9 minutes. What is happening?**

- The water is boiling
- The ice is melting: energy goes into freeing the particles from their fixed places, so the temperature doesn't rise
- Nothing: the stove was turned off

* Answer: The ice is melting: energy goes into freeing the particles from their fixed places, so the temperature doesn't rise

<!-- Speaker note: During a change of state, the energy goes into overcoming the attraction between particles, not into making them move faster, so the temperature stays constant. -->

## Why boiling takes longer

**The curve is drawn to scale: the stove gives the same energy every minute. Why is the flat part at 100 °C about seven times longer than the one at 0 °C?**

- Boiling pulls the particles completely apart, which takes far more energy than loosening them from their fixed places
- The stove gives less energy once the water is hot
- Steam is lighter than ice, so it takes longer to make

* Answer: Boiling pulls the particles completely apart, which takes far more energy than loosening them from their fixed places

<!-- Speaker note: Melting only frees the particles to slide past each other. Boiling has to pull them far apart against their attraction, so it needs about seven times as much energy (2260 kJ for each kilogram, against 334 kJ to melt it). -->

## Before melting

**What does adding energy do to the particles in a solid, before it melts?**

- They vibrate faster, so the temperature rises
- They start to leave the solid straight away
- They get bigger

* Answer: They vibrate faster, so the temperature rises

<!-- Speaker note: Temperature measures how fast the particles move on average, so adding energy makes them move faster and the temperature rises until a change of state begins. -->

## What the particles do

**What happens to the particles in each change of state?**

| | |
|---|---|
| `Melting` | A. Gas particles slow down and are pulled close together into a liquid, giving out energy |
| `Freezing` | B. Particles slow down and settle into fixed places, giving out energy |
| `Evaporating` | C. Fast particles at the surface pull free of the others and escape as a gas |
| `Condensing` | D. Particles vibrate hard enough to break free of their fixed places and start sliding past each other |

* Answer: Melting → D. Particles vibrate hard enough to break free of their fixed places and start sliding past each other; Freezing → B. Particles slow down and settle into fixed places, giving out energy; Evaporating → C. Fast particles at the surface pull free of the others and escape as a gas; Condensing → A. Gas particles slow down and are pulled close together into a liquid, giving out energy

<!-- Speaker note: Energy going in frees the particles from each other's attraction, and energy coming out lets the attraction pull them back together. -->

## Mix it up

**Looking back at states of matter: a small jar of gas is opened in the middle of a large, sealed room. A few minutes later, where are its particles?**

- Still in a clump where the jar was
- Spread through the whole room
- Mostly at the top of the room
- Mostly on the floor

* Answer: Spread through the whole room

## Why it stays flat

**Why does the stearic acid's temperature stay at 69 °C for 8 minutes while it freezes?**

- The room warms the wax
- As particles settle into fixed places they give out energy, which keeps the temperature steady
- The thermometer stopped working

* Answer: As particles settle into fixed places they give out energy, which keeps the temperature steady

<!-- Speaker note: Freezing gives energy out, so until every particle has settled, that energy balances what the wax loses to the room. -->

## Elaborate: sweating

**Why does sweating cool you down, even on a hot day?**

- Evaporating sweat takes energy in, and it takes it from your skin
- Sweat is colder than your body
- The wind blows the heat away

* Answer: Evaporating sweat takes energy in, and it takes it from your skin

<!-- Speaker note: Changing a liquid to a gas needs energy, so the fastest particles escape from the sweat and carry energy away from your skin. What this step is for: Evaporation taking energy from the skin connects 'energy in for a change of state' to everyday cooling. -->

## Evaluate: look back

Write one sentence: "At first I thought… Now I think… because…"

**Revision summary**

- Melting, boiling and evaporating take **energy in**; freezing and condensing give **energy out**.
- Adding energy to a substance makes its particles move faster (the temperature rises), until it reaches a change of state.
- During a change of state the temperature stays the same: the energy goes into separating the particles (or comes out as they settle).
- A **heating curve** shows this as flat parts at the melting and boiling points.

## Exit ticket

**Water vapour condenses on a cold window. Is energy taken in or given out, and what are the particles doing?**

* Given out. The water particles slow down and come close together, forming a liquid on the glass.
