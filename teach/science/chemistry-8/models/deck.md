# Symbols, formulas or models?

Year 8 Chemistry · Elements and compounds

<!-- Speaker note: Students compare symbols, formulas, structural formulas, models and particle diagrams, say what each shows, read formulas, and choose a representation for a purpose (AC9S8U06, node 5). About 24 minutes (Engage and Explore 7 min · Explain 10 min · Evaluate 3 min · Typical adds 3 min, Wider about 3 min). -->

---

## Today

**We are learning to** chemists show the same substance in different ways. A symbol names an element; a formula (H₂O) gives the types and numbers of atoms; a structural formula shows which atoms are joined; a 3D model shows the shape. Each shows something the others don't, so you pick the one that suits the job.

- I can read a chemical formula and count the atoms.
- I can say what symbols, formulas and models each show.
- I can choose a representation that suits the job.

## Why it matters

> H₂O, a drawing of three balls, and the word 'water' all mean the same thing. Why do chemists need so many ways to say it?

## Which shows what

**Match each representation to what it shows best.**

| | |
|---|---|
| `Symbol (O)` | A. Which atoms are joined to which |
| `Formula (CO₂)` | B. Which atoms, and how many of each |
| `Structural formula (O=C=O)` | C. Whether it is an element, compound or mixture, and how much of each |
| `3D model` | D. The shape of the particle |
| `Particle diagram of a box` | E. Which element |

* Answer: Symbol (O) → E. Which element; Formula (CO₂) → B. Which atoms, and how many of each; Structural formula (O=C=O) → A. Which atoms are joined to which; 3D model → D. The shape of the particle; Particle diagram of a box → C. Whether it is an element, compound or mixture, and how much of each

<!-- Speaker note: Each shows something; none shows everything. -->

## Choosing one

**You want to show a friend that air is a mixture of nitrogen and oxygen. Which is best?**

- The formulas N₂ and O₂
- A particle diagram of a box with separate N₂ and O₂ particles

* Answer: A particle diagram of a box with separate N₂ and O₂ particles

## What each leaves out

**Every representation leaves something out. Match each to one of its limits.**

| | |
|---|---|
| `Symbol (O)` | A. Slow to build or draw, and no use in a written equation |
| `Formula (H₂O)` | B. It doesn't show how many atoms are joined in each particle (oxygen gas is O₂) |
| `Structural formula (H–O–H)` | C. It doesn't show which atoms are joined to which, or the shape |
| `3D model` | D. Drawn flat, it can hide the real 3D shape (water is bent) |

* Answer: Symbol (O) → B. It doesn't show how many atoms are joined in each particle (oxygen gas is O₂); Formula (H₂O) → C. It doesn't show which atoms are joined to which, or the shape; Structural formula (H–O–H) → D. Drawn flat, it can hide the real 3D shape (water is bent); 3D model → A. Slow to build or draw, and no use in a written equation

<!-- Speaker note: Symbols and formulas are quick to write; a structural formula shows the joins; a 3D model shows the shape. Each has a limit, which is why chemists use several. -->

## Evaluate: look back

Write one sentence: "At first I thought… Now I think… because…"

**Revision summary**

- A **symbol** (O, Na) names an element.
- A **formula** (H₂O, CO₂) gives the types of atom and how many of each. The small number counts the atom before it; a big number in front counts particles (3CO₂).
- A **structural formula** shows which atoms are joined; a **3D model** shows the shape; a **particle diagram** shows elements, compounds and mixtures.
- No single representation shows everything: choose the one that suits the job.
