# Is it a new substance?

Year 8 Chemistry · Chemical change

<!-- Speaker note: Students state the new substance test for chemical change, explain it as atoms rearranging into new particles, and use it in place of the reversibility rule (AC9S8U07, node 2, a hinge node). About 25 minutes (Engage and Explore 8 min · Explain 10 min · Evaluate 3 min · Typical adds 4 min, Wider about 5 min). -->

---

## Today

**We are learning to** a chemical change makes a new substance, because atoms rearrange into new particles. A physical change only rearranges or spreads out the same particles. "Can it be reversed?" is not the test.

- I can state that a chemical change makes a new substance.
- I can classify changes as physical or chemical with the new substance test.
- I can explain why "can it be reversed?" is not a reliable test, with a counterexample.

## Why it matters

> You can melt ice and freeze it again. You can't unburn toast. Is that how to tell a chemical change? Dissolved salt comes back when the water dries up…

<!-- Speaker note: In Year 6 you may have learned that changes you can reverse are physical. This lesson tests that rule, and replaces it with a better one. -->

## The reaction

**On the reaction board, what happened to the atoms?**

- They rearranged into new particles: hydrogen chloride
- New atoms were made
- Some atoms were destroyed
- They melted together

* Answer: They rearranged into new particles: hydrogen chloride

<!-- Speaker note: The atoms are the same; they are arranged into different particles. A new particle means a new substance. -->

**When the ice melted and boiled, what happened to the water particles?**

- They stayed water particles; only their spacing and motion changed

## Physical or chemical

**Physical or chemical? Use the new substance test.**

Groups: Physical · Chemical

Ice melting · Salt dissolving in water · Paper burning · An iron nail rusting · Cooking an egg · Water boiling · Baking soda fizzing in vinegar · Chocolate melting

* Answer: Ice melting → Physical; Salt dissolving in water → Physical; Paper burning → Chemical; An iron nail rusting → Chemical; Cooking an egg → Chemical; Water boiling → Physical; Baking soda fizzing in vinegar → Chemical; Chocolate melting → Physical

## When the old rule fails

**Dissolving salt can be reversed, and it is physical. Which of these breaks the old "can it be reversed?" rule the other way?**

- Cutting paper
- Freezing water
- Recharging a battery: a chemical change that can be reversed

* Answer: Recharging a battery: a chemical change that can be reversed

<!-- Speaker note: Charging and using a battery runs chemical reactions forwards and backwards. Reversibility is a clue, not the test. -->

## Disappear, or spread out?

**The salt disappeared. Is dissolving a chemical change?**

- No: the salt particles are still there, spread between the water particles

* Answer: No: the salt particles are still there, spread between the water particles

## Evaluate: look back

Write one sentence: "At first I thought… Now I think… because…"

**Revision summary**

- A **chemical change** makes a **new substance**: atoms separate and join into new particles.
- A **physical change** (melting, boiling, dissolving) keeps the same particles.
- **"Is there a new substance?"** is the test. "Can it be reversed?" is only a clue: dissolving can be reversed and is physical.
- In a chemical change no atoms are made or destroyed; they are rearranged.

**Is rusting physical or chemical? How do you know?**

* Chemical: rust (iron oxide) is a new substance with different properties from iron.
