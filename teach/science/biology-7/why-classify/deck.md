# Why sort living things?

Year 7 Biology · Classification

## Today

**We are learning to** scientists classify living things to identify them, to talk about them clearly (one name the whole world understands), and to see how they are related. A classification is built for a purpose, and different systems can suit different purposes.

- I can give two reasons why living things are classified.
- I can tell classification from a simple list.
- I can give an everyday example of classification.

## Why it matters

> There are about 2 million named species of living things, and millions more not yet named. Without a system, how would anyone find their way around them?

## Engage: predict

**A scientist finds a beetle she has never seen. Why does it help that beetles are already classified and named?**

- It doesn't help: classification is just a tidy list
- She can work out what it is, or show it is new, and tell other scientists exactly which beetle she means
- So the beetle can be put in a museum
- So she knows how many legs it has

## Reasons

**Is it a reason scientists classify living things?**

Groups: A reason to classify · Not a reason

To identify an organism they don't know · To use names every scientist understands · To see which organisms are related · To make the living world look neat · To decide which organisms are better

* Answer: To identify an organism they don't know → A reason to classify; To use names every scientist understands → A reason to classify; To see which organisms are related → A reason to classify; To make the living world look neat → Not a reason; To decide which organisms are better → Not a reason

## Classification or a list

**Which is classification, not just a list?**

- Writing animals in alphabetical order
- Grouping animals by shared features, such as backbone or no backbone, then smaller groups inside
- Numbering animals in the order you saw them

* Answer: Grouping animals by shared features, such as backbone or no backbone, then smaller groups inside

<!-- Speaker note: Classification groups things by what they share, so the group tells you something about each member. -->

## Everyday classification

**A supermarket groups food by aisle: dairy, bakery, fruit. How is that like scientific classification?**

- Groups by shared features help people find things and talk about them
- It isn't: only scientists classify
- Both put things in alphabetical order

* Answer: Groups by shared features help people find things and talk about them

<!-- Speaker note: Libraries, supermarkets and phone contacts all classify, for a purpose. Scientific classification's purposes are identifying, communicating and showing relationships. -->

## Which system

**You could group these animals by where they live (land, water, air) or by body features (backbone, feathers, fur). Which is more useful for showing how they are related?**

- Body features: a platypus and a kangaroo share fur and milk, though one swims
- Both are equally useful for every purpose
- Where they live: animals in the same place are related

* Answer: Body features: a platypus and a kangaroo share fur and milk, though one swims

<!-- Speaker note: Grouping by habitat suits some jobs (planning a reserve); grouping by shared features suits others (showing relationships). The purpose decides the system. -->

## Different purposes

**Many Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples group plants and animals in ways tied to Country: by uses, seasons, relationships between species and cultural knowledge. Scientific classification groups by shared features and ancestry. What does comparing them show?**

- Each system is built for its purposes, and each holds knowledge the other may not
- Only one of them can be correct
- Classification systems never change

* Answer: Each system is built for its purposes, and each holds knowledge the other may not

<!-- Speaker note: Every classification reflects the purposes and knowledge of the people who built it. Ecologists increasingly work with First Peoples' knowledge of species and seasons. -->

**Revision summary**

- Scientists classify living things to **identify** them, to **communicate** clearly (one name everyone understands) and to show how they are **related**.
- Classification groups by shared features; a list (alphabetical, by date) does not.
- Every classification is built for a purpose; different purposes can suit different systems.
