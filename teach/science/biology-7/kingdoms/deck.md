# Plant, animal or something else?

Year 7 Biology · Classification

<!-- Speaker note: Students place organisms into kingdoms using observable features, justify each placement, recognise a hard-to-classify case, and see that kingdoms sort by features rather than relatedness, a model revised as evidence improved (AC9S7U01, node 2, a hinge node). ## Today

- I can name the five kingdoms.
- I can place an organism in its kingdom and give the observable feature that decides it.
- I can explain why some organisms are hard to classify.
- I can explain why kingdoms don't always show which organisms are related, and why classification changes with new evidence.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: A concept tutorial for introducing or revising the idea; it does not replace specimens, photos or a microscope. Set the width with `?width=minimum`, `typical` (default) or `wider`. The hinge is feature-based justification: students who sort by first impressions ("it doesn't move, so it's a plant") can't choose discriminating features when they build keys in node 4. Kingdoms are not the endpoint. The five-kingdom scheme (common in Year 7) sorts by observable features, which is what the descriptor asks for, but it is not a family tree: fungi share a more recent common ancestor with animals than with plants, "protists" is a grab-bag of unrelated groups, and "bacteria" hides the archaea. Two Explain questions make this explicit, and the history (two kingdoms, then five, then three domains as microscopes and then DNA added evidence) shows classification as a model revised under evidence, the point about scientific rigour. Model notes: algae such as kelp are grouped with protists here; many textbooks differ. The key is a simplification: a single cell with a nucleus goes to Protist and a many-celled thing that makes its own food goes to Plant, so it would misplace yeast (a single-celled fungus) and kelp. Yeast is in the sort, not the key, on purpose. Teacher: In your own words: the five-organism list with features is the node's evidence. -->

## Why it changed

**Scientists once used two kingdoms (plants and animals), then five, and now group life into three domains. Why did it keep changing?**

- New evidence (microscopes, then DNA) showed groups that didn't fit the old system
- The earlier scientists were careless
- Living things have evolved into new kingdoms since then

* Answer: New evidence (microscopes, then DNA) showed groups that didn't fit the old system

<!-- Speaker note: Microscopes revealed single-celled life that was neither plant nor animal, and DNA showed that 'bacteria' are really two very different groups (bacteria and archaea) and that protists aren't one family, so classification is a model that gets revised when the evidence demands it. -->

## Elaborate: the full address of a kangaroo

**Put the red kangaroo's classification in order, from the biggest group to the smallest.**

```science
Family: Kangaroos and wallabies (Macropodidae)
Phylum: Chordates (animals with a backbone)
Order: Diprotodonts (Diprotodontia: kangaroos, wombats, koalas and possums)
Species: Osphranter rufus (red kangaroo)
Class: Mammals
Genus: Osphranter
Kingdom: Animals
```

## Evaluate: look back

Write one sentence: "At first I thought… Now I think… because…"

**Revision summary**

- Living things are grouped by **observable features** into **kingdoms**: animals, plants, fungi, protists and bacteria.
- **Plants** make their own food from sunlight; **fungi** absorb food from other material; **animals** eat other living things.
- **Protists** are mostly single cells with a nucleus; **bacteria** are single tiny cells with no nucleus.
- Some organisms (Euglena, algae) are hard to place: classification is a tool that changes with evidence.
- Kingdoms sort by **features**; they don't always show who is **related**. Fungi are closer relatives of animals than of plants, and protists aren't one family.
- Scientists now group life by evolutionary relationships, using DNA: **three domains** (bacteria, archaea and eukaryotes). The five kingdoms are still a useful way to sort what you can see.
