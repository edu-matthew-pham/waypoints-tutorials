# Why did the beetles change?

Year 10 Biology · Evolution

<!-- Speaker note: Students model natural selection as predators, explain it in steps, reject the idea that individuals change because they need to, and connect it to common ancestry and the tree of life (AC9S10U02, node 2, a hinge node). About 26 minutes (Engage and Explore 9 min · Explain 11 min · Evaluate 3 min · Typical adds 4 min, Wider about 1 min). -->

## Today

**We are learning to** natural selection: individuals vary; more are born than survive; those best suited to the environment are more likely to survive and reproduce, passing on their alleles. Over generations the population changes. Individuals don't change to suit the environment.

- I can explain natural selection in steps.
- I can explain that populations change, not individuals.
- I can explain why "fittest" depends on the environment.
- I can explain that all living things are related in a branching tree, and why features alone can mislead about relatedness.

## Why it matters

> After a bushfire, the ground is black. A few years later, a beetle population on it is darker than before. No beetle changed colour.

* Beetles that blend in survive and breed more, so their offspring (with similar colours) make up more of the next generation. Individuals never change; the population does.

## The steps

**Put the steps of natural selection in order.**

Groups: 1st · 2nd · 3rd · 4th · 5th

Beetles in the population vary in colour · Birds eat the beetles that stand out most · Beetles that blend in are more likely to survive · Survivors breed, passing on the alleles for their colour · Over generations, the population's average colour shifts

* Answer: Beetles in the population vary in colour → 1st; Birds eat the beetles that stand out most → 2nd; Beetles that blend in are more likely to survive → 3rd; Survivors breed, passing on the alleles for their colour → 4th; Over generations, the population's average colour shifts → 5th

## No variation

**What would happen if every beetle started exactly the same colour?**

- Selection couldn't change the population: there would be no variation to select from
- They would all be eaten
- They would turn the ground's colour
- They would still evolve the same way

* Answer: Selection couldn't change the population: there would be no variation to select from

## A branching tree

**Over millions of years, populations split apart and natural selection changes each part differently. What does that mean for living things today?**

- Each species began separately, so they aren't related
- They are all related, in a branching family tree: close relatives share a recent common ancestor
- They form a ladder, with humans at the top as the most evolved

* Answer: They are all related, in a branching family tree: close relatives share a recent common ancestor

<!-- Speaker note: Splitting and selection, repeated over millions of years, give a branching tree of life. Every living thing is related; the question is how closely. -->

## Back to the kingdoms

**In Year 7, kingdoms put mushrooms in their own group, and many people think of them as plant-like. DNA shows fungi share a more recent ancestor with animals than with plants. Why don't features alone show this?**

- Similar features can evolve in unrelated groups, and close relatives can come to look very different
- Fungi evolved from plants
- DNA tests are unreliable

* Answer: Similar features can evolve in unrelated groups, and close relatives can come to look very different

<!-- Speaker note: Natural selection shapes features to suit each environment, so features can mislead about family. That is why classification moved from kingdoms by features to groups by shared ancestry, and changes as evidence improves. -->

## Evaluate: look back

Write one sentence: "At first I thought… Now I think… because…"

**Revision summary**

- **Natural selection:** individuals **vary**; more are born than survive; those **best suited** to the environment are more likely to **survive and reproduce**; they pass on their **alleles**; over **generations**, the population changes.
- **Individuals don't change** to suit the environment; the population does.
- "Fittest" means best suited to the **current** environment. Selection needs **variation**.
- Over millions of years, populations split and diverge: all living things are related in a **branching tree of life**, not a ladder. The more recent the **common ancestor**, the more similar the DNA.
- Features can mislead about relatedness (fungi are closer to animals than to plants), so modern classification uses shared ancestry, and changes as evidence improves.
