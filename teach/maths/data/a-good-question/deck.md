# A good question

Data and chance · Year 10: two-way tables and claims

<!-- Speaker note: Students turn a vague wondering about sleep into an investigation question with two named variables, a group, a measure for each and a direction. ## Today

- I can name the two variables in a question.
- I can say which variable might explain the other.
- I can rewrite a vague question so it names who, what is measured and how.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Investigate tasks yourself. The context is whether hours slept goes with reaction time, measured by a ruler drop in centimetres. The one idea: a question is ready when a stranger could collect the data from it. It names the group (Year 10 students at our school), both variables (hours slept last night, ruler-drop distance), how each is measured, and which variable might explain the other (hours slept is explanatory, reaction time the response). This is a hinge node: a vague question carries through collecting and judging, so the next step (collecting and displaying) has nothing to aim at. It has no smaller step because it is a judgement, and a smaller step would hand over the answer. Zoe's question ('Does sleep matter?') names one variable and no measure. The usual slips are leaving out the group, leaving out the measure, and picking a variable nobody can measure. Every figure and person is made up. Real reaction-time tests (ruler drop, online tests) are common school activities, but none is quoted here. -->

## Which is the explainer?

**In 'is hours slept related to reaction time?', which is the variable that might explain the other (the explanatory variable), and which is the response?**

- Explanatory: reaction time. Response: hours slept
- Both are explanatory
- Neither: they just happen
- Explanatory: hours slept. Response: reaction time

* Answer: Explanatory: hours slept. Response: reaction time

## The better question

**Which rewrite of Zoe's question fixes it?**

- Is sleep good?
- Among Year 10 students at our school, do students who slept fewer hours last night have slower reaction times on a ruler-drop test?
- Does sleep matter to everyone in the world?
- Do people who sleep a lot like school?

* Answer: Among Year 10 students at our school, do students who slept fewer hours last night have slower reaction times on a ruler-drop test?

<!-- Speaker note: It names the group, both variables, how reaction time is measured and the direction the class expects (fewer hours, slower). -->

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Making a question specific about who is asked and how things are measured, and noticing when a question can't be answered by data, builds the habit before collecting. For students who need more support: A frame: 'Among ___, is ___ (measured by ___) related to ___ (measured by ___)?' For early finishers: Write a second question about the same two variables with a different group, and say how the answer might differ. The hard part, on purpose: Writing the question with all parts. Fix: Choose a measure for each, such as hours slept last night and a test mark or a ruler-drop distance. / A student leaves out who is asked. Ask: Everyone in the world, or one class? Fix: Name the group the question is about and who will actually be asked. / A student thinks a question is good because it is interesting.
