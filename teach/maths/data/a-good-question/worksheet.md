:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students turn a vague wondering about sleep into an investigation question with two named variables, a group, a measure for each and a direction. The context is sleep and reaction time in a Year 10 class, with made-up figures (AC9M10ST05, node 1).

**Students should be able to say:** I can name the two variables in a question. I can say which variable might explain the other. I can rewrite a vague question so it names who, what is measured and how.

## From a wondering to a question


:::keep-together

### Which is the explainer?

**In 'is hours slept related to reaction time?', which is the variable that might explain the other (the explanatory variable), and which is the response?**

☐ Explanatory: reaction time. Response: hours slept

☐ Both are explanatory

☐ Neither: they just happen

☐ Explanatory: hours slept. Response: reaction time

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Explanatory: hours slept. Response: reaction time

Hours slept last night could explain the reaction time today, so hours slept is explanatory and reaction time is the response.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find the vague question

**Zoe plans the investigation. Click the line where her plan stops being a usable question.**

```
 1  My topic is sleep and how students do at school.
 2  My question: Does sleep matter?
 3  I will ask whoever I see at lunch how they slept.
 4  Then I will see what they say.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 2

Line 2: 'Does sleep matter?' names one thing, 'sleep', and 'matter' for what is not said. A usable question names both variables, such as hours slept and reaction time.

:::


:::keep-together

### The better question

**Which rewrite of Zoe's question fixes it?**

☐ Is sleep good?

☐ Among Year 10 students at our school, do students who slept fewer hours last night have slower reaction times on a ruler-drop test?

☐ Does sleep matter to everyone in the world?

☐ Do people who sleep a lot like school?

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Among Year 10 students at our school, do students who slept fewer hours last night have slower reaction times on a ruler-drop test?

It names the group, both variables, how reaction time is measured and the direction the class expects (fewer hours, slower).

:::


:::keep-together

### The display

**Hours slept and ruler-drop distance are both numbers. Which display fits the data once it is collected?**

☐ A bar chart with one bar per student

☐ A scatterplot with hours slept across and ruler drop up

☐ No display: a table is enough

☐ A pie chart of the hours slept

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** A scatterplot with hours slept across and ruler drop up

Two numerical variables go on a scatterplot, which shows direction and strength (the last unit).

### Review

**Review: five students slept $6$, $7$, $8$, $8$ and $6$ hours. What is the mean number of hours?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 7

6 + 7 + 8 + 8 + 6 = 35, and 35 ÷ 5 = 7.

## Make: your own question


**Your question.** Write it down, then swap with a partner. Can they say the two variables, the group and how each is measured without asking you?

**The checklist.** All the figures and people here are made up: the class, the sleep log and the reaction times.

**Also used in**

- **Science.** Every fair test starts with a question that names what is changed and what is measured.
- **News.** Surveys that make headlines are only as good as their questions. Check who was asked and how it was measured.
- **Next steps.** Collecting the data and describing the pattern with a scatterplot, then judging what it shows.

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?
