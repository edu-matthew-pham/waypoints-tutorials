# Linked, but not the cause

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Months with more ice-cream sales also have more drownings. Should the council ban ice cream?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students see that a strong link between two things leaves the cause open: one may cause the other, a third thing may cause both, or it may be chance. The context is ice-cream sales and drownings, with a year of made-up monthly figures (AC9M10ST03, node 3).

**Students should be able to say:** I can explain why two things that go together do not always cause each other. I can suggest a third thing that could be behind both. I can write a reply that says what the data show and what they do not.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

## Association is not cause


:::keep-together

### Shoe size and reading

Here is how to look for a third thing, with the shoe size and reading example.

1. $\text{Pattern: bigger shoes, higher reading mark}$ — Students with larger feet tend to score higher. That is a link between the two.
2. $\text{Does one cause the other?}$ — Would bigger shoes make you read better? Would reading make your feet grow? Neither sounds right.
3. $\text{Look for a third thing}$ — Age or year level. Older students have bigger feet, and they have had more practice at reading.
4. $\text{Test it: compare students of the same year level}$ — If the link almost disappears inside each year level, year level was behind both.

Now try the school figures on paper first, with the worked example beside you. The numbers are made up.

**In a school (made up), students with large shoes average $66$ on a reading test and students with small shoes average $54$. Looking only inside Year 4, large shoes average $53$ and small shoes $52$. Looking only inside Year 6, large shoes average $69$ and small shoes $68$. The gap is the same inside each year level. By how many marks does the gap shrink when only students of the same year level are compared?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Overall gap: $66 - 54 = 12$ (the gap in marks between large and small shoes for everyone); Year 4 gap: $53 - 52 = 1$, Year 6 gap: $69 - 68 = 1$ (the gap inside each year level); $12 - 1 = 11$ (the gap shrinks by 11 marks); Most of the link comes from year level (older students have bigger feet and read better, so year level is behind both)

Overall the gap is 66 − 54 = 12. Inside each year level it is only 1. So the gap shrinks by 12 − 1 = 11 marks: almost all of the link was year level, not shoe size.

:::

**Which statement do the three lines support?**

☐ Temperature has nothing to do with either

☐ In the warm months, ice-cream sales, drownings and temperature are all high, and in the cool months all are low

☐ Drownings cause ice-cream sales

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** In the warm months, ice-cream sales, drownings and temperature are all high, and in the cool months all are low

All three lines are high in summer and low in winter. That is a link between each pair, which is what the data show. Why they are linked needs more than the lines.

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

**Answer:** line 4

Line 4: the plot shows that ice-cream sales and drownings go together, which is line 3. It does not show that ice cream causes the drownings. Something else, like hot weather, could be behind both.

### The best reason

**Which is the best explanation for ice-cream sales and drownings both being high in the same months?**

☐ Ice cream makes swimmers tired

☐ It must be chance, since the two have nothing in common

☐ Hot weather: more people buy ice cream and more people swim

☐ Drownings make people sad, so they buy ice cream

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Hot weather: more people buy ice cream and more people swim

Temperature is a third thing, behind both. In hot months more people buy ice cream and more people swim, so there are more chances of a drowning.

### Front-row marks

**Sia says: 'Students who sit at the front get better marks, so moving a student to the front will raise their mark.' What is the best reply?**

☐ She is right: the data show the cause

☐ She needs a bigger plot, and then she will be sure

☐ She is wrong: seating never matters

☐ Maybe not: keen students may choose the front, so keenness could be behind both

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Maybe not: keen students may choose the front, so keenness could be behind both

The link may be due to something behind both, such as how keen a student is. Moving someone to the front tests whether seating itself matters.

:::
