# Linked, but not the cause

Data and chance · Year 10: association

<!-- Speaker note: Students see that a strong link between two things leaves the cause open: one may cause the other, a third thing may cause both, or it may be chance. ## Today

- I can explain why two things that go together do not always cause each other.
- I can suggest a third thing that could be behind both.
- I can write a reply that says what the data show and what they do not.

## Shoe size and reading

Here is how to look for a third thing, with the shoe size and reading example.

Now try the school figures on paper first, with the worked example beside you. The numbers are made up.

$$\text{Pattern: bigger shoes, higher reading mark}$$

1) $\text{Does one cause the other?}$ — Would bigger shoes make you read better? Would reading make your feet grow? Neither sounds right.
2) $\text{Look for a third thing}$ — Age or year level. Older students have bigger feet, and they have had more practice at reading.
3) $\text{Test it: compare students of the same year level}$ — If the link almost disappears inside each year level, year level was behind both.

## Shoe size and reading

**In a school (made up), students with large shoes average $66$ on a reading test and students with small shoes average $54$. Looking only inside Year 4, large shoes average $53$ and small shoes $52$. Looking only inside Year 6, large shoes average $69$ and small shoes $68$. The gap is the same inside each year level. By how many marks does the gap shrink when only students of the same year level are compared?**

* Answer: Overall gap: $66 - 54 = 12$ (the gap in marks between large and small shoes for everyone); Year 4 gap: $53 - 52 = 1$, Year 6 gap: $69 - 68 = 1$ (the gap inside each year level); $12 - 1 = 11$ (the gap shrinks by 11 marks); Most of the link comes from year level (older students have bigger feet and read better, so year level is behind both)

<!-- Speaker note: Overall the gap is 66 − 54 = 12. Inside each year level it is only 1. So the gap shrinks by 12 − 1 = 11 marks: almost all of the link was year level, not shoe size. -->

## Find the mistake

**Eli decides what the council should do. Click the line where his reasoning goes wrong.**

```
 1  In the six coolest months, ice-cream sales are low and drownings are low.
 2  In the six warmest months, both are high.
 3  So more ice-cream sales go with more drownings.
 4  So ice cream must be causing the drownings.
 5  The council should ban it.
```

* Answer: line 4

<!-- Speaker note: Line 4: the plot shows that ice-cream sales and drownings go together, which is line 3. It does not show that ice cream causes the drownings. Something else, like hot weather, could be behind both. -->

## The best reason

**Which is the best explanation for ice-cream sales and drownings both being high in the same months?**

- Ice cream makes swimmers tired
- It must be chance, since the two have nothing in common
- Hot weather: more people buy ice cream and more people swim
- Drownings make people sad, so they buy ice cream

* Answer: Hot weather: more people buy ice cream and more people swim

<!-- Speaker note: Temperature is a third thing, behind both. In hot months more people buy ice cream and more people swim, so there are more chances of a drowning. -->

## Exit ticket

**Sales of sunscreen and cases of sunburn rise together. Does that show sunscreen causes sunburn? Name a third thing.**

* No. Both rise on hot, sunny days: more people go out, so more buy sunscreen and more get sunburnt. The sun is behind both.
