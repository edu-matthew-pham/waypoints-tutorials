# Tables, Venns and trees

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** A screening test comes back positive. Does that mean the person has the condition?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students fill in a two-way table for 1000 people from stated rates, read the chance of the condition given a positive test inside the Positive column, and see the same four counts as a Venn diagram and a tree. The context is a made-up screening test for a rare condition (AC9M10P01, node 2).

**Students should be able to say:** I can build a two-way table from rates by starting with a group of people. I can read a chance given a group off a table, a Venn diagram or a tree. I can say why a test that is usually right can be wrong most of the time it says positive.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

### Given a positive test

A table, a Venn diagram and a tree all show the same four counts. Here is how to read the chance off a table.

1. $\text{A scanner checks 1000 phone cases (made up). 40 are faulty.}$ — Start from a group and sort it by the two things: faulty or not, flagged or not.
2. $36 \text{ faulty cases are flagged; } 48 \text{ of the } 960 \text{ good cases are flagged too}$ — Fill in the table from the rates: the flagged column holds 36 and 48.
3. $36 + 48 = 84 \text{ cases are flagged}$ — "Given a flag" picks this group.
4. $P(\text{faulty} \mid \text{flagged}) = \tfrac{36}{84} = \tfrac{3}{7}$ — The faulty cases inside the group, over the group.

Now try one on paper first, with the example beside you. This test is also made up. Then type your answer.

**A different screening test (made up). Of 1000 people, 50 have the condition. The test is positive for 45 of those 50 and, wrongly, for 38 of the 950 others. Given a positive test, what is the chance the person has the condition? Give a decimal to 2 places.**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** All the positives: $45 + 38 = 83$ (the group the 'given' picks); Positives with the condition: $45$ (the count you want inside the group); $45 \div 83 = 0.54$ (the part over the group)

83 people test positive, 45 of them have the condition: 45 ÷ 83 = 0.54. About 1 in 2.

:::


:::keep-together

```
 3  So the chance of having the condition, given a positive test, is 9 in 10 = 0.9.
 4  That means a positive result is right nine times out of ten.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: 9 in 10 is the chance of a positive test, given the condition. She needs the chance of the condition, given a positive test, and the positives include 49 people without it: 18 out of 67.

## Reading the table


:::keep-together

### Given a positive

**Of 1000 people (made up), 100 have a condition. The test is positive for 90 of them and wrongly for 90 of the 900 others. Given a positive test, what is the chance the person has the condition? Give a decimal.**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 0.5

180 people test positive and 90 have the condition: 90 ÷ 180 = 0.5.

:::


:::keep-together

### Given the condition

**Using the lesson's counts (18 have the condition and test positive, 2 have it and test negative, 49 don't have it and test positive, 931 don't and test negative), what is the chance of a positive test, given the person has the condition? Give a decimal.**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 0.9

The group is the 20 people with the condition and 18 test positive: 18 ÷ 20 = 0.9.

:::


:::keep-together

### A review

**Review: of 100 people, 55 play sport, 30 play music and 10 play both. How many play neither?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 25

55 + 30 − 10 = 75 play something, so 100 − 75 = 25 play neither.
