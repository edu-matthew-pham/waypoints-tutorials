## Same fact, two frames

**A council report says: "Only 12% of residents are unhappy with the new bus timetable." A second report on the same survey says: "Nearly one in eight residents are unhappy with the new bus timetable." What is the best description?**

- The same fact, framed in two ways that can push readers differently
- One of them must have the wrong number
- Neither report can be trusted without a bigger sample
- The second report is dishonest because it uses a fraction

* Answer: The same fact, framed in two ways that can push readers differently

<!-- Speaker note: 12% and one in eight are the same fact. "Only" and "nearly" are chosen to push the reader towards 'not many' or 'quite a lot', so the framing shapes the claim. (The survey is made up.) -->

## True but misleading

**A company reports: "Our drink has no added sugar!" Another company's report on its own drink says: "Cuts sugar by 40%!" Both figures are true. Which is the most important ethical question to ask about each?**

- How many people wrote the report
- Whether the percentage is a whole number
- What does the claim leave out, and who benefits from it being believed?
- Which company has the bigger factory?

* Answer: What does the claim leave out, and who benefits from it being believed?

<!-- Speaker note: A claim can be true and still misleading. Ask what it leaves out (is there other sugar? 40% of what?) and who gains if you believe it. (Both drinks and claims are made up.) -->

## Name the problem

**Match each situation to the ethical problem it shows.**

| | |
|---|---|
| `A report leaves out the results from the group that did worst` | A. Misleading presentation |
| `A graph for a school newsletter uses a scale that starts at 80 to make a small gain look big` | B. Leaving out inconvenient data |
| `A survey says "most students" want a change, but only 6 students were asked` | C. A conflict of interest |
| `A report is paid for by a company and only shows the results that help its product` | D. Overstating what the data shows |

* Answer: A report leaves out the results from the group that did worst → B. Leaving out inconvenient data; A graph for a school newsletter uses a scale that starts at 80 to make a small gain look big → A. Misleading presentation; A survey says "most students" want a change, but only 6 students were asked → D. Overstating what the data shows; A report is paid for by a company and only shows the results that help its product → C. A conflict of interest

## A fair plan

**You want to find out how many hours your classmates sleep. Which plan is the most ethical?**

- Look at who is tired in class and record your guess
- Ask the whole class to say their hours out loud, one by one
- Ask volunteers to answer without their names, and tell them how the results will be used
- Ask teachers to read out each student's answers

* Answer: Ask volunteers to answer without their names, and tell them how the results will be used

## Handling the results

**Is this an ethical way to handle the data, or does it need to change?**

Groups: Fine · Needs to change

Taking part is voluntary and a student can skip any question · The results table lists each student's name beside their answer, and is pinned on the wall · Students are told the results will be used for a class report · The survey asks students whether their family has enough money, and the answers are shared with the year level

* Answer: Taking part is voluntary and a student can skip any question → Fine; The results table lists each student's name beside their answer, and is pinned on the wall → Needs to change; Students are told the results will be used for a class report → Fine; The survey asks students whether their family has enough money, and the answers are shared with the year level → Needs to change
