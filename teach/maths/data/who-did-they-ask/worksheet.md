# Who did they ask?

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** A football club's website poll gets 50 000 votes. Does that settle what the city thinks?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students judge whether a report's sample fits its claim by asking who answered, how many, how they were chosen and who is missing, and see that a small random sample can beat a huge self-chosen one (AC9M10ST01, node 2).

**Students should be able to say:** I can say who a report's numbers describe. I can explain why a very large sample can still be a poor one. I can use a fair sample's percentage to estimate for a population.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Source, size, how chosen, who's missing


:::keep-together

### Judging a report

1. $\text{Who answered?}$ — People leaving a café who were asked at the door.
2. $\text{How many?}$ — 300 people.
3. $\text{How were they chosen?}$ — By being easy to reach, not at random.
4. $\text{Who is missing?}$ — Everyone who doesn't use the café.
5. $\text{Verdict}$ — It fits café customers, not the whole town.

Now try one yourself: a shopping centre asks 300 people leaving its café whether the town needs more cafés, and 81% say yes. Say what the biggest problem is.

**A shopping centre asks 300 people leaving its café whether the town needs more cafés, and 81% say yes. What is the biggest problem with the claim 'the town wants more cafés'?**

☐ 81% is too high to be true

☐ The people asked were café customers picked for being easy to reach, so people who don't use cafés are missing

☐ The centre asked people outside

☐ 300 is a small number, so the claim is always wrong

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** The people asked were café customers picked for being easy to reach, so people who don't use cafés are missing

The sample was chosen for being convenient and it is full of café-goers. It can say what café customers think, but not what the town thinks.

:::


:::keep-together

### How were they chosen

**How were the club poll's voters chosen?**

☐ A random draw from the electoral roll

☐ They chose themselves: it was a voluntary poll on the club's site

☐ One person from each suburb

☐ Every tenth person in the city

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** They chose themselves: it was a voluntary poll on the club's site

A voluntary poll lets the people who care most answer. They can be very different from everyone else.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find the mistake

**Lee reads the club's poll. Click the line where the reasoning goes wrong.**

```
 1  The club's poll got 50 000 votes, and 92% of them said yes.
 2  That is a very large sample.
 3  A sample this big must be reliable.
 4  So about 92% of the whole city wants the bigger stadium.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: a big sample is only reliable if it is a fair slice of the population. These 50 000 chose themselves on the club's site, so more of them doesn't make them more like the city.

:::


:::keep-together

### Who does it describe

**A headline says 'teenagers spend 6 hours a day on screens'. The survey was a pop-up on a gaming website, and 1 200 players clicked and answered. Who does the 6 hours describe?**

☐ Everyone with a phone

☐ Teenagers who never use screens

☐ Gamers who visited that site and chose to answer

☐ All teenagers

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Gamers who visited that site and chose to answer

The result describes the people who were asked. To say it of all teenagers you'd need a sample drawn fairly from all teenagers.

:::


:::keep-together

### Who didn't answer

**A school emails a survey to all 1 800 students and 140 answer. What might be wrong with using the 140 to speak for the school?**

☐ Emails can't carry surveys

☐ The 140 who bothered to answer might differ from the 1 660 who didn't

☐ The school emailed everyone, which is always biased

☐ 140 is too many to be reliable

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** The 140 who bothered to answer might differ from the 1 660 who didn't

When most people don't answer, the ones who do may feel more strongly than the rest. This is non-response.

:::

## Reading a random sample


:::keep-together

### Percent in favour

**In a random sample of 250 students, 140 chose the pool for the end-of-year party. What percentage is that?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 56

140 ÷ 250 = 0.56, so 56%.

:::


:::keep-together

### Estimate for the school

**Use that 56% to estimate how many of the school's 1 500 students would choose the pool.**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 840

0.56 × 1 500 = 840. It's an estimate: a different random sample could give a slightly different answer.

:::


:::keep-together

### Review

**Review: a website poll gets 4 000 votes and 75% say yes. How many votes were yes?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 3000

75% of 4 000 = 0.75 × 4 000 = 3 000. It's a count of those who voted, not of the city.

:::
