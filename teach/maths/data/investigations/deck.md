## Today

**We are learning to** an investigation asks a question that data can answer, plans who to measure and how, collects and displays the data, describes its shape, centre and spread, and reports an answer with its limits.

- I can write a question that data can answer.
- I can plan a fair sample and name a possible bias.
- I can report what data show using shape, centre and spread, and say the limits.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Run board yourself first. Students need the data lessons before this one (kinds of data, centre, stem-and-leaf plots, comparing data). The one idea: the answer is only as good as the question and the sample. A small, friendly sample describes that group and not the whole year (Kai's mistake). This lesson gives the shape of a whole investigation with sleep as the example. The real investigation is a class task: students choose their own question (sleep, screen time, reaction time, jumping), collect data in class, display it on the data board or a spreadsheet and write the report in the four parts of the Make task. Do at least two investigations in different contexts, so the skill transfers. A public source (the Australian Bureau of Statistics, for example) can give a second set for a social question; use it as an extension. -->

## Why it matters

> Do students your age sleep less on school nights? You could argue about it, or you could measure it. What would you need to decide first?

<!-- Speaker note: A **statistical investigation** goes through these stages: 1. **Ask** a statistical question: one that data can answer, with a variable you can measure ("How many hours do Year 7 students sleep on school nights?"). 2. **Plan**: decide the variable (**discrete** if counted, **continuous** if measured), the **sample** (who, and how many) and how to measure it. Look out for **bias**, where the sample or the measuring favours one answer. 3. **Collect** the data, **display** it (dot plot, stem-and-leaf plot), and **summarise** it: shape, centre and spread. 4. **Report** an answer to the question, with its limits (who was measured, how many, how). -->

## Find the mistake

**Kai wants to know how long Year 7 students sleep. Click the line where he draws a conclusion he cannot support.**

```
 1  Kai's question: how many hours do Year 7 students sleep on school nights?
 2  He asks the 8 students in his swimming squad.
 3  Their median is 8 hours, so Year 7 students sleep 8 hours.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: eight students from one squad are a small group and not a fair sample of Year 7. Swimmers may sleep more or less than others. The result describes his squad. -->

## A fair sample

**Which is the fairest way to choose 20 students from Year 7?**

- Ask those who put up their hands
- Ask your friends
- Pick 20 names at random from the class lists
- Ask the first 20 students at the canteen

* Answer: Pick 20 names at random from the class lists

<!-- Speaker note: Random selection gives every student the same chance and avoids bias. -->

## Exit ticket

**A survey asks only the first 10 students into school how they travel. Give one reason it may not represent the school.**

* Students who arrive early may travel differently (for example, by bus or parent drop-off), so the sample is biased.
