# A careful claim

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** In the survey, 75% of riders play sport and 40% of bus or car students do. Can the school put up a poster saying 'riding makes you sporty'?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students decide what a two-way table lets them say about a link: name the groups, give the percentages, say 'more likely', and keep 'causes' for more evidence. The context is the school survey on travel and sport, with made-up counts (AC9M10ST04, node 3).

**Students should be able to say:** I can say how much higher one group's percentage is, in points and in times as likely. I can write a claim from a table that names its groups and stops short of 'causes'. I can say what more I would need to find a cause.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## What the table can and can't say


:::keep-together

### Writing it carefully

Here is how to build the sentence.

1. $\text{Name the groups}$ — Students who ride, and students who come by bus or car.
2. $75\% \text{ against } 40\%$ — Give both percentages, each of its own group.
3. $\text{Say 'more likely', not 'because'}$ — The table shows who plays sport more often, not why.
4. $\text{Say where it comes from}$ — 'In this survey' keeps the claim to the 200 students asked.

**How many times as likely are riders to play sport as bus or car students? (2 decimal places)**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 1.88

75 ÷ 40 = 1.875, about 1.88. Riders are nearly twice as likely to play sport.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find the step too far

**Ben writes the school's report. Click the line where his reasoning goes further than the table.**

```
 1  The table shows 75% of riders play sport and 40% of bus or car students do.
 2  That is a big difference, so students who ride are more likely to play sport.
 3  So riding to school makes students play sport.
 4  The school should give every student a bike.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: the table shows that riders are more likely to play sport, which is line 2. It does not show that riding is the reason. Something else, like how close students live to the school, could be behind both.

:::


:::keep-together

### A third thing

**Which is a possible third thing that could sit behind both riding to school and playing sport?**

☐ Living close to school, so riding is easy and there is time and a nearby field

☐ Having a bike is always the cause of playing sport

☐ Nothing: a link in a table has no other explanation

☐ Playing sport makes the bike lighter

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Living close to school, so riding is easy and there is time and a nearby field

Students who live close can ride, and may also have time and a place to play. That would give the link without riding causing anything.

:::


:::keep-together

### The careful sentence

**Which sentence is the most careful claim from the table?**

☐ Bus and car students don't like sport

☐ Riding to school makes students play sport

☐ Every student who rides plays sport

☐ In this survey, students who ride were more likely to play sport (75%) than students who come by bus or car (40%)

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** In this survey, students who ride were more likely to play sport (75%) than students who come by bus or car (40%)

It names the group ('students who ride'), gives the percentages, says 'in this survey' and says 'more likely', which is what the table shows.

## Choosing words


:::keep-together

### Times as likely

**In group A, $60\%$ play sport, and in group B, $20\%$ do. How many times as likely are students in group A to play sport?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 3

60 ÷ 20 = 3 times as likely.

:::


:::keep-together

### Choosing the words

**A table shows students who eat breakfast pass a quiz more often than those who don't. Which word fits the table best?**

☐ Students who eat breakfast are more likely to pass

☐ Breakfast makes students pass

☐ Breakfast is unrelated to passing

☐ Passing makes students eat breakfast

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Students who eat breakfast are more likely to pass

'More likely' says what the table shows. 'Makes' needs more than a table.

:::


:::keep-together

### Review

**Review: $40$ students ride and $35\%$ of them have a helmet with a sticker. How many is that?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 14

35% of 40 = 0.35 × 40 = 14.

:::


## Make: your own careful claim


**The gap.** Work this out on paper first, then type your answer.

**The claim.** All the figures here are made up: the school, the survey counts, the breakfast quiz table.

**Also used in**

- **News.** Headlines often turn 'more likely' into 'causes'. Check the table behind them.
- **Health.** Studies that only record who did what, without changing anything, can show a link and leave the cause open.
- **Next lesson.** A good question: planning an investigation with two variables that a table or plot could answer.

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?
