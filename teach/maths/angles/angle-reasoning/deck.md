# Reasoning with angles

Angles and shapes · Parallel lines

<!-- Speaker note: Students find unknown angles at parallel lines in two-step chains, with a reason for each step, writing their working their own way; the platform marks the answer and the reason at the key step, then shows a model to compare with (AC9M7M04, node 3). About 45 minutes (Predict and Run 8 min · Investigate 15 min · Modify 12 min · Practise and Make 10 min). -->

---

## Today

**We are learning to** find unknown angles in steps, giving a reason for each: what you know, the rule that links it to the next angle, and the reason in words. Parallel-line rules join straight-line and vertically opposite angles.

- I can find an unknown angle in two or more steps, using parallel-line, straight-line and vertically opposite angles.
- I can give a reason for each step, naming the rule.
- I can use angles to show whether two lines are parallel.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the first reasoning task yourself first, then compare with its model. Students need both parallel-lines lessons and Year 6 angle facts (straight line, vertically opposite, at a point). The one idea: know, choose the rule, say why. Every angle found needs a named reason. The common mistake is linking two angles that only look related (Kai's b and e); it is planted after the routine. The reasoning tasks do not mark the free working: students write it their own way (notes, abbreviations such as "vert. opp." and "co-int." are fine). The answer is marked, and at one key step students choose the reason from a list, so the misconception gets feedback. Then they compare their working with a model and tick a short self-check. Look at the working in class: it is where reasoning shows. -->

---

## Why it matters

> A detective does not guess: every step has evidence. Finding an angle is the same. Each angle you find needs a reason, and the reasons are what make the answer trustworthy.

<!-- Speaker note: You know the rules: corresponding and alternate angles are equal, co-interior angles add to $180^\circ$, and from Year 6, vertically opposite angles are equal and angles on a straight line add to $180^\circ$. Most problems need more than one rule. In this lesson you find angles **in steps**, and for each step you say which rule you used. Write your working your own way: short notes are fine. The checker marks your answer and the reason at the step that matters; you compare the rest with a model. -->

## Know, choose, say why

Here is the routine for every step: what you know, the rule that links it to a new angle, and the reason.

Now find $h$ yourself. Write it your own way; the checker marks your answer and the reason for the first step, then shows you a model to compare.

$$b = 70^\circ$$

1) $f = 70^\circ$ — Choose: $f$ is in the same place as $b$ at the other crossing. Say why: corresponding angles are equal.
2) $h = 180^\circ - 70^\circ$ — Choose: $h$ is next to $f$ on the bottom line. Say why: angles on a straight line add to $180^\circ$.
3) $h = 110^\circ$ — Done: two steps, two reasons.

## Exit ticket

**A transversal crosses two parallel lines, making a 120° angle. Find the angle alternate to it and the angle co-interior to it, with reasons.**

* 120° (alternate angles are equal) and 180° − 120° = 60° (co-interior angles add to 180°).
