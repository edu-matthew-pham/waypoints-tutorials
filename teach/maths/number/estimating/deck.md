# Estimating to check

Number Foundations · Place value and estimating

<!-- Speaker note: Students estimate by rounding each number to its first digit, compare the estimate with the exact answer, and use it to catch slips such as a misplaced decimal point (AC9M7N05, node 3). About 40 minutes (Predict and Run 8 min · Investigate 12 min · Modify 10 min · Practise and Make 10 min). -->

---

## Today

**We are learning to** round each number to its first digit, then calculate: 38 × 52 ≈ 40 × 50 = 2000. If the calculator says 197.6 or 19 760, something slipped.

- I can estimate a calculation by rounding each number to its first digit.
- I can compare an estimate with the exact answer and say whether it is reasonable.
- I can use an estimate to find a slip, such as a decimal point in the wrong place.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Run board yourself first. Students need rounding (previous lesson) and multiplying by multiples of 10 (Year 6). The one idea: round to the leading digit and keep the size, calculate the easy numbers, and compare with the exact answer. The commonest mistakes are losing the size (Lina rounds 19.6 to 2), losing a zero in the calculation, and trusting a calculator answer because it looks plausible. Estimation is only worth teaching if it is used. In class, make students estimate before pressing = for the whole lesson. -->

---

## Why it matters

> You type 3.2 × 48 and the calculator shows 15.36. Looks plausible. But before you trust it, how big should the answer be?

<!-- Speaker note: An **estimate** is a quick answer that is close, found by rounding the numbers to something easy. A good habit: round each number to its **first digit** (keep its size), do the easy calculation, and compare with the real answer. $$38 \times 52 \approx 40 \times 50 = 2000$$ If the exact answer is near the estimate, it is **reasonable**. If it is ten times too big or too small, check the decimal point. -->

## Find the mistake

**Lina estimates 19.6 × 4.1. Click the line where her working goes wrong.**

```
 1  Estimate 19.6 × 4.1.
 2  Round each number to its first digit: 19.6 → 2, 4.1 → 4.
 3  2 × 4 = 8
 4  So 19.6 × 4.1 is about 8.
```

* Answer: line 2

<!-- Speaker note: Line 2: 19.6 is about 20, not 2: rounding keeps the size. 20 × 4 = 80, so 19.6 × 4.1 is about 80. -->

## Is it reasonable?

**You type $3.2 \times 48$ and the calculator shows 15.36. Is that reasonable?**

- No: 3 × 50 = 150, so the decimal point is in the wrong place
- Yes: a calculator is always right
- Yes: 3 × 5 = 15 is close
- No: the answer should be a whole number

* Answer: No: 3 × 50 = 150, so the decimal point is in the wrong place

<!-- Speaker note: 3.2 × 48 ≈ 3 × 50 = 150. The real answer is 153.6: the calculator shows 15.36 only if the 3.2 was typed as 0.32. -->

## Check the calculator

**The calculator shows $504 \div 6.3 = 8$. Is that reasonable?**

- Yes: 50 ÷ 6 is about 8
- Yes: 504 ÷ 6 is about 8
- No: 500 ÷ 6 is about 80, so 8 has lost a zero
- No: you cannot divide by a decimal

* Answer: No: 500 ÷ 6 is about 80, so 8 has lost a zero
