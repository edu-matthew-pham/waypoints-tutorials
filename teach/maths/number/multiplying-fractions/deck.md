# Multiplying and dividing fractions

Number Foundations · Fractions and decimals

<!-- Speaker note: Students use a fraction area square to see a fraction of a fraction, multiply tops and bottoms, learn that multiplying by a fraction less than 1 makes smaller, and divide by counting how many fit (AC9M7N06, node 2). ## Today

**We are learning to** a/b × c/d is c/d of a/b: multiply the tops and multiply the bottoms. Multiplying by a fraction less than 1 makes a number smaller. Dividing asks how many fit: 3 ÷ 1/4 = 12.

- I can multiply two fractions and simplify the answer.
- I can divide a fraction by a whole number and by a fraction.
- I can say whether a product will be bigger or smaller than the number I started with.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Run board yourself first. Students need equivalent fractions and simplifying, and adding fractions (previous lesson) to contrast. The one idea: a/b × c/d is c/d of a/b; the parts multiply, so the tops and the bottoms multiply. The hinge misconception is that multiplying always makes bigger (Jack's mistake); it matters for percentages next. Division is introduced as "how many fit", which justifies multiplying by the flipped fraction without a rule to memorise. -->

## Why it matters

> Half of a pizza is left, and you eat a third of what is left. How much of the whole pizza did you eat? Fractions of fractions are multiplications.

<!-- Speaker note: "Of" means multiply: $\tfrac{1}{3}$ of 12 is $\tfrac{1}{3} \times 12 = 4$. A fraction of a fraction works the same way: $\tfrac{3}{4}$ of $\tfrac{2}{3}$ is $\tfrac{2}{3} \times \tfrac{3}{4}$. To multiply fractions, **multiply the tops and multiply the bottoms**: no common denominator needed. A square cut into columns and rows shows why: the parts multiply, and so do the shaded ones. -->

## Multiply across; divide by flipping

Here is how each kind works.

Now try one: $\tfrac{2}{3} \times \tfrac{3}{5}$.

$$\tfrac{2}{3} \times \tfrac{3}{4} = \tfrac{2 \times 3}{3 \times 4} = \tfrac{6}{12} = \tfrac{1}{2}$$

1) $\tfrac{3}{4} \times 20 = \tfrac{60}{4} = 15$ — A whole number is over 1: 20 = 20/1. 15 is less than 20.
2) $\tfrac{1}{2} \div 3 = \tfrac{1}{6}$ — Share a half into 3: each share is a sixth (the same as × 1/3).
3) $3 \div \tfrac{1}{4} = 3 \times 4 = 12$ — How many quarters in 3? Dividing by 1/4 is multiplying by 4.
4) $\tfrac{2}{3} \div \tfrac{1}{6} = \tfrac{2}{3} \times 6 = 4$ — Divide by a fraction: multiply by it flipped (1/6 flipped is 6/1 = 6).
5) $0.4 \times 3 = 1.2$ — Decimals: 4 tenths × 3 = 12 tenths.

**Jack compares $\tfrac{3}{4} \times 20$ with 20. Click the line where his reasoning goes wrong.**

 1  Which is bigger: 3/4 × 20, or 20?

 4  So 3/4 × 20 is bigger than 20.

* Answer: line 3
