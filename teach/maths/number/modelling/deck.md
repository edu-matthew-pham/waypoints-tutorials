# Modelling with money

Number Foundations · Modelling

<!-- Speaker note: Students model money problems (discounts, rises, markups, profit and sharing) by formulating what is known and unknown, choosing a representation, solving, and interpreting and justifying the answer in dollars (AC9M7N09, nodes 1 to 3). About 45 minutes (Predict and Run 8 min · Investigate 12 min · Modify 12 min · Practise and Make 10 min). -->

---

## Today

**We are learning to** discounts, markups and profit: formulate the problem, choose fractions, percentages or ratios, solve, and say what the answer means in dollars.

- I can say what is known and unknown in a money problem and choose how to model it.
- I can solve problems with fractions, percentages and ratios, including percentages in two steps.
- I can write my answer in dollars and explain why I chose that representation.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Run board yourself first. Students need percentages of amounts (Percentages), sharing in a ratio, and rounding for money. Three nodes in one lesson: formulate (known, unknown, representation), solve and interpret in dollars, and justify the choice. The two traps are using the wrong base (profit as a percentage of the selling price, Ben) and adding percentages that are of different amounts (20% then 10% is not 30%). Calculators are fine. The estimate comes first, and the answer is written to the cent with units. -->

---

## Why it matters

> A $200 bike is 20% off, and then a coupon takes a further 10% off the new price. Is that the same as 30% off?

<!-- Speaker note: Money problems are ratio and percentage problems with a context. The habit is the same three steps: 1. **Formulate:** what is known (price, discount, cost), what is unknown, and which representation (fraction, decimal, percentage, ratio) fits? 2. **Solve:** choose an efficient calculation, estimate first, and use a calculator where it helps. 3. **Interpret:** say what the answer means, in dollars, with a sensible amount of rounding, and **justify** why you chose that representation. Two traps: a percentage is always *of* something (the **original** amount for profit or discount), and percentages of different amounts cannot be added. -->

## Find the mistake

**Ben finds the percentage profit on a scarf. Click the line where his working goes wrong.**

```
 1  A scarf costs $5 to make and sells for $8.
 2  Profit = 8 − 5 = $3
 3  Percentage profit = 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375 = 37.5%
 4  Answer: 37.5% profit.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: profit is a percentage of the cost, the original amount: 3 ÷ 5 = 0.6 = 60%. Ben divided by the selling price. -->

## Which representation?

**Shop A takes $\tfrac{1}{3}$ off a $90 jacket. Shop B takes 30% off. Which is the better deal, and why?**

- Shop A: $\tfrac{1}{3}$ is about 33%, more than 30%
- They are the same
- Shop B: 30 is bigger than 3
- You cannot tell

* Answer: Shop A: $\tfrac{1}{3}$ is about 33%, more than 30%

<!-- Speaker note: $\tfrac{1}{3}$ of $90 = $30 off; 30% of $90 = $27 off. Writing both as percentages (33⅓% and 30%) makes the comparison immediate. -->

## Fractions of a prize

**A $240 prize is shared: $\tfrac{1}{4}$ to Ann, and the rest equally among 3 others. How much does each of the other three get, in dollars?**

* Answer: 60

<!-- Speaker note: Ann gets $60, leaving $180. $180 ÷ 3 = $60 each. -->

**A $80 jacket is marked up 25% then sold at 25% off the new price. Is the final price more than, less than or equal to $80? Work it out.**
