# Prisms and capacity

Area, volume, circles and Pythagoras · Volume

<!-- Speaker note: Students build triangular prisms in layers, find volume as cross-section area × length for any right prism, and link volume to capacity (1 cm³ = 1 mL, 1000 cm³ = 1 L) (AC9M7M02, node 2). ## Today

**We are learning to** any right prism has the same shape all the way along: its cross-section. Volume = area of the cross-section × length, for triangles too (halve first). Capacity: 1 cm³ holds 1 mL, and 1000 cm³ is a litre.

- I can find the cross-section of a prism.
- I can find the volume of a triangular prism.
- I can change between cm³, mL and litres.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Run board yourself first. Students need V = A × h for boxes (previous lesson) and triangle area (Area unit). The one idea: every right prism is its cross-section repeated along its length, so V = cross-section area × length. ## Why it matters

> A tent, a chocolate bar in a triangular box, a drink carton: how much do they hold? If the shape is the same all the way through, one rule finds them all.

<!-- Speaker note: A **right prism** has the same shape all the way through, like a loaf of bread: cut it anywhere, straight across, and you get the same slice. That slice is the **cross-section**. A box has a rectangle cross-section; a **triangular prism** (a tent, a triangular chocolate box) has a triangle. The layer idea works for every prism. Each layer is the cross-section, and the layers go all the way along: $$V = \text{area of the cross-section} \times \text{length}$$ Volume and **capacity** (how much a container holds) are linked: 1 cm³ holds 1 millilitre, so 1000 cm³ holds 1 litre. -->

## Any prism

Here is the rule for any prism, and capacity.

Now try one: a triangle end with base 6 cm and height 4 cm, 10 cm long.

$$A = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 4 = 12 \text{ cm}^2$$

1) $V = 12 \times 10 = 120 \text{ cm}^3$ — Times the length: one layer of 12 for each centimetre.
2) $120 \text{ cm}^3 = 120 \text{ mL}$ — Capacity: 1 cm³ holds 1 mL.
3) $1000 \text{ cm}^3 = 1 \text{ L}$ — 1000 mL is a litre: a 10 cm cube holds 1 L.
