## Today

**We are learning to** roll any circle out flat and its circumference is just over 3 of its diameters. C ÷ d is the same number for every circle: π (pi), about 3.14. π's digits never end: 3.14 and 22/7 are close, not exact.

- I can explain what π means: how many diameters fit round a circle.
- I can estimate a circumference from a diameter.
- I can say why 3.14 and 22/7 are close to π but not exact.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Run board yourself first, then try it with real objects: string round tins and plates, measured against their diameters. The common mistake is thinking π is exactly 3.14 or 22/7 (Kai's mistake). -->

## Why it matters

> A bike wheel, a coin and the Earth's equator: wildly different sizes, but for each one the distance round is the same number of times the distance across. People have known this for four thousand years.

<!-- Speaker note: The **circumference** of a circle is hard to measure with a ruler, because it is curved. But you can roll the circle along a line for one full turn: the track it leaves is exactly its circumference, laid flat. Compare that track with the **diameter**, and something remarkable happens: for every circle, big or small, the circumference is the same number of diameters. That number is called **π** (the Greek letter pi, said "pie"): $$\pi = \frac{C}{d} \approx 3.14$$ -->

## What π means

Here is what π is, and what it is not.

Now: what does the table show?

$$\pi = C \div d$$

1) $\pi = 3.14159265\ldots$ — Its digits never end and never settle into a repeating pattern.
2) $\pi \approx 3.14 \quad \text{or} \quad \pi \approx \tfrac{22}{7}$ — Two handy approximations (22/7 = 3.142…).
3) $C \approx 3 \times d$ — A quick estimate: a bit more than 3 diameters.
