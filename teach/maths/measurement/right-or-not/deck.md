# Right, acute or obtuse?

Area, volume, circles and Pythagoras · Pythagoras

<!-- Speaker note: Students open and close the angle between two sides to see that c² = a² + b² only at 90°, then use the comparison to classify triangles from their side lengths as right-angled, acute or obtuse, and hunt for Pythagorean triples (AC9M8M06, node 3). ## Today

**We are learning to** pythagoras works backwards: square the two shorter sides and add, then compare with the longest side squared. Equal: right-angled. The longest square is bigger: obtuse. Smaller: acute.

- I can tell from three side lengths whether a triangle is right-angled, acute or obtuse.
- I can explain why the longest side must be c in the test.
- I can find and check Pythagorean triples.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Run board yourself first. Students need Pythagoras (previous two lessons) and acute, right and obtuse triangles (Classifying triangles). The one idea: compare a² + b² with the longest side squared; equal means right-angled, and the bigger angle opposite c pushes c² up. The common mistake is testing with a shorter side as c (Ava's mistake). The test is the converse of Pythagoras' theorem: if a² + b² = c², the angle opposite c is a right angle. It is a separate fact from the theorem (which starts from a right angle); the board's angle slider shows why it holds. The board's angle slider keeps a and b fixed and works out c, so students see c² change continuously. -->

## Why it matters

> Given only three lengths, can you tell whether a triangle has a right angle, without drawing it? Builders do exactly this when they mark out a square corner with a tape measure.

<!-- Speaker note: Pythagoras' theorem only works for right-angled triangles, and that is what makes it a test. Take the three sides of any triangle, call the **longest** one $c$, and compare: | If… | the angle opposite $c$ is… | the triangle is… | |---|---|---| | $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ | 90° | right-angled | | $a^2 + b^2 < c^2$ | more than 90° | obtuse | | $a^2 + b^2 > c^2$ | less than 90° | acute | Whole numbers that fit exactly, like 3, 4, 5, are called **Pythagorean triples**. -->

## The test

Here is the test, step by step.

Now try one: sides 6, 8 and 11.

$$c = 13 \text{ (the longest)}$$

1) $5^2 + 12^2 = 25 + 144 = 169$ — Square the other two and add.
2) $13^2 = 169$ — Square the longest.
3) $169 = 169 \Rightarrow \text{right-angled}$ — Equal: right-angled. Less: obtuse. More: acute.
