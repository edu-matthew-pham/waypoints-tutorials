## Today

**We are learning to** a lake or a leaf has no formula. Lay a grid over it: count the full squares, add half the part squares, and multiply by the area of one square. A finer grid gives a better estimate; straight pieces round the edge estimate the perimeter.

- I can estimate an irregular area by counting full squares and half the part squares.
- I can explain why a finer grid gives a better estimate.
- I can estimate a curved perimeter and say how precise my answers are.

## Why it matters

> How big is a lake? Councils, farmers and park rangers estimate areas like this from maps all the time, with a grid and some careful counting.

<!-- Speaker note: An **irregular shape** has curved or uneven edges, so no formula fits. Estimate instead: - **Area:** put a grid over the shape. Count the **full squares**, and count each **part square** as half (some are mostly in, some mostly out, so on average half). Multiply by the area of one square. A **finer grid** gives a closer estimate. - **Perimeter:** join points on the edge with **straight pieces** and add their lengths. More, shorter pieces follow the curve more closely. Always say how precise your estimate is ("about 25 km², to the nearest whole km²"). -->

## Find the mistake

**Sam estimates his lake's area. Click the line where his method goes wrong.**

```
 1  Sam's lake covers 20 squares completely and part of 14 more.
 2  Each square is 1 km by 1 km, so 1 km².
 3  He counts all 34 squares as 1 km² each.
 4  The lake's area is 34 km².
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: a square the edge passes through is only partly inside. Count each as half: 20 + 7 = 27 km². -->

**A shape covers 30 full squares and part of 12 more. Each square is 1 m². Estimate its area in m².**

* Answer: 36
