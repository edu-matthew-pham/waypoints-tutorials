# Linear or quadratic?

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Why does 60 km/h take so much longer to stop than 50 km/h, when it is only 10 km/h faster?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students use first and second differences to compare a straight-line and a quadratic model of real stopping distances, and say where each fails (AC9M9A05, node 1).

**Students should be able to say:** I can use differences to decide between a line and a curve. I can say when neither model fits exactly. I can say why an under-estimate matters in a real situation.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Differences


:::keep-together

### The jump to 60

To choose a model, look at the differences. Equal first differences mean a line. Equal second differences mean a quadratic.

1. $\text{Table A: } 2, 5, 8, 11$ — Four values at equal steps.
2. $\text{first differences: } 3, 3, 3$ — Equal, so linear.
3. $\text{Table B: } 1, 4, 9, 16$ — Another table.
4. $\text{first differences: } 3, 5, 7$ — Not equal.
5. $\text{second differences: } 2, 2$ — Equal, so quadratic.
6. $\text{real data } 19, 27, 37, 56$ — Neither is exactly equal.
7. $\text{choose the better model and say why}$ — And say where it stops working.

Work out the differences of $19, 27, 37, 56$ on paper, then answer.

**The stopping distances at 30, 40, 50 and 60 km/h are $19, 27, 37, 56$ m. What is the first difference between 50 and 60 km/h?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 19

56 − 37 = 19 m.

:::


:::keep-together

### A straight line?

**The first differences are $8, 10, 19$. What does that say about a straight-line model?**

☐ A line doesn't fit: the distance goes up by more for each extra 10 km/h

☐ A line fits exactly: the differences are all even or odd

☐ A line fits well: the distance always goes up

☐ You can't tell from three differences

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** A line doesn't fit: the distance goes up by more for each extra 10 km/h

A line needs equal first differences. These grow, so the data curves upward.

:::


:::keep-together

### Why it curves

**The distance a car travels while the driver reacts grows in step with speed. The braking distance grows with the square of the speed. What shape is the total stopping distance?**

☐ A straight line

☐ Curved upward: a mix of a straight part and a squared part

☐ A flat line

☐ A curve that bends downward

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Curved upward: a mix of a straight part and a squared part

Reaction distance is linear and braking distance is quadratic, so the total curves upward.

:::


:::keep-together

```
 2  From 30 to 40 km/h the distance goes up by 8 m.
 3  So every 10 km/h more adds 8 m.
 4  At 60 km/h: 19 + 8 + 8 + 8 = 43 m.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: one step doesn't set the pattern. The next differences are 10 and 19, not 8, so the estimate of 43 m is far too low.

## Choose the model


:::keep-together

### Second difference

**A table gives $y = 2, 5, 10, 17$ for $x = 1, 2, 3, 4$. What is the second difference?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 2

First differences 3, 5, 7. Second differences 2, 2.

:::


:::keep-together

### A line?

**A table gives $y = 4, 9, 14, 19$. Which model fits?**

☐ Linear: the first differences are all 5

☐ Exponential

☐ Quadratic: the second differences are 5

☐ Neither

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Linear: the first differences are all 5

9 − 4 = 5, 14 − 9 = 5, 19 − 14 = 5: equal first differences.

:::


:::keep-together

### Neither exact

**A table gives $y = 3, 4, 7, 13$ for $x = 1, 2, 3, 4$. Which is the best description?**

☐ No pattern at all

☐ A perfect quadratic

☐ Curving upward: neither exact, but a curve fits better than a line

☐ A perfect line

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Curving upward: neither exact, but a curve fits better than a line

First differences 1, 3, 6 and second differences 2, 3: not exact, but clearly curving upward.

:::

**Also used in**

- **Road safety.** Speed limits near schools and crossings are set knowing that stopping distance grows faster than speed.
