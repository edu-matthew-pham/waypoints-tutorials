# Solving equations with graphs

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** A graph can solve an equation you have no method for yet: draw both sides and look where they meet. Calculators, spreadsheets and engineers do exactly this.


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students solve linear equations by drawing each side as a line and reading the x where the lines meet, then check by substitution (AC9M8A02).

**Students should be able to say:** I can solve an equation by drawing each side as a line and finding where they meet. I can explain why the solution is the x of the meeting point, not the y. I can check a graph's answer by substitution, and recognise parallel lines as no solution.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::


## Predict

$$2x + 3 = 11$$

On the line $y = 11$, every point has $y = 11$. On $y = 2x + 3$, $y$ is 11 at just one value of $x$.

:::keep-together

**The lines $y = 2x + 3$ and $y = 11$ are drawn. What is $x$ at the point where they meet?**

☐ $x = 3$

☐ $x = 8$

☐ $x = 11$

☐ $x = 4$

My prediction, and why:

{{lines-3}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**What happens:** x = 4. Where they meet, both lines have the same y, so 2x + 3 = 11. That happens when x = 4 (2 × 4 + 3 = 11). The point is (4, 11): 11 is its y, not its x.

:::

## Reading the solution


:::keep-together

### Solving with a graph

Here is the method, one line at a time.

1. $2x + 3 = 11$ — Solve this equation with a graph.
2. $y = 2x + 3, \quad y = 11$ — Draw each side as a line.
3. $(4, 11)$ — Find where they meet. There, both sides have the same value, 11.
4. $x = 4$ — The solution is the $x$ of the meeting point, not the $y$.
5. $2(4) + 3 = 11 \; \checkmark$ — Check by substitution in the first equation.

**On a graph, $y = 3x - 2$ meets $y = 7$ at $(3, 7)$. What is the solution of $3x - 2 = 7$?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 3

x = 3. Check: 3 × 3 − 2 = 7.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find the mistake

**Kai solves an equation with a graph. Click the line where his working goes wrong.**

```
 1  Solve x + 5 = 2x + 1 with a graph.
 2  Draw y = x + 5 and y = 2x + 1.
 3  The lines meet at (4, 9).
 4  So the solution is x = 9.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 4

Line 4: 9 is the y of the meeting point, the value of both sides. The solution is the x: x = 4. Check: 4 + 5 = 9 and 2 × 4 + 1 = 9.

:::


:::keep-together

### Which equation?

**The lines $y = x + 5$ and $y = 2x + 1$ meet at $(4, 9)$. Which equation has the solution $x = 4$?**

☐ $x + 5 = 2x + 1$

☐ $2x + 1 = 4$

☐ $x + 5 = 4$

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** $x + 5 = 2x + 1$

The meeting point is where the two sides are equal, so it solves x + 5 = 2x + 1. Check: 4 + 5 = 9 and 2 × 4 + 1 = 9.

:::

## Change the equation


**Subtraction.** Each task draws both sides of a new equation. Solve $3x - 4 = 8$.

**A negative answer.** Solve $2x + 3 = 1$.

**$x$ on both sides.** Solve $x + 5 = 3x + 1$. Now both sides are sloping lines.

**Between the lines.** Solve $2x + 1 = 6$.

**Never meeting.** $2x + 1 = 2x + 4$. Look at the two lines. What does the last lesson say about them?

## Read the graph

### From a graph

**A graph shows $y = 4x - 3$ meeting $y = 9$ at $(3, 9)$. Solve $4x - 3 = 9$.**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 3

x = 3. Check: 4 × 3 − 3 = 9.

### Both sides sloping

**$y = 2x - 1$ and $y = -x + 5$ meet at $(2, 3)$. What value of $x$ makes $2x - 1 = -x + 5$ true?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 2

x = 2. Check: 2 × 2 − 1 = 3 and −2 + 5 = 3.

:::


:::keep-together

### Which equation?

**The lines $y = 5$ and $y = x + 2$ meet. Which equation does the meeting point solve?**

☐ $x + 2 = 5$

☐ $x = 5$

☐ $x + 5 = 2$

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** $x + 2 = 5$

Set the two rules equal: x + 2 = 5. They meet at (3, 5), so x = 3.

:::
