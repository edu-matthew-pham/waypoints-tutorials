# Describing a relationship

Linear relations · Rules, tables and graphs

<!-- Speaker note: Students describe how one measurement changes as the other increases, section by section, and tell the story a graph shows without reading it as a picture (AC9M7A04, node 2). About 45 minutes (Predict and Run 8 min · Investigate 12 min · Modify 14 min · Practise and Make 11 min). -->

## Today

**We are learning to** describe how one measurement changes as the other increases, one section of the graph at a time: it increases, decreases or stays constant.

- I can describe a section of a graph as increasing, decreasing or constant.
- I can tell the story of a graph in order, with the measurements named.
- I can draw a graph that matches a story.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Run, Modify (bath) and Make boards yourself first. In Make, the points move up and down only, so students choose each value and the times stay fixed. The data are plausible examples, not real measurements. The new move is describing a section by comparing its start and end: as time increases, the other measurement increases, decreases or stays constant. The common slip is reading the graph as a picture of the journey, so that a line going down means a hill. Omar's working addresses it. A flat section is a constant measurement while time still passes: the cyclist is stopped, not going slowly. The cooling drink adds a decrease that slows down: the line is always going down but gets less steep. -->

---

## Why it matters

> A graph tells a story about how two things change together. Telling that story in words, section by section, is how scientists and journalists use graphs.

<!-- Speaker note: A graph does not just give values. It shows how one measurement **changes** as the other changes. Look at one section at a time, and ask: as the time goes on, does the other measurement **increase**, **decrease**, or stay **constant** (not change)? The data on these graphs are examples. -->

## A rest

Here is how to describe a graph, one section at a time.

$$\text{0 to 2 h: } 0 \to 60 \text{ km}$$

1) $\text{2 to 3 h: } 60 \to 60 \text{ km}$ — The distance does not change. It is constant.
2) $\text{3 to 5 h: } 60 \to 0 \text{ km}$ — The distance decreases.
3) $\text{increases} \to \text{constant} \to \text{decreases}$ — Put the sections in order. Say it: "As time increases, the distance from home increases, then stays constant, then decreases."
