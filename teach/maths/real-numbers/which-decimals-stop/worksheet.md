# Which decimals stop?

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Without dividing anything, you can tell that 7/40 stops and 7/30 goes on forever. The secret is in the denominator's prime factors.


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students predict whether a fraction's decimal terminates from the prime factors of its simplified denominator, and check on the long-division board (AC9M8N03, node 2).

**Students should be able to say:** I can predict whether a fraction terminates without dividing. I can explain the rule using 10 = 2 × 5. I can simplify a fraction before using the rule.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Why 2s and 5s


:::keep-together

### Why it works

**Why do only 2s and 5s in the denominator give a terminating decimal?**

☐ Because 2 + 5 = 7

☐ 2 and 5 are the smallest primes

☐ 10 = 2 × 5, so only those can multiply up to 10, 100, 1000…

☐ Because even numbers always terminate

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 10 = 2 × 5, so only those can multiply up to 10, 100, 1000…

A terminating decimal is a fraction over a power of 10, and 10 = 2 × 5.

:::


:::keep-together

## Which stop


:::keep-together

### Simplify first

**Does 7/56 terminate or recur?**

☐ Terminate: 7/56 = 1/8

☐ Recur: 56 has a 7

☐ Recur: 56 is too big

☐ You cannot tell

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Terminate: 7/56 = 1/8

7/56 = 1/8 = 0.125.

:::


:::keep-together

### Places

**1/16 terminates. How many decimal places does it have?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 4

16 = 2⁴, so 1/16 = 625/10 000 = 0.0625: four places.
