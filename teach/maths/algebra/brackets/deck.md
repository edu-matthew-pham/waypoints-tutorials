# When brackets are needed

Algebra Foundations · Expressions

<!-- Speaker note: Students decide where brackets belong when a sentence applies a later step to a whole result, and see that brackets change the value (AC9M7A02, node 2). About 45 minutes (Predict and Run 8 min · Investigate 15 min · Modify 12 min · Practise and Make 10 min). -->

---

## Today

**We are learning to** brackets keep a part together so it is worked out first. If a sentence says to do something to a whole result, such as "add 3, then double it", the expression needs brackets.

- I can say which step is done first, and what is then done to its whole result.
- I can put brackets around the part that is done first, and leave them out when they are not needed.
- I can show with a number that 2(n + 3) and 2n + 3 are different.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Run and Modify boards yourself first. They hide the formula: the board does what the words say, so students check their brackets against it. The new move is brackets as "do this part first". The common error is leaving them out (Jo's mistake: 6x + 2), so the multiplier only reaches the x. Always check with one number: add 2 to 1 and multiply by 6 is 18, but 6 × 1 + 2 is 8. Not every sentence needs brackets. The 'books' and 'multiply then add' questions are there on purpose: when a later step is add or subtract, no brackets are needed. Ask: "what is done to what?" -->

## Why it matters

> "Add 4, then double it" and "double it, then add 4" sound alike. They give different answers, and different expressions.

<!-- Speaker note: In the last lesson each sentence was written from left to right. Some sentences need more care: "add 3 to $n$, then double *the result*" doubles the whole of $n + 3$. **Brackets** group that part so it is worked out first. -->

## Add 3, then double

Here is how to decide where the brackets go.

$$\text{add 3 to } n, \text{ then double the result}$$

1) $n + 3$ — The first step: add 3 to $n$.
2) $\text{double the result}$ — "The result" is the whole of $n + 3$, not just $n$.
3) $2(n + 3)$ — Brackets keep $n + 3$ together, so it is worked out first and then doubled.
4) $n = 4: \; 2(4 + 3) = 2 \times 7 = 14$ — Check: add 3 to 4 to get 7, then double it.
5) $2n + 3$ — Compare: this doubles $n$ only and then adds 3. For $n = 4$ it gives 11, not 14.

**Which expression means "subtract 3 from $n$, then divide the result by 2"?**

- $\frac{n}{2} - 3$
- $\frac{n - 3}{2}$
- $n - \frac{3}{2}$

* Answer: $\frac{n - 3}{2}$

## Exit ticket

**Write an expression for: (a) add 6 to n, then multiply by 3; (b) multiply n by 3, then add 6. Which one gives 15 when n = 3?**

* (a) 3(n + 6); (b) 3n + 6. (b): 3 × 3 + 6 = 15; (a) gives 27.
