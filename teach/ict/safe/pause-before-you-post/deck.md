# Pause before you post

Staying safe and well · Kind and careful together

<!-- Speaker note: Students predict how a joking message lands, sort replies to a friend's video as kind or likely to hurt, find the mistake in judging a message by what was meant, and learn a three-question pause plus what a bystander can do. Typical width (Years 7 and 8): a screenshot in a big year-group chat. About 40 minutes (Engage and Explore 10 min · Explain 12 min · Elaborate 8 min · Evaluate 10 min). -->

## Today

- I can explain why a message can land harder than it was meant.
- I can use a pause before I send.
- I can say what I could do when a chat turns on someone.

## Why it matters

## Think, pair, share

**Ari says, 'I was only joking, so it's not my fault Sam left.' Is he right?**

- Does what you meant matter? Does what they felt matter more?
- What could Ari do now to fix it?

<!-- Speaker note: Both matter, but the reader only has the words. Fixing it (saying sorry, checking on Sam) is part of the habit. Keep it about Ari. Give 30 seconds alone, 2 minutes in pairs, then take answers from both sides. There is no single right answer: ask for a reason, and let students change their minds. -->

## Engage: predict

**Ruby types 'nice drawing 🙄' to Leo as a joke between friends. How might Leo read it?**

- As mean: he can't see her smile or hear her tone
- He won't notice the emoji
- As a joke: Ruby meant it as one

<!-- Speaker note: What happens: He might read it as mean. A message has no face and no voice. What Ruby meant does not travel with the words. Take two or three predictions before anyone runs it. -->

## Ari's mistake

**Ari adds to a group chat. Click the line where his thinking first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Someone posts "lol look at Sam's haircut" in the class group chat.
 2  Ari thinks: I'm only joking, so it's fine if I add one too.
 3  He posts "haha did his little brother cut it?"
 4  Two more people join in, and Sam leaves the group.
```

* Answer: line 2

<!-- Speaker note: Line 2: Ari decides it's fine because he means it as a joke. Sam only sees the words, from several people, in front of the whole group. -->

## On Sam's side

**Ari wants to show he is on Sam's side without making things worse in the group. What could he do?**

- Screenshot the chat and post it somewhere else
- Leave the group without saying anything
- Post 'stop being mean' and tag everyone
- Send Sam a private message to check he is OK

* Answer: Send Sam a private message to check he is OK

<!-- Speaker note: A private message to Sam supports him without adding to the group. Telling an adult is the next step if it keeps going. -->

## The pause

**Which pause question catches a message that's fine to say out loud, but not in text?**

- How will they read it?
- Would I say it to their face?
- Is it a good joke?

* Answer: How will they read it?

<!-- Speaker note: Without a voice or a face, even a friendly line can be read the wrong way. Ask on mini whiteboards or by show of hands before the reveal. -->

## Elaborate: when the chat is bigger

**In a year-group chat of 80 people, someone posts an embarrassing screenshot of a private message Mei sent. Most people are reacting. What is the best thing to do?**

- Don't react or share, check on Mei privately, and tell a trusted adult
- Forward it to Mei so she knows
- Reply with a joke to change the subject
- React too, so you don't stand out

* Answer: Don't react or share, check on Mei privately, and tell a trusted adult

<!-- Speaker note: Not adding to it, a private check-in and telling a trusted adult are the bystander's three moves. An adult can help get it taken down. What this step is for: Years 7 and 8: bigger chats, screenshots and pressure to join in; the same pause, plus what a bystander can do. -->

**Name one thing a bystander can do when a group chat turns on someone.**
