> **Key question:** In the class group chat, someone posts 'lol look at Sam's haircut'. Three people add laughing faces in ten seconds. What happens next?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students predict how a joking message lands, sort replies to a friend's video as kind or likely to hurt, find the mistake in judging a message by what was meant, and learn a three-question pause plus what a bystander can do. ## Explain: the pause

Before you send, **pause** and ask:

1. **How will they read it?** Read it as the other person, with no face and no voice.
2. **Would I say it to their face?** If not, don't type it.
3. **Is there a kinder version, or is no reply better?** Both are good choices.

When a chat turns on someone, you have choices too: don't add to it, check on them privately, and tell a trusted adult if it keeps going. Everyone sends something they regret sometimes. Saying sorry and fixing it is part of the habit.

:::keep-together

### On Sam's side

**Ari wants to show he is on Sam's side without making things worse in the group. What could he do?**

☐ Screenshot the chat and post it somewhere else

☐ Leave the group without saying anything

☐ Post 'stop being mean' and tag everyone

☐ Send Sam a private message to check he is OK

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Send Sam a private message to check he is OK

A private message to Sam supports him without adding to the group. Telling an adult is the next step if it keeps going.

☐ Don't react or share, check on Mei privately, and tell a trusted adult

:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Don't react or share, check on Mei privately, and tell a trusted adult

Not adding to it, a private check-in and telling a trusted adult are the bystander's three moves. An adult can help get it taken down.
