# Pictures out of place

Investigating and judging information · Numbers and pictures

<!-- Speaker note: Students see that real pictures mislead through a new caption, an old date or a crop, sort three made-up posts by how they mislead, and write a source line (who, where, when) for a picture. About 30 minutes (Engage and Explore 8 min · Explain 6 min · Elaborate and Practise 8 min · discussion and reflection 8 min). -->

---

## Today

- I can explain how a real picture can mislead through its caption, date or crop.
- I can ask where a picture first appeared, who took it and when.
- I can decide what to do before sharing a surprising picture.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: The one idea: a picture is a source; the caption, date and crop can mislead even when the picture is real. The pictures are simple drawings made by the board (no photos, no real people), and the posts are made up. Reverse image search exists on real devices (Google Lens, Bing Visual Search, TinEye), but this lesson does not use it: the drawings are not online. If you want to show it, use a safe picture of your own on the class screen. AI-made pictures are taught in AI in your life › Can you spot a fake? (provenance, not glitch-spotting). This lesson stays with real pictures out of place. Contested topics: real out-of-context pictures are often from disasters, protests or conflicts. Do not bring those in. If a student mentions one, ask only the three questions (where first, who, when) and park the event itself. Years 5 to 6: the floor and Practise. Years 7 to 8: the typical reasoning step as well. -->

## Why it matters

> A post shows a crowd and says hundreds queued for the new skate park. The picture is real. Can the post still be wrong?

<!-- Speaker note: A picture feels like proof: you can see it. But a picture only shows what was in front of the camera at one moment. The **caption** says what it is, and anyone can write a caption. Three ways a real picture can mislead: - **The caption**: words that say it shows something it doesn't. - **The date**: an old picture passed off as new. - **The crop**: cutting off the edges hides what was around it. A picture of four people, cropped close, can look like the front of a big crowd. A picture is a **source**, so ask the same questions as for a page: where did it **first** appear, who took it, and when? The pictures in this lesson are simple drawings made for practice, and the posts are made up. None shows a real person. -->

## Think, pair, share

**Is a cropped picture a lie? When is cropping fine?**

- Have you ever cropped a photo? Why?
- When does cutting something out change what the picture means?

<!-- Speaker note: Cropping is normal: it removes clutter. It misleads when what was cut would change the meaning (empty seats, the rest of a sign). The test is whether the caption is still true of the whole scene. Give 30 seconds alone, 2 minutes in pairs, then take answers from both sides. There is no single right answer: ask for a reason, and let students change their minds. -->

## Engage: predict

**A post shows a real picture of a crowd and says hundreds queued for the new skate park. Could the post be wrong?**

- No: the picture is real
- Only if the picture was edited
- Yes: the caption, the date or the crop could be wrong
- Only if the people are actors

<!-- Speaker note: What happens: Yes. The picture can be real and the caption wrong. On the board, the crowd picture is from a school fair in 2019, and it shows four people, not hundreds. Take two or three predictions before anyone runs it. -->

## The first question

**What is the best first question to ask about a surprising picture?**

- Where did it first appear, and when?
- Is it in colour?
- How many people have shared it?
- Is it a big picture?

* Answer: Where did it first appear, and when?

<!-- Speaker note: Where it first appeared and when tells you what it really shows. A reverse image search can help find that on a real device. -->

## Find Noor's mistake

**Noor shares a post that turns out to be wrong. Click the line where Noor first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Noor sees a post: a picture of a crowd with the caption Hundreds queue for the new skate park!
 2  She decides it is true because the picture looks real.
 3  She shares it with her class group.
 4  Later someone finds the picture first appeared in 2019, at a school fair.
```

* Answer: line 2

<!-- Speaker note: Line 2: Noor decided the post was true because the picture looked real. A real picture can carry a wrong caption, an old date or a crop. She needed to ask where it first appeared. -->

## Elaborate: the crop

**A wide photo shows one person waving a flag at an empty sports ground. A post crops it close to the person and the flag and writes Riverbend fans pack the ground! What did the crop hide?**

- The colour of the flag
- The person's face
- That the ground was empty
- Nothing important

* Answer: That the ground was empty

<!-- Speaker note: Cutting off the empty seats changed the story from one fan to a packed ground. Ask what was outside the frame. What this step is for: The crop changes the task: what is outside the frame can change what a picture means. -->
