# Numbers and charts that mislead

Investigating and judging information · Numbers and pictures

<!-- Speaker note: Students fix a made-up chart whose axis starts at 50 so a 6% rise looks like a tripling, spot a pie whose parts add to 140%, and ask how many were counted before trusting a percentage. ## Why it matters

> A made-up newsletter says 'Fruit sales soar!' and shows a column three times taller than the other. The numbers are true. Is the headline?

<!-- Speaker note: In [Choose a chart](../../data/choose-a-chart/) you picked the chart that answers a question. Here the chart has been chosen for you, by someone who wants you to see something. The numbers can all be true and the picture can still mislead. Three things to check: - **The axis.** Where does the vertical axis start? A column chart whose axis starts above zero cuts off the bottom of every column, so a small difference looks huge. - **The whole.** A pie chart shows parts of **one whole**. If the parts add to more than 100%, or are not parts of the same thing, it is the wrong chart. - **How many.** How many people or things were counted? "75% of people loved it" means little if only four were asked. The canteen, the newsletter and the numbers are made up. -->

## Think, pair, share

**If every number on a chart is true, can the chart still be dishonest?**

- Who chose where the axis starts?
- What would an honest headline for the fruit chart say?

<!-- Speaker note: A chart is made of choices: the axis, the type, the title. True numbers with a misleading picture can still give a false idea. Rewriting the headline ('Fruit sales up a little') makes the point. Give 30 seconds alone, 2 minutes in pairs, then take answers from both sides. There is no single right answer: ask for a reason, and let students change their minds. -->

## The pie

**A pie chart of 'What students do at lunch' has slices for sport 60%, reading 30% and talking 50%. What is wrong?**

- Pie charts can't show percentages
- The parts add to 140%, so they are not parts of one whole: students could pick more than one
- There should be more colours
- Nothing: the biggest slice is sport

* Answer: The parts add to 140%, so they are not parts of one whole: students could pick more than one

<!-- Speaker note: A pie shows parts of one whole, adding to 100%. When people can choose more than one thing, a column chart of each choice fits better. -->

## Find Kai's mistake

**Kai's poster says something the survey cannot show. Click the line where Kai first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Kai wants to know what students think of the new canteen menu.
 2  He asks four of his friends, and three say they love it.
 3  He writes on a poster: 75% of students in the school love the new menu!
 4  His teacher asks how many students he asked.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: Kai wrote that most students in the school love the new menu, from a survey of four friends. Four people can't speak for a whole school, and friends may think alike. 75% of 4 is 3 people. -->

## Make: Elaborate: in your spreadsheet app

In Excel: double-click the vertical axis, then under Axis Options set Bounds, Minimum. In Google Sheets: Chart editor, Customise, Vertical axis, Min. Other apps have the same setting under the axis options.

## Exit ticket

**Why should a column chart's vertical axis start at zero?**

* Columns compare amounts by height. Cutting off the bottom makes small differences look big.
