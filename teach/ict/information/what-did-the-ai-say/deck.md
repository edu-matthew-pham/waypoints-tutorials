# What did the AI say?

Investigating and judging information · AI answers

<!-- Speaker note: Students treat an AI answer as a source with no author: they sort scripted replies into ones to check and ones to judge for themselves, choose an independent check, find the mistake of asking the AI to check itself, and open a named source rather than trusting it. About 30 minutes (Engage and Explore 8 min · Explain 6 min · Elaborate and Practise 8 min · discussion and reflection 8 min). -->

## Today

- I can explain why an AI answer is a source to check, not a check.
- I can check an AI fact in a place that would know.
- I can explain why asking the same AI again is not checking.

## Why it matters

> You ask a chatbot when the Riverbend library opens on Saturday. It answers at once, sounding sure. Is that an answer, or a lead?

<!-- Speaker note: An AI chatbot writes an answer by predicting words that are likely to come next. Likely is not the same as true. So an AI answer is a **source**, and an unusual one: - It has **no author** you can look up, and often no link to where its facts came from. - It sounds **sure** whether it is right or wrong. - It may be **out of date** or mix up places with similar names. Use the same checks as for any page: say the claim, **leave the reply**, and see whether places that would know agree. The replies below are **scripted examples, not a real AI**. They were written for this lesson to show what AI answers can look like. How AI makes its answers is in [AI Foundations](../../../ai/foundations/). > **You asked:** When does the Riverbend library open on Saturday? > > **Scripted reply (not a real AI):** The Riverbend Library is open on Saturdays from 10 am to 4 pm. It's a great place to visit at the weekend! -->

## Think, pair, share

**If an AI is right most of the time, do you still need to check it?**

- What if it is about something that matters, like a time, a medicine or a quote in your report?
- How would you know which answers are the wrong ones?

<!-- Speaker note: Right most of the time still means sometimes wrong, and you can't tell which from the reply. Checking depends on what is at stake. Keep the discussion on when to check and how, not on whether AI is good or bad. Give 30 seconds alone, 2 minutes in pairs, then take answers from both sides. There is no single right answer: ask for a reason, and let students change their minds. -->

## Find Eli's mistake

**Eli's report has a made-up quote in it. Click the line where Eli first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Eli asks a chatbot for a famous quote about science for his report.
 2  It gives him a quote and the name of a famous scientist.
 3  He asks the same chatbot if the quote is real, and it says yes, so he counts that as checking it.
 4  He puts the quote in his report with the scientist's name.
 5  His teacher cannot find the quote anywhere.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: Eli asked the same AI whether the quote was real and took its 'yes' as a check. The AI cannot check itself. He needed to find where the scientist actually said it. -->

## Elaborate: a reply with a source

**A scripted reply says: According to riverbend-council.example, the pool opens on 1 December. What should you do?**

- Ask the AI for a second source
- Ignore it: AI can't name sources
- Trust it: it named a source
- Open the council's page and see if it really says that

* Answer: Open the council's page and see if it really says that

## Exit ticket

**Why isn't asking the same AI 'are you sure?' a way to check its answer?**

* It is the same source again. It may just agree, or change its answer without a reason. A check needs a place that would know.
