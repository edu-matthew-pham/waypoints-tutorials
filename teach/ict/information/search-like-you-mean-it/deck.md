# Search like you mean it

Investigating and judging information · Search

<!-- Speaker note: Students turn a question into a few strong search words (the thing, the place, the detail) and read a results page before clicking: adverts, dates, addresses and snippets. Every result is made up, with .example addresses. About 30 minutes (Engage and Explore 8 min · Explain 6 min · Elaborate and Practise 8 min · class search and reflection 8 min). -->

## Today

- I can turn a question into a few strong search words.
- I can find the advert, the date and the address on a results page before I click.
- I can say why the top result is not always the best one.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: The one idea: a search is a few strong words, and a results page is read before it is clicked. ## Why it matters

> You want to know when the Riverbend library opens on Saturday. You type the whole question and click the first result. What could go wrong?

<!-- Speaker note: A search box looks for pages that contain your words. A few **strong words** work better than a whole sentence: the thing you want, the place, and the detail that matters. Then read the **results page** before you click. Each result has a title, an address and a short **snippet** from the page. Some are marked **Ad**: someone paid to put them there. Riverbend and every page in these lessons are made up for practice. The addresses end in `.example`, so none of them is a real site. -->

## Think, pair, share

**If a search engine puts a result first, does that mean it is the best answer? Who decides the order?**

- What would make a page come first?
- Would you trust a result more if it was first? Why?

<!-- Speaker note: Order comes from many things: how well the words match, how popular a page is, and paid adverts. None of them is a check that the page is true. Keep the discussion on what order can and cannot tell you. Give 30 seconds alone, 2 minutes in pairs, then take answers from both sides. There is no single right answer: ask for a reason, and let students change their minds. -->

## Engage: predict

**You want to know when the Riverbend library opens on Saturday. Which search would you type?**

- when does the library open
- library
- can you please tell me what time the library in Riverbend opens on Saturday morning
- Riverbend library Saturday hours

<!-- Speaker note: What happens: Riverbend library Saturday hours. It has the thing (library), the place (Riverbend) and the detail (Saturday hours). 'When does the library open' has no place, so it could find any library. One word finds far too much. A long sentence works in many search engines, but the extra words can pull in pages that only match 'please' and 'morning'. Take two or three predictions before anyone runs it. -->

<!-- Speaker note: Teacher: take votes on the four searches. Ask what each extra or missing word does. -->

Write a long question on the board. Students take turns crossing out a word that a search engine does not need. Stop when three or four strong words are left.

## What happened?

* Riverbend library Saturday hours. It has the thing (library), the place (Riverbend) and the detail (Saturday hours). 'When does the library open' has no place, so it could find any library. One word finds far too much. A long sentence works in many search engines, but the extra words can pull in pages that only match 'please' and 'morning'.

## What Ad means

**What does the word Ad on a result tell you?**

- It is a news story
- Someone paid to put it there, so its place says nothing about how good it is
- The search engine checked it
- It is the best answer

* Answer: Someone paid to put it there, so its place says nothing about how good it is

<!-- Speaker note: An advert is paid for. It may be useful, but its place at the top is bought. -->

## Find Leo's mistake

**Leo turns up at the library and it is closed. Click the line where Leo first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Leo wants to know when the Riverbend library opens on Saturday.
 2  He types Riverbend library Saturday hours.
 3  He clicks the first result, a 2019 news story, without reading its date or address.
 4  It says the library opens on Saturday afternoons, so he goes at 2 pm.
 5  The library is closed.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: Leo clicked the first result without reading its date or address. Being first is not a check. The library's own page, further down, had this month's hours. -->

## Make: Elaborate: search for real

Do this only when your teacher leads it. Pick a safe, everyday question, such as the opening hours of a place near the school. Before anyone clicks, point to the adverts, the dates and the addresses.

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: A real search on a safe question, led by the teacher, shows the same parts on a real results page. For students who need more support: A card with three boxes: the thing, the place, the detail. Fill each with one word, then search. For early finishers: Write two searches for the same question that find different kinds of pages: one finds the place's own page, one finds news about it. What changed? The hard part, on purpose: Students click the first result out of habit. Ask them to point at the answer on the results page before anyone clicks. When students get stuck: A student types the whole question. Ask: Which three words matter most? Fix: Cross out the small words. Keep the thing, the place and the detail. / A student sorts the 2019 page as Click this first. Ask: What year is it from? Do opening hours change? Fix: Read the date on every result. / A student says adverts are always bad. Ask: Could an advert ever be useful? What does Ad tell you about its place? Fix: An advert can be useful. Its place is bought, so it is not a sign it answers the question. -->
