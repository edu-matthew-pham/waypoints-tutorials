# Challenge: answer it from three sources

Investigating and judging information · Challenge

<!-- Speaker note: The challenge: students answer a small question (do bean seeds sprout faster in warm soil?) from three made-up sources, rate how far they trust each, separate what the trusted sources agree on from a sales claim, credit all three and write a short answer that says no more than the sources show. About 40 minutes (Engage and Explore 8 min · Explain 8 min · Elaborate 5 min · Make 12 min · sharing and reflection 7 min). -->

---

## Today

- I can answer a question from more than one source and say how far I trust each.
- I can write an answer that says no more than my sources show.
- I can credit my sources with who, where and when.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: The challenge joins the course: strong sources, purpose, reading sideways, fact or advert, small samples, and credits (Documents and slides › Whose is it?). The three sources are made up. Source 1's numbers are typical gardening guidance for beans (they like warm soil and sprout slowly when it is cold), written loosely on purpose; the lesson is not a horticulture claim, and the model answers say the agriculture guide should be checked. Marking: the write-up is unmarked on the page (students compare with two model answers and self-check). For assessment, use the criteria in the step: an answer, a trust rating with a reason for each source, no claim beyond the trusted sources, and what could not be checked. Contested topics: if you swap in your own question, keep it small, checkable and not argued about in the news. The skill transfers; the heat of a contested topic swamps it. If your class is growing beans, students can add their own results as a fourth source (the typical step). -->

## Why it matters

> Your class is planting beans next week, and someone asks whether to put the trays somewhere warm. You have three sources and ten minutes. What do you tell them?

## Think, pair, share

**Is it better to give a clear answer or an honest 'probably'?**

- When would a clear answer be wrong to give?
- When would 'we don't know' be unhelpful?

<!-- Speaker note: A good answer is as clear as the evidence allows: 'probably yes, warmth helps' is both clear and honest. Overclaiming and refusing to answer are both failures. Give 30 seconds alone, 2 minutes in pairs, then take answers from both sides. There is no single right answer: ask for a reason, and let students change their minds. -->

## What you can claim

**Which claim can you make from the sources you trust?**

- Every bean seed sprouts in 24 hours on a heat mat
- Beans will not grow at all in cold soil
- Heat makes beans grow taller too
- Warm soil, around 20 °C, helps bean seeds sprout faster than cold soil

* Answer: Warm soil, around 20 °C, helps bean seeds sprout faster than cold soil

<!-- Speaker note: The garden club and the class test agree that warmth helps sprouting. No source you trust says more than that. -->

<!-- Speaker note: Teacher: ask where Tom's 24 hours came from. -->

## Find Tom's mistake

**Tom's answer says more than the sources show. Click the line where Tom first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Tom reads all three sources about warm soil and bean seeds.
 2  He sees that they all say warm soil helps.
 3  He writes that bean seeds sprout in 24 hours in warm soil, because that is the most exciting number.
 4  He does not say where the number came from.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: Tom used the shop's 24 hours as his answer, because it was the most exciting number. It comes from one source, which sells mats and gives no evidence. The sources he could check say about a week. -->

## How far does it go?

**The class test found 4 of 5 warm beans sprouted and 1 of 5 cold beans. What can it tell you on its own?**

- That warmth may help, from a small test
- That warmth always works
- Nothing at all

* Answer: That warmth may help, from a small test

<!-- Speaker note: It is a small honest test: a clue that agrees with the guide, not proof on its own. Ask on mini whiteboards or by show of hands before the reveal. -->

## Make: write your answer



<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Writing a short answer that says how far each source is trusted has students use every move in the course. For students who need more support: A frame: The answer is ... Source 1 says ... I trust it because ... Source 3 says ... but ... I did not use Source 2 because ... For early finishers: Find a fourth source you would like to have (who would know?) and write what it would need to show to change your answer. The hard part, on purpose: Students pick the most exciting number, or write an answer with no hedging. Ask: which source did that come from, and would you trust it? When students get stuck: A student trusts the shop because all three agree. Ask: Does the shop's 24 hours agree with the others? Fix: They agree warmth helps, not on the number. Use what the sources you trust say. / A student's answer has no hedging. Ask: How many beans did the class test? What could you not check? Fix: Add one sentence on what is still unknown. / A student leaves out the shop entirely. Ask: Why did you leave it out? Fix: That is fine. Saying why you did not use it shows your judgement. -->

## Check each sentence <span style="font-size:.6em;color:#64748b">(6 min)</span>

Swap answers with a partner. For each sentence, the partner writes which source it came from. Any sentence with no source is crossed out or hedged.

## Exit ticket

**Why should an answer say what you could not check?**

* So readers know how far to trust it, and where more checking is needed.
