# Two pages, one question

Investigating and judging information · Who made it?

<!-- Speaker note: Students compare two made-up pages that disagree about watering beans, sort clues into good reasons and not reasons, rate each page by the same questions and argue which to use. About 30 minutes (Engage and Explore 8 min · Explain 6 min · Elaborate and Practise 6 min · debate and reflection 10 min). -->

---

## Today

- I can compare two pages by who made them, when, and where their facts came from.
- I can say why looks, excitement and big numbers are not reasons to trust a page.
- I can say when I need a third source.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: The one idea: compare what you can find out about two pages, not how they look. Good reasons are checkable: author, date, source, experience, purpose. Both pages are made up (.example addresses), and the watering advice on Page B is general, not a rule for every soil and climate. The debate is the heart of the lesson: let students disagree, as long as each reason is something they could check. "Neither yet, I'd check a third source" is a strong answer, not a cop-out. Contested topics: if students bring two real pages on an argued topic, do not decide the topic. Ask which clues on each page are checkable, and where a third source would be. Keep the class on the method. Years 5 to 6: the floor (Engage to Practise). Years 7 to 8: the typical written argument as well. Teacher: Debate: two sides, each reason must be checkable. -->

## Why it matters

> Two pages answer the same question and disagree. One is bright and polished, the other is plain. Which do you believe, and why?

<!-- Speaker note: Here is the question: **should you water a bean seedling every day?** Two made-up pages answer it, and they disagree. > **Page A** · bean-boost.example > > **THE #1 SECRET TO GIANT BEANS!!!** > > Water your beans three times a day, every day. ## Think, pair, share

**A plain page with a named expert, or a polished page with no name: which would you trust? Is there a case where the polished one wins?**

- What would the polished page need to show to win?
- What would make you doubt the plain page?

<!-- Speaker note: Looks can come with care, and an expert can be out of date. The reasons that count are still checkable ones. Let students find a case where the polished page wins on reasons, not looks. Give 30 seconds alone, 2 minutes in pairs, then take answers from both sides. There is no single right answer: ask for a reason, and let students change their minds. -->

## Engage: predict

**Which page would you trust more on watering beans?**

- Both the same

## A reason or not?

**Which of these is a reason you could check?**

- It names the scientist who did the study
- It has a modern design
- It says it is the best site
- It has lots of photos

* Answer: It names the scientist who did the study

## Page A's number

**Page A says its drops make beans grow 300% faster. What is the best reason to doubt it?**

- 300 is too big a number
- It uses capital letters
- It sells the drops and gives no source for the number
- Nobody likes beans that big

* Answer: It sells the drops and gives no source for the number

## Find Zoe's mistake

**Zoe's beans start to rot. Click the line where Zoe first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Zoe reads Page A and Page B about watering beans.
 2  She picks Page A because it looks exciting and has big headings.
 3  She waters her beans three times a day.
 4  After a week the roots start to rot.
```

* Answer: line 2

<!-- Speaker note: Line 2: Zoe trusted Page A because it looked exciting and professional. Looks are not a reason. She never asked who made it, when or where its facts came from. -->

## Rate them, then debate <span style="font-size:.6em;color:#64748b">(8 min)</span>

In pairs, rate each page from 1 to 5 on: who made it, how recent it is, where its facts came from, what it wants. Then two sides argue which page to use. Every reason must be something you could check.

<!-- Speaker note: Accept 'neither yet, check a third source'. Stop anyone who argues from looks and ask what they could check. -->

## Elaborate: a closer call

**Page C, from a plant nursery, says water every day. Page B, the garden club, says every one or two days. Both name an author and a date. What is the best next move?**

- Give up: nobody knows
- Average them: every one and a half days
- Pick the one with the nicer design
- Check a third source, such as the state agriculture guide Page B names

* Answer: Check a third source, such as the state agriculture guide Page B names

<!-- Speaker note: When two good pages disagree, a third source can settle it. Page B names one. Both pages may also be right for different soils or weather. What this step is for: Two pages that are both reasonable change the task: the answer is to weigh them, or to find a third source. -->

<!-- Speaker note: Teacher: ask why averaging two claims is not checking. -->

## what would you ask?

**A page about Riverbend's history has a smart design and lots of photos, but no author and no date. Which question would help you most?**

- How many pages does the site have?
- Is the font easy to read?
- Are the photos in colour?
- Who made this, and where did they get the facts?

* Answer: Who made this, and where did they get the facts?

## Make: Elaborate: argue it

## Exit ticket

**Name two good reasons to trust a page more, and one thing that is not a reason.**

* Good reasons: a named author, a date, a source you can check, makers who would know. Not a reason: how it looks, exclamation marks, 'thousands agree'.
