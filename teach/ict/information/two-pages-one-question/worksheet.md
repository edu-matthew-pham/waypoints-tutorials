# Two pages, one question

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Two pages answer the same question and disagree. One is bright and polished, the other is plain. Which do you believe, and why?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students compare two made-up pages that disagree about watering beans, sort clues into good reasons and not reasons, rate each page by the same questions and argue which to use.

**Students should be able to say:** I can compare two pages by who made them, when, and where their facts came from. I can say why looks, excitement and big numbers are not reasons to trust a page. I can say when I need a third source.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (Engage, Explore, Explain, Evaluate). Typical and Wider sections add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::


## Engage


:::keep-together

**Which page would you trust more on watering beans?**

☐ Neither, yet

☐ Both the same

☐ Page B

☐ Page A

My prediction, and why:

{{lines-3}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**What happens:** Most people trust Page B more, and there are reasons you can point to: a named author, a date, a source and people who grow beans. Page A names no one, gives no date and wants to sell drops. 'Neither, yet' is a careful answer too: you could check both against a third source.

:::

## Explain: rate each page


:::keep-together

### Page A's number

**Page A says its drops make beans grow 300% faster. What is the best reason to doubt it?**

☐ 300 is too big a number

☐ It uses capital letters

☐ It sells the drops and gives no source for the number

☐ Nobody likes beans that big

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** It sells the drops and gives no source for the number

The page wants to sell the drops, and it gives no source you can check for the number.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find Zoe's mistake

**Zoe's beans start to rot. Click the line where Zoe first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Zoe reads Page A and Page B about watering beans.
 2  She picks Page A because it looks exciting and has big headings.
 3  She waters her beans three times a day.
 4  After a week the roots start to rot.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 2

Line 2: Zoe trusted Page A because it looked exciting and professional. Looks are not a reason. She never asked who made it, when or where its facts came from.

:::

## Elaborate: a closer call


:::keep-together

**Page C, from a plant nursery, says water every day. Page B, the garden club, says every one or two days. Both name an author and a date. What is the best next move?**

☐ Give up: nobody knows

☐ Average them: every one and a half days

☐ Pick the one with the nicer design

☐ Check a third source, such as the state agriculture guide Page B names

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Check a third source, such as the state agriculture guide Page B names

When two good pages disagree, a third source can settle it. Page B names one. Both pages may also be right for different soils or weather.

:::

:::


## what would you ask?


:::keep-together

**A page about Riverbend's history has a smart design and lots of photos, but no author and no date. Which question would help you most?**

☐ How many pages does the site have?

☐ Is the font easy to read?

☐ Are the photos in colour?

☐ Who made this, and where did they get the facts?

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Who made this, and where did they get the facts?

Who made it and where the facts came from are what let you check it.

## Exit ticket

:::keep-together

**Name two good reasons to trust a page more, and one thing that is not a reason.**

{{lines-3}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Good reasons: a named author, a date, a source you can check, makers who would know. Not a reason: how it looks, exclamation marks, 'thousands agree'.
