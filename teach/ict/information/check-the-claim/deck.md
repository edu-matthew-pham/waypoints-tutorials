# Check the claim

Investigating and judging information · Check the claim

<!-- Speaker note: Students check a made-up claim by reading sideways: they leave the page, look at three other places, tell independent sources from copies, order the checking steps and decide when to stop. About 30 minutes (Engage and Explore 8 min · Explain 7 min · Elaborate and Practise 8 min · discussion and reflection 7 min). -->

---

## Today

- I can check a claim by leaving the page and seeing what others say.
- I can tell a source that found something out from one that only copied it.
- I can say when I have checked enough, and when I could not check it.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: The one idea: leave the page. Professional fact-checkers read sideways (opening new tabs about the source and the claim) rather than reading one page more closely. The claim, the blog and the three places are all made up (.example addresses). Contested topics: if students want to check a real argued claim, use the same steps but do not settle the topic in class. Ask who would know, whether the places copy each other, and what could not be checked. If the topic is upsetting or political, park it and practise on a small claim instead. Years 5 to 6: the floor and Practise. Years 7 to 8: add the wider science claim (?width=wider). The wider step's claim is invented and only asks how students would check it; do not tell them the answer is false or true. -->

## Why it matters

> A post says Riverbend Primary's netball team has never lost a game in ten years. How would you find out, without asking the person who posted it?

<!-- Speaker note: Reading a page more carefully only tells you what **that page** says. To check a claim, **leave the page**: open a new tab, search for the claim or for the page's maker, and see what other places say. Checkers call this **reading sideways**. Then ask: do the other places agree? Did they find it out for themselves, or did they copy the first page? Here is a made-up claim and three other places you could look. > **The claim** · riverbend-sport-fans.example (a fan blog, no author named) > > Riverbend Primary's netball team has **never lost a game in ten years**! > **Place 1** · riverbend-junior-league.example · Results, 2016 to 2025 > > Riverbend Primary: won 61, drew 4, lost 7. > **Place 2** · riverbend-gazette.example · 12 June 2023 > > Riverbend Primary's netball team lost its first game of the season to Hilltop, 14 to 12. > **Place 3** · netball-legends.example > > Amazing fact: Riverbend Primary has never lost a game in ten years! *(No source given. Posted a week after the fan blog.)* -->

## Think, pair, share

**If ten websites say the same thing, is it more likely to be true than if one does?**

- What if all ten copied the first?
- What would make two websites a real double check?

<!-- Speaker note: Ten copies are one source. Two places that each found it out are two. Bring it back to: where did each page get it from? **What would you do first to check the claim?**

- Ask the blog's comments section
- Search for the team's results somewhere else
- Trust it: the blog is about netball

## Put the steps in order

**Put the steps for checking a claim in order.**

```science
Search for the claim or for who made the page.
Find places that would know, such as the people who keep the records.
Leave the page and open a new tab.
Decide how far to trust it, and say what you could not check.
See if they agree without copying each other.
Say the claim in a few words.
```

## Find Raj's mistake

**Raj tells his class the claim is true. Click the line where Raj first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Raj reads that Riverbend Primary has never lost a netball game in ten years.
 2  He searches for the claim in a new tab.
 3  He finds netball-legends.example saying the same thing, and counts it as a second source.
 4  He stops there and tells his class the claim is true.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: Raj counted Place 3 as a second source, but it gave no source and appeared after the blog. A copy is not a check. Two places that found it out for themselves would be. -->

## Copy or check?

**Two pages say the same thing in the same words, and neither names a source. How many separate checks are they?**

- Possibly none: one may copy the other, and neither says where it came from
- Two
- Three

* Answer: Possibly none: one may copy the other, and neither says where it came from

<!-- Speaker note: Same words and no source suggest a copy. Neither found it out in a way you can see. Ask on mini whiteboards or by show of hands before the reveal. -->

## The pool

**A post says the Riverbend pool opens on 1 December. The council's page and the pool's own page both say 1 December. What now?**

- Trust it: two places that would know agree
- Ask the person who posted it
- Keep searching for ten more pages
- Don't trust it: it was a post

* Answer: Trust it: two places that would know agree

<!-- Speaker note: The council and the pool would know. When places like that agree, you can stop. -->

## Nothing anywhere

**You search for a claim and find nothing about it anywhere else. What does that tell you?**

- Trust it less: no one else says it, so you cannot check it
- It must be a secret, so it is true
- It is true: no one has said it is wrong
- It is false

* Answer: Trust it less: no one else says it, so you cannot check it

<!-- Speaker note: If nothing else mentions it, you cannot check it. Hold off, and say you could not check it. -->

**A post says a new skate park opens in Riverbend in May. Is each place a separate check or only a copy?**

Groups: A separate check · Only a copy

* Answer: The council's page: Skate park opens 3 May, with a building update → A separate check; A share of the same post, word for word → Only a copy; The local paper: we spoke to the builders, who say May → A separate check; Another post: 'heard the skate park opens in May!' → Only a copy

## Exit ticket

**Why is a page that repeats a claim not always a second source?**

* It may have copied the first page. A separate check found it out for itself.
