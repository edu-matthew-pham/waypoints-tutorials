# Choose a chart

> **Key question:** Anyone can click 'chart' and get a picture. A good scientist asks first: what do I want the reader to see?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students choose a chart type to answer a question (column to compare groups, line for change over time, scatter for two measurements, pie for parts of a whole) and give it a title and axis titles with units. They chart a cooling cup of water, animal lifespans, arm span against height and how a day is spent, and repeat it in the spreadsheet app the school uses.

**Students should be able to say:** I can choose the chart type that answers my question. I can say what each of four chart types shows. I can give a chart a title and axis titles with units.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: the question picks the chart


:::keep-together

### Four charts

1. $\text{Column chart}$ — Compares amounts in separate groups, such as the lifespan of each animal.
2. $\text{Line chart}$ — Shows change over time, joined up so you see the trend.
3. $\text{Scatter chart}$ — Each dot is one thing with two measurements, such as a person's arm span and height. It shows whether the two go together.
4. $\text{Pie chart}$ — Shows how one whole is shared, so the parts must add up to something.
5. $\text{Title and axis titles}$ — Say what is measured and in what units, so a reader needs nothing else.

Now: which chart compares groups?

**A class counted how many beans were in each of four size groups. Which chart compares the four counts best?**

☐ A line chart

☐ A pie chart of one number

☐ A column chart

☐ A scatter chart

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** A column chart

Separate groups with a number each are compared with a column chart: the height of a column is the size of the count.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find the mistake

**Sam's chart does not show the water cooling. Click the line where Sam first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Sam has the temperature of a cup of water every minute for five minutes.
 2  Sam makes a pie chart of the six temperatures.
 3  The slices are almost the same size.
 4  Nobody looking at the pie can tell the water was cooling.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 2

Line 2: a pie chart shows parts of one whole, and six temperatures are not parts of anything. The slices look nearly the same and the cooling disappears. A line chart was the right choice.

:::

**Answer:** What the columns measure, and in what units

A title says what the chart is about and the axis titles say what each axis measures, with units such as years or °C.

## Make: In your own spreadsheet app

Excel, Google Sheets and Numbers all make charts the same way: select the cells, choose Insert and then Chart, and the app offers a type. It often suggests one, and the suggestion is only a guess. Check it answers your question, then add a title and axis titles. Leave out 3D effects: they make the sizes harder to read.
