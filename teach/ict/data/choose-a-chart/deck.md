# Choose a chart

Working with data · Show the data

<!-- Speaker note: Students choose a chart type to answer a question (column to compare groups, line for change over time, scatter for two measurements, pie for parts of a whole) and give it a title and axis titles with units. They chart a cooling cup of water, animal lifespans, arm span against height and how a day is spent, and repeat it in the spreadsheet app the school uses. About 50 minutes (Predict and Try 10 min · Worked example 10 min · Vary 7 min · Practise 12 min · Make and own app 11 min). -->

## Today

- I can choose the chart type that answers my question.
- I can say what each of four chart types shows.
- I can give a chart a title and axis titles with units.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Try board yourself: build the line chart, then change the type to Pie and Column and ask what each hides. The one idea: the question decides the chart. Column: compare groups. Line: change over time. Scatter: do two measurements go together. Pie: parts of one whole. On the page, students type the ranges (Labels and Values) rather than drag over the cells, because the principle is the same in every app: the chart needs labels and values. For a scatter chart, the Labels box holds the numbers for the horizontal axis, as in most apps' 'X values'. Honest charts: column charts start at 0. A pie of unrelated numbers is meaningless. Titles and axis titles with units mean the chart stands alone. 3D effects and decoration are left out on purpose. The values in the animal and arm span tables are rough, for teaching. Use class data if you have it, and have students predict what the scatter will look like before they build it. Teacher: Try: ask what the chart tells them that the table does not, and which type hides it. Teacher: Worked example: ask what each of the four charts is for, in their own words. Teacher: Own app: walk round. Common slips: selecting the heading row as a value, and leaving the title as 'Chart 1'. -->

**A newspaper chart shows two columns, 51 and 52, but the vertical axis starts at 50, so the second looks twice as big. Is the chart lying?**

- What does the reader see first, the numbers or the heights?
- How would you redraw it so it is fair?

## Four charts

Now: which chart compares groups?

$$\text{Column chart}$$

1) $\text{Line chart}$ — Shows change over time, joined up so you see the trend.
2) $\text{Scatter chart}$ — Each dot is one thing with two measurements, such as a person's arm span and height. It shows whether the two go together.
3) $\text{Pie chart}$ — Shows how one whole is shared, so the parts must add up to something.
4) $\text{Title and axis titles}$ — Say what is measured and in what units, so a reader needs nothing else.

## Find the mistake

**Sam's chart does not show the water cooling. Click the line where Sam first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Sam has the temperature of a cup of water every minute for five minutes.
 2  Sam makes a pie chart of the six temperatures.
 3  The slices are almost the same size.
 4  Nobody looking at the pie can tell the water was cooling.
```

* Answer: line 2

<!-- Speaker note: Line 2: a pie chart shows parts of one whole, and six temperatures are not parts of anything. The slices look nearly the same and the cooling disappears. A line chart was the right choice. -->

## Make: In your own spreadsheet app

Excel, Google Sheets and Numbers all make charts the same way: select the cells, choose Insert and then Chart, and the app offers a type. It often suggests one, and the suggestion is only a guess. Check it answers your question, then add a title and axis titles. Leave out 3D effects: they make the sizes harder to read.

## Exit ticket

**A student measured how far a ball rolled after being released from five different heights. Which chart shows whether a higher release goes with a longer roll?**

* A scatter chart: release height along the bottom and distance rolled up the side. Each dot is one roll.
