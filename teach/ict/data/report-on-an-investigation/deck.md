# Report on an investigation

Working with data · Challenge: report on an investigation

<!-- Speaker note: A challenge that brings the course together. Students summarise a class investigation (beans grown in dark, shade and sun) with AVERAGE, MAX, MIN and COUNTIF written once and copied across, chart the group averages with a title and axis titles, and pick a conclusion that matches the evidence. They then repeat it in the spreadsheet app the school uses. About 55 minutes (Predict and Plan 10 min · Summarise 15 min · Chart 12 min · Conclude 8 min · Own app 10 min). -->

---

## Today

- I can summarise groups of measurements with formulas and functions.
- I can chart a comparison with a title and axis titles with units.
- I can write a conclusion that matches the data and no more.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Summarise board yourself: write the four formulas for column B and copy them across. This is the course's challenge and there is no single path. It uses copying (moving names), functions on ranges, counting with a rule and a chart. If a student is stuck, point them back to the lesson that taught the move: copy, SUM, AVERAGE, COUNTIF or chart. The data is made up for teaching but behaves as real data does: the groups overlap in spread and differ in average. Use your own class's investigation if you have one, and have students enter their own results in the last step. Three equal groups of five keeps the sums easy to check by hand. The conclusion step is deliberately a question with four statements, one of which claims too much, one of which is false and one of which says only what the data shows. Then students write their own in two sentences. You may want to collect a few to read aloud and ask what could be challenged in each. Teacher: Plan: ask why average and not total, with equal groups, and what a slip looks like. Teacher: Summarise: walk round. Common slips: a range that stops at row 5, a missing colon, or the cell names not moved when copied across. Teacher: Chart: ask what the reader should see in one glance, and what the axes need to say. Teacher: Conclude: collect two or three sentences and ask what the data cannot say. -->

## Why it matters

> A class grew bean plants in three places and measured every one. Now they have fifteen numbers and a question: does light matter? Turn the numbers into an answer someone else can check.

<!-- Speaker note: A class asked: **does light change how tall bean plants grow?** They grew five beans in the dark, five in shade and five in the sun, and measured each plant's height on day 10 (in cm). Here are the results. | | A | B | C | D | |---|---|---|---|---| | **1** | Plant | Dark | Shade | Sun | | **2** | 1 | 4 | 9 | 12 | | **…** | … | … | … | … | | **6** | 5 | 4.5 | 9.5 | 14.5 | Your job is to report on it: summarise each group, chart the comparison, and say what the data shows. The last step also asks you to do the same in your own spreadsheet app. -->

## Think, pair, share

**The Dark plants averaged about 5 cm and the Sun plants about 13 cm. Does this prove light makes beans grow?**

- What else might differ between the dark and the sunny place?
- How many plants were measured, and would you be surprised if another class got a different result?

<!-- Speaker note: It supports the claim for these beans, in this setup. It is not proof for all beans. Fair tests control other variables (water, temperature, soil) and repeat the measurement. Give 30 seconds alone, 2 minutes in pairs, then take answers from both sides. There is no single right answer: ask for a reason, and let students change their minds. -->

## Predict

**Before any sums: which group do you think grew tallest on average?**

- Sun
- Dark
- They are all about the same
- Shade

<!-- Speaker note: What happens: Sun, with an average of 13 cm, against 9.6 cm for Shade and 4.6 cm for Dark. The data agrees with the guess, but the sheet turns the guess into numbers anyone can check. Take two or three predictions before anyone runs it. -->

## Typical height

**You want to compare how tall a typical bean is in each group. Which function on each column of five heights do you use?**

- SUM
- MIN only
- COUNT
- AVERAGE

* Answer: AVERAGE

<!-- Speaker note: AVERAGE gives the typical height of each group, so the three groups can be compared fairly. -->

---

<span style="background:#dcfce7; color:#1a1a1a; border:1.5px solid #22c55e; padding:6px 18px; border-radius:8px; font-size:1em; font-weight:500;">✅ Check</span>

## Catching a slip

**A ruler slip makes one Sun plant read 145 cm instead of 14.5. Which summaries would show something is wrong?**

- Only the count of plants
- Only the shortest
- The tallest, and the average
- None of them

* Answer: The tallest, and the average

<!-- Speaker note: A slip far from the others shows in the tallest (MAX) and pulls the average. Checking the tallest and shortest is a quick check on the data. -->

---

<span style="background:#dcfce7; color:#1a1a1a; border:1.5px solid #22c55e; padding:6px 18px; border-radius:8px; font-size:1em; font-weight:500;">✅ Check</span>

## Fair comparison

**Three groups of five beans are compared. Which summary lets you compare the groups fairly?**

- The average of each group
- The tallest of all fifteen
- The number of plants
- The first plant in each group

* Answer: The average of each group

<!-- Speaker note: Each group's average is its typical height, so the three can be compared. Ask on mini whiteboards or by show of hands before the reveal. -->

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Choosing the chart that compares three groups and naming it so a reader can use it is the move from the previous lesson applied to a result. Hints, in order: You want to compare three groups, so choose Column. / Labels are the three group names in B1:D1. Values are the three averages in B8:D8. / Say what is measured and the unit on the vertical axis. For students who need more support: A card: write the formula for one column first, then copy it across. Row 8 AVERAGE, row 9 MAX, row 10 MIN, row 11 COUNTIF with the rule in quotes. For early finishers: Add a fourth condition, Dark with a lamp, with five plants of your own invention. Update every formula and the chart. How much work was it, and what made it easy? The hard part, on purpose: Writing a conclusion that stops where the data stops. Students want to say 'light makes beans grow' for all beans. Ask 'how many beans, and where?' and let them rewrite. When students get stuck: A student's average for a column is far too big or too small. Ask: Which cells does your range cover? Is it the five heights only? Fix: Use B2:B6 for the five plants, not the heading or the other rows. / A student copies across but the numbers are the same in every column. Ask: Click D8 and read the formula. Which column does it use? Fix: Copy the formula, do not type the same text. Copy and paste moves the column letters. / A student's chart shows only one column. Ask: Which range did you give for the labels and for the values? How many cells does each cover? Fix: Both ranges are three cells wide: B1:D1 and B8:D8. / A student's conclusion says light is the only thing that matters. Ask: What else could differ between the dark, shade and sun places? How would you check? Fix: Say what the averages show for these plants, and name one thing you would repeat or control. -->

Swap conclusions with a partner. Underline anything the numbers cannot support. Rewrite one sentence so it says only what the data shows.

## Exit ticket

**Name one thing in your report that would change automatically if one measurement were corrected, and one thing you would have to change yourself.**

* The averages, tallest, shortest and counts (and the chart) change by themselves. The conclusion sentence is written by hand, so you would re-read it.
