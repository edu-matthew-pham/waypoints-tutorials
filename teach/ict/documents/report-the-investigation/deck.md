# Report the investigation

Documents and slides · Challenge: report the investigation

<!-- Speaker note: A challenge that uses the whole course. Students turn a class bean investigation into a one-page report (real headings, a numbered method, a table with a header row, a look set through styles, room to read) and a three-slide talk (one idea per slide, a picture with alt text and a credit, readable size and contrast), then repeat it with their own class's results in the school's apps. About 60 minutes (Predict and Structure 12 min · Look 8 min · Slides 12 min · Show 10 min · Judge and own apps 18 min). -->

---

## Today

- I can make a report with real headings, a list and a table.
- I can make three slides that carry one idea each and can be read from the back.
- I can credit a picture and say what it shows.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do each board yourself in order. The boards chain: Structure gives the report its parts, Look sets the styles and layout on a report that already has them, Slides splits the story, Show adds the picture, the credit and the colours. The challenge reuses the bean results from Working with data (average 4.6, 9.6 and 13 cm), so a class that did that course will know the numbers. Use your own class's results if you have them: the last step asks for a report and three slides in the school's apps. The checks are rough counts (headings, steps, slides, words, contrast). They do not judge whether a report is good. Use the whole-class discussion for that. Teacher: Structure: ask which lines a reader would want to jump to. Teacher: Look: ask what changes if they add a heading afterwards. Teacher: Slides: ask what they would say aloud on each slide. Teacher: Show: ask a partner to listen to the alt text with eyes closed. Teacher: Own apps: walk round and look at headings panels and slide titles first. -->

## Why it matters

> The class has the bean results from the Working with data course, and a teacher who wants a one-page report and a three-slide talk by Friday. What do you do first?

<!-- Speaker note: A class asked: **does light change how tall bean plants grow?** They grew beans in the dark, in shade and in the sun, and measured them after four days. On average the beans grew **4.6 cm in the dark, 9.6 cm in the shade and 13 cm in the sun**. Your job is to report on it twice, for two audiences: a **report** for someone who will read it at a desk, and **three slides** for a talk. Use what the course taught: styles, lists and tables, room to read, one idea per slide, pictures that help, size and contrast, and credits. -->

## Think, pair, share

**The same investigation, two audiences: a reader at a desk and a listener in a hall. What changes between the report and the slides, and what stays the same?**

- Which words from the report make it onto the slides?
- What does the reader get that the listener does not?

<!-- Speaker note: The facts stay the same. The report has the detail and lets the reader choose where to go. The slides carry one idea at a time, and the speaker carries the rest. Give 30 seconds alone, 2 minutes in pairs, then take answers from both sides. There is no single right answer: ask for a reason, and let students change their minds. -->

## Make: Judge: is it a good report?



<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Choosing what a report should say, and no more than the data shows, is the last step of the report. For students who need more support: A card for each part: Styles, not by hand. One idea per slide. Big, strong colours. Alt text and a credit. For early finishers: Add a Sources slide and a short text version of the report for someone who uses a screen reader. What does the text version leave out? The hard part, on purpose: Students change the look by hand because it feels faster. Ask them to check the headings panel and add a new heading. When students get stuck: A student makes a heading bold by hand. Ask: What does the headings panel say? Fix: Choose the Heading 1 style. / A student puts all the findings on one slide. Ask: How many ideas are there? Fix: One slide for each: what we did, what we found, what it means. / A student has a picture with no credit. Ask: Who made it, and what does the licence say? Fix: Credit it with who, where and the licence, or use their own. -->
