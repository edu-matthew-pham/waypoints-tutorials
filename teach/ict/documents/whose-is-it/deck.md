# Whose is it?

Documents and slides · Whose is it?

<!-- Speaker note: Students learn that pictures and words found online were made by someone, that a licence says what may be done with them, and that a credit gives who made it, where it was found and the licence. They learn what needs a credit (other people's pictures, copied words and ideas) and what does not (their own work and common knowledge), and write credits on a slide. About 45 minutes (Predict and Try 8 min · Worked example 10 min · Vary 7 min · Practise 8 min · Make and own app 12 min). -->

- I can say why something I find online still has an owner.
- I can write a credit with who, where and the licence.
- I can tell what needs a credit and what does not.

## Why it matters

> A student finds a good picture of a bean plant by searching the web, copies it onto her slide and says it is hers because she found it. Is it?

<!-- Speaker note: Everything made by a person belongs to them, unless they say otherwise. A **licence** is how they say it. Some licences let anyone use a picture if they give a credit. Others do not allow it at all. A good **credit** gives three things, in a line under the picture: 1. **Who** made it. 2. **Where** you found it. 3. **The licence**, what you are allowed to do. The pictures in these lessons use a pretend source card so you can practise. A real picture comes with its own details on the page where you find it. -->

## Think, pair, share

**You paint a picture and put it online. Someone uses it in an advert and says they found it. How do you feel, and what would you want them to do?**

- What would you want them to ask?
- What would a credit change?

<!-- Speaker note: Most students say they would want to be asked or at least credited. That is the point of licences and credits: the maker decides, and the user checks. Give 30 seconds alone, 2 minutes in pairs, then take answers from both sides. There is no single right answer: ask for a reason, and let students change their minds. -->

## Writing a credit

Now: what does a good credit give?

$$\text{Find the maker}$$

1) $\text{Check the licence}$ — It says what you may do. Some say you must give credit, some say no.
2) $\text{Write the credit}$ — Who, where and the licence, in a line under the picture.
3) $\text{If you cannot find them}$ — Do not use it. Draw your own or choose one that has the details.

<!-- Speaker note: Model this live on the board before revealing anything: write each line and say why, pausing to ask the class what comes next. Then click to reveal the lines and compare. Script. Start: write Find the maker and say "The page the picture is on usually says who made it. If it does not, be careful." Line 1: write Check the licence and say "It says what you may do. Some say you must give credit, some say no." Line 2: write Write the credit and say "Who, where and the licence, in a line under the picture." Line 3: write If you cannot find them and say "Do not use it. Draw your own or choose one that has the details." -->

## Writing a credit

**What three things does a good credit give?**

- Your name, the school and the date
- The file name, the size and the date
- The title, the colour and the owner
- Who made it, where you found it, and the licence

* Answer: Who made it, where you found it, and the licence

<!-- Speaker note: Who made it, where you found it and the licence. That is enough to check the original and see what you may do. -->

## Find the mistake

**Omar's teacher says he cannot use his picture. Click the line where Omar first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Omar needs a picture of a bean plant for his slide.
 2  Omar searches the web and clicks the first picture.
 3  He puts it on his slide without finding out who made it or checking the licence, because it was on the internet.
 4  His teacher says he cannot use it.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: Omar used the picture without finding out who made it or checking the licence. Being on the internet does not make a picture free. Someone made it, and the licence says what you may do. -->
