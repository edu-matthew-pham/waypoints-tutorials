# What do you believe about learning?

How you learn best · What do you believe about learning?

<!-- Speaker note: Students take a position on six beliefs about learning (no right answers), match made-up learners' beliefs to what those beliefs make them do, find Nib's slip (slower than a friend, so bad at maths), sort statements by what research usually finds, and write the learning coach's reply to an unhelpful belief. The workshop names self-regulated learning as being your own learning coach. Students finish by privately looking back at their own answers. ## Today

- I can explain how a belief about learning can change what I do.
- I can name a common belief about learning that is often wrong, and say why.
- I can say what being my own learning coach means.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: **Why beliefs first.** Students already have beliefs about learning, so the course starts by drawing them out before naming anything (elicit, then teach). Beliefs about learning are one of the lenses Barr uses to look at self-regulated learning; the specific beliefs here come from research on epistemic beliefs (Schommer: quick learning, fixed ability) and on illusions of competence and desirable difficulties (Bjork). **Kept honest on mindset.** The lesson doesn't promise that believing you can grow is enough. Large reviews find growth mindset interventions have small average effects, larger for some groups (Sisk and colleagues 2018; Yeager and colleagues 2019). The claim here is narrower and better supported: beliefs steer behaviour (giving up, rereading, guessing), and practice with good strategies usually helps. Keep "usually" and "often". **The definition.** Self-regulated learning means understanding and adjusting your own thinking, motivation, emotions and behaviour in learning (Barr). Students meet it as "being your own learning coach", and workshop 2 builds the coach in full. **No right answers in Think first.** Accept every reasoned view. The Check sort uses clearer statements than the opening six on purpose. **Personal reflection.** Apply asks students to look back at their own answers privately. They share only what they're comfortable with, and the class discussion stays on the made-up learners. Nothing is collected. Teacher: Think first: run it as a line across the room (agree at one end, disagree at the other) for two or three statements. Ask for reasons from both ends. Teacher: The idea: after the match, ask what Zara, Omar, Lucy and Theo could each believe instead, and what they'd do then. Teacher: The idea: read the definition aloud and write 'be your own learning coach' on the board for the rest of the course. Teacher: Apply is private. Don't ask which statements students agreed with. -->

## Think first

For each statement, decide: **agree**, **disagree** or **not sure**. Have a reason ready for at least one of them.

1. If you're smart, learning should feel easy.
2. If you have to work hard at something, you're probably not good at it.
3. If something looks familiar, you know it.
4. Some people are just maths people, and some aren't.
5. If you get it quickly, you've learned it.
6. Mistakes mean you're doing it wrong.

You'll come back to these at the end, privately.

## Beliefs steer what you do

**Each made-up learner has a belief about learning. Match it to what it might make them do.**

| | |
|---|---|
| `Zara believes smart people find it easy.` | A. He rereads his notes instead of testing himself. |
| `Omar believes that if it looks familiar, he knows it.` | B. He hides his working so nobody sees his mistakes. |
| `Lucy believes she's 'not a maths person'.` | C. When a question gets hard, she decides she's not smart and stops. |
| `Theo believes mistakes mean you're doing it wrong.` | D. She guesses instead of checking, because what's the point? |

* Answer: Zara believes smart people find it easy. → C. When a question gets hard, she decides she's not smart and stops.; Omar believes that if it looks familiar, he knows it. → A. ## Nib's slip

**Nib is doing a maths question. Click the line where Nib's thinking first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Nib starts a maths question about sharing pizzas fairly.
 2  Nib's friend finishes it in two minutes. Nib has been going for ten.
 3  Nib thinks: I'm much slower, so I must be bad at maths.
 4  Nib stops working on it and copies the friend's answer.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: taking longer than a friend doesn't mean Nib is bad at maths. It means this question takes Nib more steps right now. Working through it is how Nib gets faster. -->

## What would they do?

**Ari believes mistakes mean you're doing it wrong. What might Ari do in a test?**

- Leave questions blank rather than risk a wrong answer
- Check every answer twice
- Ask for help more often

* Answer: Leave questions blank rather than risk a wrong answer

## Usually true?

**Sort each statement: is it usually true, or often misleading?**

Groups: Usually true · Often misleading

You can get better at most things with practice and good strategies. · How fast you get something shows how smart you are. · If it feels hard, you're learning nothing. · Checking without your notes shows what you really know. · A mistake often shows you where to work next. · Some people just can't learn maths.

* Answer: You can get better at most things with practice and good strategies. → Usually true; How fast you get something shows how smart you are. → Often misleading; If it feels hard, you're learning nothing. → Often misleading; Checking without your notes shows what you really know. → Usually true; A mistake often shows you where to work next. → Usually true; Some people just can't learn maths. → Often misleading

## Ella's coach

**Ella has been on a science question for ten minutes. She thinks, "This is taking ages, so I must be bad at science." What would her learning coach say?**

- "Taking a while doesn't mean I'm bad at it. Which part is slowing me down?"
- "Everyone else is faster, so I should just copy them."
- "Yep, science isn't for me. I'll skip to the next one."
- "If I was any good at this, it would be easy by now."

* Answer: "Taking a while doesn't mean I'm bad at it. Which part is slowing me down?"

## Apply

Look back at the six statements from the start. Just for yourself:

- Did you agree with any of the ones that are often misleading? Has your answer changed?
- Think of a subject where learning feels slow or hard for you. Next time it does, what could your coach say instead of "I'm bad at this"?

You don't need to share any of this. If you want to, share one reason with your partner.
