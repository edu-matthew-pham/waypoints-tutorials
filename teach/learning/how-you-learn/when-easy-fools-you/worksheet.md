# When easy fools you

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Ana and Ben study the same page for 20 minutes. Ana reads it three times and highlights the key words. Ben reads it once, covers it, writes what he remembers, then checks. Who feels more ready? Who remembers more a week later?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students predict whether rereading or self-testing works better a week later, sort study habits by what research suggests rather than how they feel, find Nib's slip (familiar means learned) and learn to check instead of trusting the feeling of knowing. Typical width (Years 7 and 8): the same trap when an AI explains something.

## Explain: the feeling of knowing

When something looks familiar, your brain says "I know this". That feeling is real, but it can fool you. Recognising something on the page is easier than producing it without the page, and a test asks you to produce it.

So **don't trust the feeling: check it.** Cover the notes, try a question, explain it out loud. If it's hard, good: that's the learning happening, and now you know where the gaps are.

Any help that makes a task feel easy can do the same: a worked solution you read, a friend's answer, or an AI that explains it for you. They can be useful, as long as you then check what you can do **without** them. Does AI Help Us Learn? has a whole lesson on this: [The illusion of competence](../../../ai/does-ai-help-us-learn/illusion-of-competence/).

:::keep-together

### Nib's slip

**Nib revises for a quiz on the planets. Click the line where Nib's thinking first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Nib has a quiz on the order of the planets tomorrow.
 2  Nib reads the notes page three times.
 3  The page looks really familiar now, so Nib must know it.
 4  In the quiz, Nib can only remember four of the eight planets.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: the page looks familiar, so Nib decides it's learned. Familiar isn't the same as knowing. Covering the page and trying to name the planets would have shown the gap.

:::

### A better check

**What's the quickest way for Nib to find out what it really knows?**

☐ Rate how confident it feels

☐ Cover the notes and write the planets in order, then check

☐ Read the notes once more

☐ Ask a friend if they think Nib knows it

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Cover the notes and write the planets in order, then check

A check without the notes shows what Nib can do on its own, not what looks familiar.

## Elaborate: when the help is AI


:::keep-together

**Kim asks an AI chatbot to explain photosynthesis. She reads the explanation and feels she gets it. What would show whether she really does?**

☐ Asking the AI if she understands it

☐ Asking the AI to explain it again more simply

☐ Copying the explanation into her notes

☐ Closing the chat and explaining photosynthesis in her own words, then comparing

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Closing the chat and explaining photosynthesis in her own words, then comparing

Explaining it without the help is the check. If she gets stuck, she's found exactly what to go back to.
