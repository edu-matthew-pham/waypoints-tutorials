# When easy fools you

How you learn best · When easy fools you

## Today

- I can explain why studying that feels easy can fool me.
- I can say why testing myself usually works better than rereading.
- I can use a quick check without my notes to find out what I really know.

## Why it matters

> Ana reads her notes three times. Ben reads them once, covers them and writes what he remembers. Who feels more ready straight afterwards? Who remembers more a week later? They're not the same answer.

## Think first

**Ana reads her notes three times and highlights the key words. Ben reads them once, covers them, writes what he remembers, then checks. Which is most likely?**

- They feel the same and remember the same: they spent the same time
- Ben feels more ready, and remembers more
- Ana feels more ready, and remembers more
- Ana feels more ready, and Ben remembers more a week later

## Feels good, or works?

**For each way of studying: does it mostly feel like learning, or does it feel harder and usually stick?**

Groups: Feels like learning, often isn't much on its own · Feels harder, usually sticks

Reading your notes again · Covering your notes and writing what you remember · Highlighting the key words · Explaining it out loud, to someone or to yourself · A few practice questions each day, spread over a week · Watching the same video again

* Answer: Reading your notes again → Feels like learning, often isn't much on its own; Covering your notes and writing what you remember → Feels harder, usually sticks; Highlighting the key words → Feels like learning, often isn't much on its own; Explaining it out loud, to someone or to yourself → Feels harder, usually sticks; A few practice questions each day, spread over a week → Feels harder, usually sticks; Watching the same video again → Feels like learning, often isn't much on its own

## Nib's slip

**Nib revises for a quiz on the planets. Click the line where Nib's thinking first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Nib has a quiz on the order of the planets tomorrow.
 2  Nib reads the notes page three times.
 3  The page looks really familiar now, so Nib must know it.
 4  In the quiz, Nib can only remember four of the eight planets.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: the page looks familiar, so Nib decides it's learned. Familiar isn't the same as knowing. Covering the page and trying to name the planets would have shown the gap. -->

## Why the checks work

**Why does each one usually work better than rereading? Match them.**

| | |
|---|---|
| `Covering your notes and writing what you remember` | A. You have to link the ideas together, and you find the bits you can't explain |
| `A few questions each day over a week` | B. Pulling it out of your memory is practice for the test, and it shows the gaps |
| `Explaining it out loud` | C. Remembering again after you've started to forget makes it last longer |

* Answer: Covering your notes and writing what you remember → B. Pulling it out of your memory is practice for the test, and it shows the gaps; A few questions each day over a week → C. Remembering again after you've started to forget makes it last longer; Explaining it out loud → A. You have to link the ideas together, and you find the bits you can't explain

## Tom's plan

**Tom says, "I read my notes twice and they all made sense, so I'm ready for the test." What's the best reply?**

- Try a few questions without your notes first.
- Highlight the hard bits so they stand out.
- Read them once more, slowly, to make it stick.
- You're ready: if it made sense, you know it.

* Answer: Try a few questions without your notes first.

<!-- Speaker note: Making sense when you read it isn't the same as knowing it. A few questions without the notes are a quick, honest check. -->

## Lina's half

**Lina covered her notes and could only write half of what was on the page. What does that tell her?**

- Which half to go back to, and that the check is working

## Vary

Any help that makes a task feel easy can fool you the same way: a worked solution you read, a friend's answer, or an AI that explains it. Use it, then check what you can do **without** it. Does AI Help Us Learn? has a whole lesson on this: [The illusion of competence](../../../ai/does-ai-help-us-learn/illusion-of-competence/).

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Years 7 and 8: the same trap when an AI explains something, and the same fix. For students who need more support: One question on a card: 'Can I do it without looking?' For early finishers: Plan a week of revision for a test using two 'feels harder, usually sticks' habits. When would you check yourself? The hard part, on purpose: Students like rereading and highlighting and may feel told off. Say these are fine as a first step; the point is to add a check, not to ban anything. When students get stuck: A student says rereading works for them. Ask: How would you know? What would a check look like? Fix: Keep rereading if you like; add a quick test without the notes afterwards. / A student thinks hard means they're bad at it. Fix: Hard can be good: trying to remember is the learning happening. / A student thinks AI explanations are always bad for learning.
