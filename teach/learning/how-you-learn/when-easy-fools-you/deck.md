# When easy fools you

How you learn best · When easy fools you

<!-- Speaker note: Students predict whether rereading or self-testing works better a week later, sort study habits by what research suggests rather than how they feel, find Nib's slip (familiar means learned) and learn to check instead of trusting the feeling of knowing. ## Today

- I can explain why studying that feels easy can fool me.
- I can use a quick check without my notes to find out what I really know.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: **The research, kept honest.** Students judge their learning poorly when material feels fluent and familiar (Bjork's "illusions of competence"; Koriat and Bjork). In a large review of study techniques, Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rated practice testing and spreading practice out as high-utility and rereading and highlighting as low-utility, while noting the evidence comes mostly from particular tasks and ages. The lesson says "usually" and "often" for that reason, and says rereading and highlighting are fine as a first step. **"Desirable difficulties."** Testing yourself feels harder and is worth it (Bjork). That's the message: hard can be good. **Overlap with Does AI Help Us Learn?** That course's The illusion of competence and Offloading thinking lessons go further with AI and the research. ## Why it matters

> Ana and Ben study the same page for 20 minutes. Ana reads it three times and highlights the key words. Ben reads it once, covers it, writes what he remembers, then checks. Who feels more ready? Who remembers more a week later?

<!-- Speaker note: Everyone here is made up. Nobody needs to say how they study. -->

## Engage: predict

**Ana rereads and highlights. Ben covers the page, writes what he remembers and checks. A week later, who usually remembers more?**

- Ben: he tested himself
- About the same: they spent the same time
- Ana: she went over it three times

<!-- Speaker note: What happens: Usually Ben. Right after studying, Ana often feels more confident, because the page looks familiar. Ben's way feels harder, but trying to remember is what makes it stick, and it shows him what he's missed. Take two or three predictions before anyone runs it. -->

<!-- Speaker note: Teacher: vote, then ask who would feel more confident straight after, Ana or Ben. Often the class predicts Ana. -->

## Nib's slip

**Nib revises for a quiz on the planets. Click the line where Nib's thinking first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Nib has a quiz on the order of the planets tomorrow.
 2  Nib reads the notes page three times.
 3  The page looks really familiar now, so Nib must know it.
 4  In the quiz, Nib can only remember four of the eight planets.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: the page looks familiar, so Nib decides it's learned. Familiar isn't the same as knowing. Covering the page and trying to name the planets would have shown the gap. -->

<!-- Speaker note: Teacher: think aloud: 'This looks familiar… but can I say it without looking? Let me try.' -->

## A better check

**What's the quickest way for Nib to find out what it really knows?**

- Rate how confident it feels
- Cover the notes and write the planets in order, then check
- Read the notes once more
- Ask a friend if they think Nib knows it

* Answer: Cover the notes and write the planets in order, then check

<!-- Speaker note: A check without the notes shows what Nib can do on its own, not what looks familiar. -->

## Familiar or known?

**You recognise every word in your notes. What does that show?**

- That they're familiar, not that you could write them without looking
- That you know it all
- Nothing at all

* Answer: That they're familiar, not that you could write them without looking

<!-- Speaker note: Recognising is easier than producing. A check without the notes shows the difference. Ask on mini whiteboards or by show of hands before the reveal. -->

## Elaborate: when the help is AI

**Kim asks an AI chatbot to explain photosynthesis. She reads the explanation and feels she gets it. What would show whether she really does?**

- Asking the AI if she understands it
- Asking the AI to explain it again more simply
- Copying the explanation into her notes
- Closing the chat and explaining photosynthesis in her own words, then comparing

* Answer: Closing the chat and explaining photosynthesis in her own words, then comparing

<!-- Speaker note: Explaining it without the help is the check. If she gets stuck, she's found exactly what to go back to. What this step is for: Years 7 and 8: the same trap when an AI explains something, and the same fix. -->

## Exit ticket

**Why can rereading your notes make you feel ready when you're not?**

* The words look familiar, which feels like knowing, but you haven't checked whether you can do it without them.
