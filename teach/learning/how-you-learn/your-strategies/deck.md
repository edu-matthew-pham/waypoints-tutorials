# Your strategies

How you learn best · Your strategies

## Today

- I can say what kind of stuck I'm in and choose a strategy that fits.
- I can plan to use one strategy on a real task in another subject.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: **The transfer step is the point.** Knowing a strategy isn't the same as using it when it matters (Shyam Barr calls this the difference between having the umbrella and taking it out of the bag). The Elaborate step asks every student to plan one strategy on a real task from another subject. If you can, follow up in a later lesson with a quick "did it help?", without collecting what they chose. **The strategies** are general ones with good research behind them: understanding the task (Winne and Hadwin), planning, predicting and checking confidence (metacognitive monitoring and calibration), self-explanation, and looking back (Zimmerman's self-reflection phase). Each has a card on the Reference page. **Teaching one properly later.** For any strategy you want a class to really use, the teacher page (For Learning skills teachers) describes NEMO-T: Name, Explain, Model (a think-aloud), Opportunity to practise, Transfer. **Nothing personal is shared.** Students write their task and strategy for themselves. Teacher: Close by saying when you'll ask 'did it help?' (next lesson or next week). -->

## Think, pair, share

**Why do people keep using a strategy that isn't working, like reading the question again and again?**

- Is it easier than trying something new?
- How would you notice a strategy isn't working?

<!-- Speaker note: Familiar strategies feel safe. Noticing that one isn't working is itself the 'during' move. Give 30 seconds alone, 2 minutes in pairs, then take answers from both sides. There is no single right answer: ask for a reason, and let students change their minds. -->

## Engage: predict

**Lee has stared at the same homework question for ten minutes. What should Lee try next?**

- Read it again, more slowly
- Ask: what is it actually asking me to find?
- Look up the answer
- Skip it and never come back

<!-- Speaker note: What happens: It depends what's wrong. If Lee doesn't understand the question, asking what it's asking helps most. If Lee understands it but has no plan, a smaller example might help. Reading it a third time rarely does. The next step matches strategies to kinds of stuck. Take two or three predictions before anyone runs it. -->

## Nib's slip

**Nib has learned one strategy and loves it. Click the line where Nib's thinking first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Nib learns to test itself without notes, and it works brilliantly for the spelling quiz.
 2  Next, Nib gets a science question it doesn't understand at all.
 3  Nib decides to test itself without notes again, because that strategy always works.
 4  Nib still doesn't know what the question is asking.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: Nib uses the same strategy for a different kind of stuck. Testing yourself checks what you remember, but Nib doesn't understand the question. Asking 'what is it asking?' fits better. -->

## When to use them

**Sort the strategies by when you'd usually use them.**

Groups: Before · During · After

What is it asking? · Make a plan · Predict first · Check your confidence · Explain it to yourself · Look back

* Answer: What is it asking? → Before; Make a plan → Before; Predict first → During; Check your confidence → During; Explain it to yourself → During; Look back → After

## Which kind of stuck?

**You've got an answer, but it seems far too big. Which strategy fits?**

- What is it asking?
- Make a plan
