# Knowing it vs using it

How you learn best · Knowing it vs using it

## Today

- I can say what a strategy is, how to do it, when it fits and why it works.
- I can explain the difference between knowing a strategy and using it, and why a new one can feel worse at first.
- I can make an if-then plan to use one strategy on a real task.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: **NEMO-T inside the primer.** Barr's routine for teaching a strategy: Name, Explain, Model, Opportunity, Transfer (his podcast adds Time for reflection). Here, the Try step lets students hit the problem first; The idea names What is it asking? and explains it (what, how, when and why); the coach's think-aloud is the Model; Check is the Opportunity; Apply is the Transfer. The "did it help?" follow-up is the reflection. **Having it vs using it.** Barr's availability versus production deficiency: a student who doesn't have a strategy needs teaching; one who has it but doesn't use it needs prompts at the right moment, not reteaching. (He describes it as the difference between having the umbrella and taking it out of the bag.) Mo's question in Check is this point. It's also why the stuck link on every lesson page and the deck nudges ask "what might help you here?" instead of naming a strategy. **What, how, when and why.** Declarative, procedural and conditional knowledge (Paris, Lipson and Wixson; Schraw). Teaching a strategy without the how and why is what Barr calls strategy stripping; that's why each strategy card now carries why it works and what it costs. **The dip.** Using a new strategy before it pays off, so it gets dropped: utilisation deficiency (Miller, building on Flavell). Kit's question and Nib's slip are this point. **If-then plans.** Implementation intentions (Gollwitzer): "When ___, I'll ___" links a strategy to a moment, which usually makes it more likely to happen. **Personal reflection.** The own task stays in the student's browser and isn't collected. Plan the "did it help?" follow-up a week or two later: ask the class as a whole, hands up; nobody has to say what they chose. ## Think, pair, share

- How would you notice a strategy isn't working?
- Why might people drop a new one too soon?

## Nib's slip

**Nib is trying a new way to revise. Click the line where Nib's thinking first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Nib learns a new way to revise: cover the notes and write what you remember.
 2  Nib tries it once. It's slower than rereading, and Nib only remembers half.
 3  Nib thinks: that didn't work, so I'll go back to rereading.
 4  In the next test, Nib has the same gaps as before.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: one go isn't a fair test. A new strategy often feels slower and shows up gaps at first, and that's the dip. Rereading felt better, but it left the same gaps. -->

## Mo's strategy

**Mo can explain 'Predict first' perfectly, but he never uses it in maths. What would help Mo most?**

- Learning a different strategy instead

* Answer: A plan for the moment he'll use it

## Kit's two minutes

**Kit tried 'What is it asking?' on his English homework. It took two extra minutes and he didn't notice anything new. What should Kit do?**

- Drop it: it didn't help
- Do it more quickly so it costs less
- Use it only on tasks he finds easy
- Try it on a few more tasks before deciding

* Answer: Try it on a few more tasks before deciding

<!-- Speaker note: That's the dip. Some tasks won't need it, and on others it'll catch something. A few goes tells you more than one. -->

## Apply

Choose a task you have this week in another subject: a maths homework set, a science write-up, an English paragraph, a coding task, revising for a test. If you have nothing on, pick one from the list:

- **Maths:** five questions from your current topic. Use *Predict first*: estimate each answer before working it out.
- **Science:** the method of your last experiment. Use *What is it asking?*: write in one line what the experiment was meant to find out.
- **English:** a paragraph you wrote recently. Use *Look back*: reread it as a reader, and change one sentence.
- **Any subject:** tomorrow's revision. Use *Explain it to yourself*: explain the idea out loud without your notes.

This stays in your browser. Remember the dip: if it feels slow the first time, give it another go. Your teacher will ask "did it help?" in a week or two, and you only need to say as much as you want to.
