# Pulleys

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** A 400 N sack has to go up onto a stage. You can pull 150 N. How many pulleys do you need, and how much rope will you pull?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students find that one fixed pulley changes only the direction of a pull, add ropes until 150 N lifts a 400 N sack, work out the pull (load ÷ supporting ropes) and the rope pulled (height × ropes), see that work in equals work out, choose a system for a brief and write a brief that needs four ropes.

**Students should be able to say:** I can explain that one fixed pulley changes the direction of a pull, not its size. I can work out the pull and the rope pulled by counting the ropes that hold the load up. I can explain that a pulley system's work in equals its work out. I can choose the fewest pulleys that meet a brief.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: count the ropes


:::keep-together

### Four ropes, then six

1. $4 \text{ ropes hold the bottom block}$ — Count only the ropes between the blocks. The rope you pull goes over the top, so it does not hold the load up.
2. $400 \div 4 = 100 \text{ N}$ — The ropes share the load: each holds 100 N, and so does your hand.
3. $4 \times 1 = 4 \text{ m}$ — To lift the block 1 m, each of the 4 ropes must get 1 m shorter: you pull 4 m.
4. $100 \times 4 = 400 \quad 400 \times 1 = 400$ — Work = force × distance. Work in (100 N × 4 m) equals work out (400 N × 1 m): no free lunch.

Now 6 ropes hold up a 600 N load. Start with the pull.

**6 ropes hold up the bottom block, and the load is 600 N. What pull lifts it, in newtons?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 100

600 ÷ 6 = 100 N.

:::


:::keep-together

### Rope

**With those 6 ropes, how much rope do you pull to lift the load 2 m, in metres?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 12

6 × 2 = 12 m. Work in: 100 N × 12 m = 1200 J. Work out: 600 N × 2 m = 1200 J.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find the mistake

**Mia thinks she needs only 80 N. Click the line where Mia's working first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Mia's block and tackle: 4 ropes hold up the bottom block, and she pulls a fifth rope that goes over the top.
 2  That is 5 ropes, so it shares the 400 N five ways.
 3  Pull = 400 ÷ 5 = 80 N.
 4  To lift the sack 1 m she pulls 5 × 1 = 5 m of rope.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 2

Line 2: the rope she pulls goes over the top block, so it does not hold the bottom block up. Four ropes share the load: 400 ÷ 4 = 100 N, and she pulls 4 m.

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 40

### Trade-off

**Which uses less rope to lift a 600 N load 2 m: 3 ropes or 6 ropes?**

☐ They use the same rope

☐ 3 ropes: 6 m of rope instead of 12 m, but a 200 N pull instead of 100 N

☐ 6 ropes: more pulleys always means less work

☐ You cannot tell without the pulley sizes

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 3 ropes: 6 m of rope instead of 12 m, but a 200 N pull instead of 100 N

Rope = ropes × height. Fewer ropes: more pull, less rope.

:::


:::keep-together

### Same idea everywhere

**Each mechanism trades one thing for another. Match each to what it gives up to make your force bigger.**

A. Length of rope you pull

B. Distance your hand moves

C. Speed of the output

**Answer:** Gears, small driving big → C. Speed of the output; Lever, pivot near the load → B. Distance your hand moves; Block and tackle → A. Length of rope you pull

All three make your force bigger, and all three make you move farther or faster by the same factor: work in equals work out.
