## Today

**We are learning to** every real gear mesh and pulley wheel turns a little of the work into heat and sound. Students find a gear train lifts less than the ideal, that adding pulleys stops helping, work out overall efficiency by multiplying, and redesign a brief with friction in it.

- I can work out a machine's overall efficiency by multiplying the efficiency of each stage.
- I can explain where the 'lost' work goes, and why energy is still conserved.
- I can explain why adding more pulleys or gears eventually stops helping.
- I can redesign a machine so it still meets its brief with friction.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do Gear trains and Pulleys first; Science › Year 9 Efficiency and Conservation are the Science homes of the ideas (the page links them). ## Why it matters

> The ideal block and tackle said more pulleys always means less pull. Real riggers stop at about six. Why?

<!-- Speaker note: The earlier Mechanisms boards had no friction, so **work in = work out**: a smaller force over a longer distance did exactly the same work. Real machines rub. Each gear mesh and each pulley wheel turns a little of the work into **heat** and **sound**, and the energy is still all there (it is conserved), but less of it is **useful**. **Efficiency** = useful work out ÷ work in. Here each **gear mesh** passes on **95%** of the turning force, and each **pulley wheel** passes on **90%** of the pull. -->

## Four pulleys, step by step

$$0.9 \times 0.9 \times 0.9 \times 0.9 = 0.66$$

1) $\text{pull} = \frac{400}{4 \times 0.66} = 153 \text{ N}$ — The ideal pull (400 ÷ 4) divided by the efficiency.
2) $\text{work in} = 153 \times 4 = 610 \text{ J}$ — You still pull 4 m of rope to lift the sack 1 m.
3) $\text{work out} = 400 \times 1 = 400 \text{ J}$ — The useful work: the sack's weight times the height.
4) $610 - 400 = 210 \text{ J of heat and sound}$ — Efficiency = 400 ÷ 610 = 66%.

## Where it goes

**Where does the 'lost' work go?**

- It stays in the rope
- It goes back into the motor
- It is destroyed by friction
- It becomes heat and sound in the rubbing parts: the energy is still there, just not useful

* Answer: It becomes heat and sound in the rubbing parts: the energy is still there, just not useful

<!-- Speaker note: Energy is conserved. Friction transfers some of it to heat (and a little sound), which is no use for lifting. -->

## Better

**Which change makes a gear train more efficient?**

- More meshes
- Heavier gears
- A faster motor
- Fewer meshes (for example one bigger stage instead of two small ones), or better lubrication

* Answer: Fewer meshes (for example one bigger stage instead of two small ones), or better lubrication

<!-- Speaker note: Every mesh loses some energy, so fewer meshes and less rubbing (oil, bearings) mean less waste. That trades against gear size. -->
