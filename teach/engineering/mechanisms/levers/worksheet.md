# Levers

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** A 600 N rock, a 2 m bar, and a block to rest it on. You can push 200 N at most. Where does the block go?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students slide a pivot along a bar until a 200 N push lifts a 600 N rock, learn that turning effects (force × distance from the pivot) balance and that the hand moves farther by the same factor, meet class 2 and class 3 levers in a wheelbarrow and their own arm, and design a lever that meets two competing criteria.

**Students should be able to say:** I can work out the push a lever needs from force × distance on each side of the pivot. I can explain that a lever makes my push bigger by the same factor it makes my hand move farther. I can say whether a lever is class 1, 2 or 3 from where the pivot, the load and the push are. I can design a lever that meets more than one criterion.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: turning effects balance


:::keep-together

### Balance 600 N with the pivot at 0.5 m, then 0.4 m

1. $600 \times 0.5 = 300$ — The rock's turning effect: its force times its distance from the pivot.
2. $2 - 0.5 = 1.5 \text{ m}$ — Your push is at the other end: 1.5 m from the pivot.
3. $300 \div 1.5 = 200 \text{ N}$ — Your push must turn as much: push × 1.5 = 300.
4. $\text{advantage} = 600 \div 200 = 3$ — The lever makes your push 3 times as strong (its mechanical advantage).
5. $1.5 \div 0.5 = 3$ — Your hand is 3 times as far from the pivot as the rock…
6. $0.1 \times 3 = 0.3 \text{ m}$ — …so to lift the rock 0.1 m, your hand moves 0.3 m. Less force, more distance.

Now the pivot is 0.4 m from the rock. Start with the push.

**Now the pivot is 0.4 m from the 600 N rock, and you push at the other end. What push balances it, in newtons?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 150

600 × 0.4 = 240, and 240 ÷ 1.6 = 150 N.

:::


:::keep-together

### Distance

**With the pivot at 0.4 m, how far does your hand move to lift the rock 0.1 m, in metres?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 0.4

1.6 ÷ 0.4 = 4, so your hand moves 4 × 0.1 = 0.4 m. A quarter of the force, four times the distance.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find the mistake

**Leo thinks the push is 120 N. Click the line where Leo's working first goes wrong.**

```
 1  The pivot is 0.4 m from the 600 N rock. I push at the other end of the 2 m bar.
 2  Turning effect of the rock = 600 × 0.4 = 240
 3  My push is 2 m from the end of the bar, so push = 240 ÷ 2 = 120 N.
 4  120 N is less than 200 N, so I can lift it easily.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: distances are measured from the pivot, not the end of the bar. The push is 1.6 m from the pivot, so it is 240 ÷ 1.6 = 150 N.

## Levers


:::keep-together

### Seesaw

**A seesaw: a 300 N child sits 1.2 m from the pivot. Where must a 400 N child sit to balance, in metres from the pivot?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 0.9

300 × 1.2 = 360, and 360 ÷ 400 = 0.9 m.

:::


:::keep-together

### Crowbar

**A crowbar: the load is 0.1 m from the pivot and you push 0.8 m from it. How many times as strong is your push (the mechanical advantage)?**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** 8

0.8 ÷ 0.1 = 8: your push is 8 times as strong, and your hand moves 8 times as far.

:::


:::keep-together

:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Scissors → Class 1: pivot in the middle; Crowbar lifting a rock → Class 1: pivot in the middle; Wheelbarrow → Class 2: load in the middle; Nutcracker → Class 2: load in the middle; Bottle opener → Class 2: load in the middle; Tweezers → Class 3: push in the middle; Fishing rod → Class 3: push in the middle
