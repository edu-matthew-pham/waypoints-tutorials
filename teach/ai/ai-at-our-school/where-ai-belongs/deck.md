## Today

- I can say which existing school policy should own an AI situation.
- I can write a short AI statement that points to other policies instead of repeating them.
- I can explain why someone has to hold the map, and why most AI use can't be policed.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: **Core idea:** AI changes how existing rules apply, so its rules mostly belong inside the policies that already own each situation. A short AI statement holds the principles, the map, who holds it and when it's reviewed. Overreach test: if an existing policy owns the situation, the rule goes there and the statement points to it. Risk: without someone holding the map, scattered AI lines drift apart. Westbrook's six policies are made up; real schools name them differently (ICT acceptable use, assessment, child safety, privacy collection notice, learning support). Adjustments for students with disability are a legal duty as well as a fairness principle: an AI rule must not quietly remove them. For deepfakes and other harm, follow your school's wellbeing and child safety procedures; keep class discussion general. **Policing (background; product names out of student text):** OpenAI withdrew its AI text classifier on 20 July 2023 "due to its low rate of accuracy". Liang and colleagues (2023) found detectors flagged over half of 91 human-written TOEFL essays as AI-generated (average false positive rate 61%). Rules built on detection mostly catch the honest and wrongly flag second-language writers. **Rules from outside (checked 19 Oct 2026):** **IB Academic integrity policy, Appendix 6 (AI tools; added March 2023; read 19 Oct 2026):** the IB "will not ban the use of AI software" and aims to avoid the detector "arms race": detectors are not yet reliable enough to be "the sole evidence of academic misconduct"; better that "students can speak about their work with their teachers". AI-produced work, "even only in part", is not the student's own: copied or paraphrased AI text, images or graphs must be credited in the body (quotation marks, in the school's referencing style, with **the prompt and the date**) and in the bibliography; otherwise it's misconduct. Authentication rests on process: the teacher has seen the work develop over time, the student can explain it, referencing is clear, quality fits the student. **It is the school's decision**, under its own academic integrity policy, how to deal with work that isn't the student's own; the IB only requires that such work isn't submitted. Language acquisition is an exception (language and translation tools not permitted); software for access and inclusion requirements is considered under the IB's Access and inclusion policy. It also says to teach integrity through discussion, and that discussing ethical AI use is "a great classroom exercise". This fits the map: the IB sets what counts as the student's own work and how to cite AI; the school's integrity policy owns the consequences; inclusion owns access arrangements; language subjects need their own AI line. The ACT BSSS Teacher Guide on AI and Academic Integrity (2023) says "the unattributed or disallowed use of AI in assessment is a breach of academic integrity", that teachers decide acceptable AI use for each task, that students declare permitted use, and warns "Don't rely solely on AI plagiarism detectors". ## Think, pair, share

**Should a school have one AI policy, or AI rules inside its other policies?**

- Where would a teacher look first when a student cheats with AI?
- Who notices if the rules in different places start to disagree?

## Make: Elaborate: our school's map

Your teacher may give you a list of the school's policies (dated). For each one:

- Does it mention AI? Should it?
- Who owns it, and when is it reviewed?
- Does any rule appear in two places, saying different things?
- Which outside rules (an exam board, a programme) sit above it?
