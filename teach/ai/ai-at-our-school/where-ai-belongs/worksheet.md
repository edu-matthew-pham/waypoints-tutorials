# Where does AI belong?

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** AI changes how a school's existing rules apply: integrity, behaviour, privacy, inclusion, device use, staff conduct. Sort situations into the policy that should own each one, then write a short AI statement: the principles, a map of where each rule lives, and who holds the map.


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students see that AI changes how existing rules apply. They sort AI situations into the policy that should own each one (integrity, behaviour, privacy, inclusion, devices, staff conduct) and write a short AI statement (principles, a map of where each rule lives, who holds it, when it's reviewed) instead of one big AI rulebook.

**Students should be able to say:** I can say which existing school policy should own an AI situation. I can write a short AI statement that points to other policies instead of repeating them. I can explain why someone has to hold the map, and why most AI use can't be policed.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (Engage, Explore, Explain, Evaluate). Typical and Wider sections add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Explain: a statement and a map

- **AI changes how existing rules apply.** Cheating, harming others, privacy, support for students with disability, device use and staff conduct already have policies and owners.
- **The AI statement stays thin:** the principles, the **map** of where each AI rule lives, **who holds the map**, and **when it's reviewed**. Here's the test: if an existing policy owns the situation, the rule goes there, and the statement only points to it.
- **Someone must hold the map.** Without a map, AI lines scattered across six policies drift apart, and nobody notices when AI changes and they all need updating.
- **Rules from outside the school sit above it.** An exam board's rules on AI can't be loosened by the school.
- **Build on what you value, not on what AI can't do yet.** AI's limits keep changing.
- **Most AI use can't be policed.** Rules work mainly through task design, honesty about AI use, and understanding why the thinking matters. "Check" means checking the rules work, not checking up on each student.

:::keep-together

## Elaborate: rules from outside

Exam boards and programmes (for example, senior secondary boards and international programmes) publish their own rules on AI in assessed work. A school's map shows them as **rules it can't change**, and its own integrity policy lines up with them.

:::keep-together

**Westbrook's Year 10s complete a task marked by an outside exam body, which has its own rules on AI. Westbrook's statement allows more AI than those rules. Which applies?**

☐ Whichever the student prefers

☐ Westbrook's, because it's their school

☐ The exam body's rules: the school can't loosen them, so its map should point to them

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** The exam body's rules: the school can't loosen them, so its map should point to them

## Make: Elaborate: our school's map

Your teacher may give you a list of the school's policies (dated). For each one:

- Does it mention AI? Should it?
- Who owns it, and when is it reviewed?
- Does any rule appear in two places, saying different things?
- Which outside rules (an exam board, a programme) sit above it?

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?
