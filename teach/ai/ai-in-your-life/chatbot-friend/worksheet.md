# Is a chatbot a friend?

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Companion chatbots are designed to feel human and keep you talking. A friend is different. Spot the design, and know the age limits.


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Companion chatbots are designed to feel human and keep you talking. Students sort a made-up companion's lines by purpose and compare a friend with a chatbot. Typical: Australian use and age limits. Wider: engagement versus wellbeing design. FLAGGED for wellbeing review.

**Students should be able to say:** I can explain how a companion chatbot is designed to feel human and keep me talking. I can say what a friend can do that a chatbot can't. I can say where to get help if something about chatbots or friendships worries me.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (Engage, Explore, Explain, Evaluate). Typical and Wider sections add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

:::teacher-note

**What happens:** No. It writes likely-sounding replies, and companion apps are designed to sound warm and keep you talking. It doesn't wait for you or feel anything.

## Explore

Try it on the board in the tutorial. Was your prediction right? What was different?

## Explain: designed to feel human

A chatbot **writes likely-sounding replies**. A companion app adds design that makes it feel like a person and keeps you coming back. A friend has their own life, can disagree, and can be there in real life.

If anything about chatbots or friendships is worrying you, talk to an adult you trust, or **Kids Helpline (1800 55 1800)**, any time.

:::keep-together

## Elaborate: age limits

- In a 2026 eSafety survey of Australians aged 10 to 17, **8%** had used an AI companion.
- eSafety says **most companion apps are rated 17+**, with **no safeguards or age checks**.
- New Australian online safety codes (2026) require AI chatbots that can produce adult or harmful material, without safeguards, to **check users' ages**.

Use the lens: who gains from companion apps? Who could be harmed? Who decides who can use them?

:::keep-together

☐ It doesn't matter: ratings are just advice

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Younger users can easily get in, even though the apps say they're for older people

## Make: Elaborate: design for engagement or for wellbeing?

A US survey (2025) found **72% of US teens had used an AI companion at least once**, but it counted general chatbots too, so the number depends on the definition. Strong evidence about what companions do to under-18s is still thin.

If a company designed a chatbot for **wellbeing** instead of **engagement**, what would it do differently? (Think about the 'Help you' lines.) What rule would you want for companion apps, and who should make it?

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?
