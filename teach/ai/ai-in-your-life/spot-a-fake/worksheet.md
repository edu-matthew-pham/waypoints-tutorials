# Can you spot a fake?

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** People sort AI-made pictures, video and voices from real ones at about chance, and feel surer than they are. Tips for spotting glitches go out of date. So what can you do instead?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** People detect AI-made media at about chance (faces 48.2%; a meta-analysis of 56 studies, not significantly above chance; about 65% with training), feel surer than they are, and glitch tips go stale. So check provenance instead.

**Students should be able to say:** I can say what research found about how well people spot AI-made media. I can explain why glitch tips go out of date. I can explain why feeling sure isn't evidence that something is real.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (Engage, Explore, Explain, Evaluate). Typical and Wider sections add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Explain: why spotting doesn't work

**Glitch tips go stale.** When people find a flaw (weird hands, no blinking), the makers fix it. A researcher who found the blinking flaw in 2018 wrote a year later that the latest deepfakes had already adapted.

So looking harder isn't the answer. **Next lesson:** check **where it came from** instead.

:::keep-together

### The blinking tip

## Make: Elaborate: fakes and bias

A 2023 study found that **AI-made White faces were judged to be human more often than real White faces** (the researchers called it "AI hyperrealism"). The effect was found for **White faces only**.

Why might AI make some groups' faces more convincing than others? (Think back to AI Foundations: what's in the examples it learned from?) Who could be harmed by that?
