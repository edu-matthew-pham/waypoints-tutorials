# Will AI take jobs?

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** AI changes the tasks inside a job before it removes whole jobs. "Exposed to AI" is not the same as "lost to AI". The evidence so far, and what's still argued about.


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Jobs as bundles of tasks: exposed is not lost, augment versus automate, no widespread displacement yet in Australia (JSA 2025). Typical: call-centre novices gained most. Wider: the contested junior-jobs evidence.

**Students should be able to say:** I can split a job into tasks and say which AI could do, help with, or not do. I can explain the difference between a job being exposed to AI and being lost to AI. I can explain augment and automate.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (Engage, Explore, Explain, Evaluate). Typical and Wider sections add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Explain: exposed is not lost

- **Augment:** AI **helps** people do their job. Some tasks change.
- **Automate:** AI **does** the task, so fewer people may be needed for it.

So far, studies find **far more augmenting than automating**. In Australia, Jobs and Skills Australia (2025) found **no current evidence of widespread displacement**. Jobs are **changing**, task by task.

:::keep-together

## Elaborate: who gains most?

A study of a real call centre (published 2025) found that an AI helper raised productivity **by 14% on average**, and **most for new workers**. Use the lens: who gains? Who might lose if a company then hired fewer people? Who decides?

:::keep-together

**In a call centre, an AI helper raised workers' productivity by 14% on average, and by much more for new workers. Who gained most, and why might that be?**

☐ Nobody: productivity didn't change

☐ New workers: the AI shared tips that experienced workers already knew

☐ Experienced workers: they knew how to use it

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** New workers: the AI shared tips that experienced workers already knew

## Make: Elaborate: the junior-jobs question

- **Stanford researchers** (revised 2026): in the US, workers **aged 22 to 25** in the jobs most exposed to AI were **about 19% below** where their employment would be expected. The authors say this **describes** a pattern; it doesn't prove AI caused it.
- **Yale Budget Lab** (2025): the US labour market **has not experienced a discernible disruption** since ChatGPT arrived.

Can both be true? (One looks at a small group, the other at the whole economy.) Why might new workers both **gain most** from AI and be **hired less**? What would you want to measure next?

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?
