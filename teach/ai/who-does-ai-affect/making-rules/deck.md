# Making rules

Who Does AI Affect? · What happens next?

## Today

**We are learning to** a rule about AI balances the people it affects, and a rule existing isn't the same as it working. Write a class rule with the lens, then compare it with real ones.

- I can write a rule about AI and name who gains, who loses and who decides.
- I can explain why a rule needs a way to check that it works.
- I can explain why some AI uses get stricter rules than others.

## Checking

**Why should a rule about AI include a way to check it's working?**

- Checking is the AI's job
- Rules always work once they're written
- A rule can exist and still not work, so someone has to check and change it

* Answer: A rule can exist and still not work, so someone has to check and change it

## Elaborate: Australia's rules

**Australia's framework for generative AI in schools was kept after a review, and is now reviewed every year. Why review a rule every year?**

- So nobody has to follow it
- Rules must be rewritten every year by law
- AI and how people use it change fast, so a rule can go out of date

* Answer: AI and how people use it change fast, so a rule can go out of date

## Make: Elaborate: rules for high-risk AI

The European Union's **AI Act** sorts AI uses by risk. AI used to **filter job applications and evaluate candidates** is **high-risk**, so it has extra duties, such as human oversight. Those duties were delayed and are set to apply from **December 2027**.

Why might hiring be high-risk, but a chatbot that suggests recipes not? (Think back to lessons 3 and 4.) Which uses from this course would you call high-risk? Who should decide: governments, companies, or the people affected? Why might rules be delayed?

## Exit ticket

**Why isn't a rule about AI finished once it's written?**

* It may not work as expected, and AI changes fast, so someone has to check it and change it.
