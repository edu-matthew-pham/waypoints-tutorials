# How do we know when we need help?

Does AI Help Us Learn? · Who decides what learning looks like?

<!-- Speaker note: Students answer a seasons question, sort feedback by whether it gives a next step, and use the try–feedback–act–try again loop. About 40 minutes. Sources: Hattie and Timperley (2007), The power of feedback, https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487 (checked 18 October 2026); Black and Wiliam (1998), Assessment and classroom learning, https://doi.org/10.1080/0969595980050102 (checked 18 October 2026). -->

## Today

**We are learning to** answer a seasons question, sort feedback by what it lets you do next, and use feedback to choose your next step.

- I can tell feedback that gives me a next step from feedback that only marks me right or wrong.
- I can choose a next step from feedback and explain why it might help.
- I can explain why AI feedback is worth checking before I act on it.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: **Core idea:** Feedback helps when you use it for a next step, and check it. The minimum path is the student's own seasons answer and the feedback sort; choosing a next step from real feedback is typical. This lesson is about the learner using feedback (self-regulation), not about comparing tutoring tools again. The seasons misconception is an illustration; have a simple diagram of Earth's tilt ready. Words used for students: formative feedback (feedback you can use to improve) and self-regulation (choosing your own next step). Sources for the feedback claims: Hattie and Timperley (2007), "The power of feedback" (https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487): feedback helps most when it tells the learner where they are going, how they are going and what to do next, and much less when it is praise or a grade alone. Black and Wiliam (1998), "Assessment and classroom learning" (https://doi.org/10.1080/0969595980050102): formative assessment, where evidence is used to adjust teaching and learning, can raise achievement. The optional AI-tool step (typical width) is never needed to finish, and every check can be done without AI. Students use an AI tool only if the school allows it and you say so; check the tool's terms first, since many require users to be 13 or older. The step gives a no-AI version (a partner or a table). -->

## Think, pair, share

**When an explanation sounds clear, how do you know you understand it well enough to move on?**

- What could you do right now to test yourself?
- When is it time to ask a person instead?

## When AI disagrees

**An AI tells Nina her new explanation is wrong, but she thinks it's right. What should she do?**

- Check with a diagram, a textbook or her teacher before changing it
- Change it: the AI knows best
- Ignore it: feedback isn't useful

* Answer: Check with a diagram, a textbook or her teacher before changing it

## Make: Elaborate: choose the next step

A learner gets three messages: **"Incorrect"**, **"Try thinking about Earth's tilt"**, and **"Here is a diagram with an explanation."** Which is most helpful right now? Would you change your answer if the learner already knows about the tilt, but mixes it up with distance?

Now think about feedback you got recently on something you are learning. Write:

- what you think you understand,
- what evidence you have,
- what you will try next,
- what result would make you change your plan.

## Exit ticket

**How can feedback help you decide what to do next?**

* Use it for a next step, then try again to see if the step helped. Check feedback, including AI feedback, before acting on it.
