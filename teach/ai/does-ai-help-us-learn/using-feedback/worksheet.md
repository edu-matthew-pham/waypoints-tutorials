# How do we know when we need help?

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Answer a seasons question, sort feedback by what it lets you do next, and use feedback to choose your next step.


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students answer a seasons question, sort feedback by whether it gives a next step, and use the try–feedback–act–try again loop.

**Students should be able to say:** I can tell feedback that gives me a next step from feedback that only marks me right or wrong. I can choose a next step from feedback and explain why it might help. I can explain why AI feedback is worth checking before I act on it.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (Engage, Explore, Explain, Evaluate). Typical and Wider sections add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

## Explain: use feedback, then try again

Feedback is most useful in a loop:

**try → get feedback → do something with it → try again**

- **A try is evidence.** What you write shows what you understand now. One try doesn't show everything.
- **Choose a next step.** Practise more, look at a diagram, check a source, try a harder question, or ask a person. Picking a good next step for yourself is called **self-regulation**.
- **Check the feedback.** Feedback from AI is evidence too, and it can be wrong. Check it before you act on it.

Feedback that helps you improve, not just tells you a mark, is called **formative** feedback.

:::keep-together

**An AI tells Nina her new explanation is wrong, but she thinks it's right. What should she do?**

☐ Check with a diagram, a textbook or her teacher before changing it

☐ Change it: the AI knows best

☐ Ignore it: feedback isn't useful

:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Check with a diagram, a textbook or her teacher before changing it

## Make: Elaborate: choose the next step

A learner gets three messages: **"Incorrect"**, **"Try thinking about Earth's tilt"**, and **"Here is a diagram with an explanation."** Which is most helpful right now? Would you change your answer if the learner already knows about the tilt, but mixes it up with distance?

Now think about feedback you got recently on something you are learning. Write:

- what you think you understand,
- what evidence you have,
- what you will try next,
- what result would make you change your plan.

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?
