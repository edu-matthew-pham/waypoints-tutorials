# Challenge: repair the report

Holding writing together · Challenge: repair the report

<!-- Speaker note: The course challenge: students repair a report on a school rubbish audit that has the right facts but is hard to follow. They fix unclear pointing words, choose joining words, put the paragraphs in the order the overview promises, and add a missing explanation, then run the same four checks on their own writing. About 50 minutes (Predict 4 min · Part 1 10 min · Part 2 6 min · Part 3 10 min · Part 4 10 min · Own writing 10 min). -->

---

## Today

- I can find and fix the parts of a text that make it hard to follow.
- I can choose the right move for each problem.
- I can run the same checks on my own writing.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Try Part 1 yourself: trace We and they, then mark both Its as Not clear and choose the fix the last sentence supports. The challenge combines four of the course's moves: pointing words (warm-up), joining words (warm-up), overviews and order (AC9E7LA04 node 2), and substantiation (AC9E8LA04 node 2). Fixing an unclear pointing word by naming the thing is the repetition move from Say it again. Word chains and topic sentences are not tested here. The rubbish figures are made up for the lesson but realistic for a school week. The 10-cent container refund applies in every state and territory. Students who skipped the warm-up unit may need the reminder in Part 1: a pointing word is unclear when two things fit. Teacher: Part 1: ask a student who traced It to the canteen bins to read the sentence with the classroom bins swapped in. Does it still work? Teacher: Part 2: name the job before choosing the word. Teacher: Part 3: ask which words in the overview decide the order. Teacher: Part 4: read three explanations aloud. Which one would convince the principal? -->

## Why it matters

> A report has all the right facts, but readers keep getting lost. Can you fix it without changing a single fact?

<!-- Speaker note: Nib's class weighed the rubbish in the school's bins for a week. Their facts are right, but the report is hard to follow. You will repair it in four parts, with four moves from this course: pointing words, joining words, the overview's order, and explaining evidence. -->

## Think, pair, share

**Can a text with all the right facts still be bad writing?**

- What does a reader need besides the facts?
- Which of the four problems would annoy you most as a reader?

<!-- Speaker note: Yes. Facts need to be connected and ordered so the reader can follow them. That connecting is cohesion. Give 30 seconds alone, 2 minutes in pairs, then take answers from both sides. There is no single right answer: ask for a reason, and let students change their minds. -->

## Predict

**A reader says: 'I kept asking what it meant, some ideas didn't connect, the paragraphs jumped around, and some facts just sat there.' Which moves will fix that?**

- Longer sentences
- A better title
- More facts
- Clear pointing words, joining words, an order that follows the overview, and explanations

<!-- Speaker note: What happens: Clear pointing words fix 'what does it mean', joining words connect the ideas, following the overview fixes the jumping, and explanations stop facts just sitting there. That is the plan for this challenge. Take two or three predictions before anyone runs it. -->

## Make: repair your own



<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Running the same four checks on their own longer writing is the course's transfer step. For students who need more support: The four-check card: Pointing words clear? Joining words right job? Order follows the overview? Evidence explained? For early finishers: Write the paragraph for the bottle collection recommendation, with a claim, evidence and explanation. Then add it to the report in the right place. The hard part, on purpose: Part 1 is the hardest: students must decide which pointing words are clear before tracing, then choose the fix the facts support. Some will trace It to one kind of rubbish confidently. Ask whether the other could fit too, then ask which sentence settles it. When students get stuck: A student traces every pointing word, including the unclear ones. Ask: Could this point to anything else? Fix: If two things fit, it's not clear: name the thing instead, and let the other facts decide which. / A student reorders the report by guessing. Ask: What does the overview say comes first? Fix: Follow the overview's order. / A student's explanation repeats the 23 kilograms. Ask: Why does that number matter for the compost bin? Fix: Say what it shows: food is the biggest part, so composting cuts the most. -->

## Which check?

**A paragraph gives a figure and then moves on without saying what it shows. Which check catches it?**

- Is the evidence explained?
- Are the pointing words clear?
- Do the joining words do the right job?
- Does the order follow the overview?

* Answer: Is the evidence explained?

<!-- Speaker note: A figure with no explanation is evidence that hasn't been explained. Ask on mini whiteboards or by show of hands before the reveal. -->

## Exit ticket

**Name the four checks you used to repair the report.**

* Pointing words are clear; joining words do the right job; paragraphs follow the overview's order; evidence is explained.
