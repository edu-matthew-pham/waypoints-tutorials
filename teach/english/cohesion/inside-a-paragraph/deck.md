# Inside a paragraph

Holding writing together · Inside a paragraph

<!-- Speaker note: Students learn the parts inside an argued paragraph: a claim, evidence (an example or a quotation) and an explanation of how the evidence supports the claim. They label the parts in a model paragraph about a short story written for the lesson, add a missing explanation, label a history paragraph, and write and label their own. About 50 minutes (Story and Predict 6 min · Try 5 min · Worked example 10 min · Vary 7 min · Practise 7 min · Make and own writing 15 min). -->

## Today

- I can find the claim, the evidence and the explanation in a paragraph.
- I can explain how a piece of evidence supports a claim.
- I can write a paragraph with a claim, evidence and an explanation.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Read the short story at the top of the lesson; it is written for this lesson, so it can be quoted freely. ## Why it matters

> Someone says 'Our team is the best' and shows you a photo of a trophy. Are you convinced? What else would you want to know?

<!-- Speaker note: A paragraph that argues a point has parts inside it, each with a job: - A **claim** says what the paragraph argues (often the topic sentence). - **Evidence** backs it up: an **example** told in your own words, or a **quotation** in the writer's exact words. - An **explanation** says *how* the evidence supports the claim. Here is a short story written for this lesson. The paragraphs below argue about it. > Mei always waited for the last bus at the back of the shelter, hood up, eyes on her shoes. She never looked at the driver. One wet night an old man stumbled on the step and his wallet slid into the gutter. Before anyone else moved, Mei ran into the rain without a second thought. She handed it back, dripping, and sat down at the front of the bus. -->

## Think, pair, share

**Does a quotation ever speak for itself?**

- Would every reader see the same thing in 'ran into the rain without a second thought'?
- What does the explanation add that the quotation can't?

<!-- Speaker note: Different readers see different things in the same words. The explanation tells the reader which thing you see, and why it matters to your claim. Give 30 seconds alone, 2 minutes in pairs, then take answers from both sides. There is no single right answer: ask for a reason, and let students change their minds. -->

## Predict

**'Mei is braver than she seems. She "ran into the rain without a second thought."' Is that paragraph finished?**

- No: it needs a title
- No: it doesn't say how running into the rain shows bravery
- Yes: it has a claim and a sentence
- Yes: the quotation proves it

<!-- Speaker note: What happens: No. It makes a claim and gives evidence, but it never says why running into the rain shows bravery. That explanation is the missing step. Take two or three predictions before anyone runs it. -->

<!-- Speaker note: Teacher: ask students who said yes to explain how the quotation shows bravery. Their answer is the missing sentence. -->

## Building the paragraph

Now: what is the explanation's job?

$$\text{Claim}$$

1) $\text{Evidence}$ — Give an example in your own words, or a short quotation in the writer's exact words.
2) $\text{Explain}$ — Say how the evidence shows the claim: This shows that... or Running into the rain suggests...
3) $\text{Check the link}$ — Read the claim and the explanation together. Do they connect without the evidence in between?

<!-- Speaker note: Model this live on the board before revealing anything: write each line and say why, pausing to ask the class what comes next. Then click to reveal the lines and compare. Script. Start: write Claim and say "Say what you are arguing: Mei is braver than she first seems." Line 1: write Evidence and say "Give an example in your own words, or a short quotation in the writer's exact words." Line 2: write Explain and say "Say how the evidence shows the claim: This shows that... or Running into the rain suggests..." Line 3: write Check the link and say "Read the claim and the explanation together. Do they connect without the evidence in between?" -->

## Building the paragraph

**What is the explanation's job in a paragraph?**

- To tell the reader the story again
- To add a new claim
- To say how the evidence supports the claim
- To repeat the quotation in other words

* Answer: To say how the evidence supports the claim

<!-- Speaker note: The explanation joins the evidence back to the claim: how does this show that? -->

**Here is Nib's working on a paragraph. Click the line where Nib first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Nib writes a claim: Mei is kind.
 2  Nib adds a quotation about the wallet: she "handed it back, dripping."
 3  Nib starts the next paragraph, because the quotation proves the claim.
 4  Nib's teacher writes in the margin: how does this show she is kind?
```

* Answer: line 3

## Explaining or evidence?

**Which sentence explains, rather than giving more evidence?**

- This shows that Mei stops hiding once someone needs her.
- She never looked at the driver.
- 'She handed it back, dripping.'
- She sat down at the front of the bus.

* Answer: This shows that Mei stops hiding once someone needs her.

<!-- Speaker note: 'This shows that...' joins the evidence back to the claim. The others are more evidence. -->

## Which part?

**'This suggests that Mei cares more about others than about herself.' Which part of a paragraph is this?**

- Explanation
- Claim
- Example
- Quotation

* Answer: Explanation

## Vary: add the missing step

The board checks that you added and labelled an explanation. Whether it explains well is for you and a partner to judge: read the claim and your explanation together.

[Open this step](https://tutorials.learningwaypoints.com/english/cohesion/inside-a-paragraph/?full=step-4#step-4)

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Adding the explanation to a paragraph that lacks one is the exact repair node 2 asks for. Hints, in order: Click the quotation, then press Add a sentence after. Type your explanation in the box. / Start with 'This shows that...' and say how sitting at the front shows Mei has changed. Then label your sentence as the explanation. For students who need more support: A paragraph frame: Claim: ___. For example, ___. The writer says '___'. This shows that ___ because ___. For early finishers: Write a second paragraph that argues the opposite claim (Mei is not brave, only impulsive), with its own evidence and explanation. The hard part, on purpose: Students commonly write a claim and a quotation and stop, treating the quotation as self-evident. The explanation is the hard part: it is where the student's own thinking shows. Give the stem 'This shows that... because...'. When students get stuck: A student's explanation retells the quotation. Ask: What does this show about the character? Why does it matter for your claim? Fix: Use the stem: This shows that... because... / A student uses a long quotation. Ask: Which few words do the work? Fix: Quote the shortest part that proves the point. / A student labels the claim as an example. Ask: Is this sentence arguing something, or showing something? Fix: The claim argues; the example shows. -->

## Exit ticket

**'Dogs make better pets than cats. My dog fetches the newspaper every morning.' What is missing, and write it.**

* The explanation: for example, 'This shows that dogs can be trained to help, which makes them more useful pets.'
