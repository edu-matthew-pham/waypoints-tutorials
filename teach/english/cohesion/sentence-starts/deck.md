# Where a sentence starts

Holding writing together · Warm-up: pointing and joining

<!-- Speaker note: Students learn that the start of a sentence tells the reader what it is about, and the rest adds something new. When each start picks up something known, a paragraph flows. Moving a time, reason or contrast to the start puts it in the spotlight. Students choose between versions of the same sentence, find a start that jumps, and write a paragraph of their own and read its starts to a partner. About 40 minutes (Predict and Try 8 min · Worked example 10 min · Vary 6 min · Practise 6 min · Make and own writing 10 min). -->

---

## Today

- I can say what the start of a sentence tells the reader.
- I can choose the version of a sentence that flows from the one before.
- I can read the starts of my own sentences to check that my paragraph flows.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Try board yourself: each gap offers the same sentence in two orders. The one idea: start with something the reader already knows, end with something new. This is the "theme" idea from AC9E5LA04 (sentence and paragraph starting points). You don't need the word theme with students; "the start" works. This is an optional warm-up from Year 5. Some of the "wrong" versions are passive (are run by, is used by). That isn't the lesson's point, but it's a good moment to notice that the passive can be useful when it puts the known thing first. Teacher: Try: before each choice, ask what the reader already knows at that point. Teacher: Worked example: read only the starts of Nib's paragraph aloud as a class. Where does the thread snap? Teacher: Vary: here the start is chosen to put something in the spotlight. What does each writer want you to notice? -->

## Why it matters

> Two sentences say exactly the same thing in different orders. Why does one fit the paragraph and the other make you stop?

<!-- Speaker note: The **start** of a sentence tells the reader what the sentence is about. The rest of the sentence adds something **new** about it. When each start picks up something the reader already knows, the paragraph flows from one sentence to the next. -->

## Think, pair, share

**If two sentences mean exactly the same, can one still be better?**

- Better for whom?
- Does it depend on what came just before?

<!-- Speaker note: Yes: the better one depends on the sentence before it. On its own, neither is wrong. In a paragraph, the one that starts with something known flows. Give 30 seconds alone, 2 minutes in pairs, then take answers from both sides. There is no single right answer: ask for a reason, and let students change their minds. -->

## Predict

**'Wombats dig long burrows under the ground.' Which sentence fits best next?**

- Both fit equally well
- Their burrows can be up to 30 metres long.
- Up to 30 metres is how long their burrows can be.
- Neither fits

<!-- Speaker note: What happens: Their burrows can be up to 30 metres long. It starts with what you just read about (burrows) and adds the new fact (30 metres). The other version starts with the new fact, so you have to hold it until the end to see what it is about. Take two or three predictions before anyone runs it. -->

## Reading the starts

Now look at what a start can do when the writer moves a time to the front.

$$\text{Underline each start}$$

1) $\text{Read only the starts}$ — Read them in a row. Do they follow a thread (wombats, their burrows, they)?
2) $\text{Find a jump}$ — A start that names something new breaks the thread. The reader has to stop.
3) $\text{Move it}$ — Rewrite so the start picks up something known, and the new thing comes at the end.

<!-- Speaker note: Model this live on the board before revealing anything: write each line and say why, pausing to ask the class what comes next. Then click to reveal the lines and compare. Script. ## Reading the starts

**'After the storm, the creek flooded the walking track.' What does putting After the storm first do?**

- Nothing: the start of a sentence doesn't matter
- It shows the storm was the most important thing in the whole text
- It puts the time in the spotlight, so the reader knows when before what
- It makes the sentence a question

* Answer: It puts the time in the spotlight, so the reader knows when before what

<!-- Speaker note: Moving the time to the start gives it the spotlight: the reader learns when first, then what happened. -->

## Find Nib's jump

**Here is Nib's paragraph, one sentence per line. Click the sentence whose start jumps to something the reader hasn't met yet.**

```
 1  Bees live together in a hive.
 2  They build the hive from wax.
 3  A sticky substance called propolis is used by them to seal gaps.
 4  Inside the hive, the bees store honey for winter.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3 starts with 'A sticky substance called propolis', which is brand new. Starting with 'They' (the bees) and ending with propolis would keep the thread: They seal gaps with a sticky substance called propolis. -->

## Why known first?

**Why does starting with something the reader knows make writing easier to follow?**

- It makes sentences shorter
- It means you never repeat a word
- The reader connects the new information to what they already have
- Teachers prefer it

* Answer: The reader connects the new information to what they already have

<!-- Speaker note: Known first gives the reader a hook to hang the new information on. -->

**Which version puts the reason in the spotlight?**

- Because it was raining, the match was cancelled.

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Choosing which part goes first for a reason (a time, a cause, a contrast) shows that the start is a choice the writer makes on purpose. Hints, in order: The first sentence after each gap tells you what the writer wants the reader to notice. / If the surprise is that it happened despite the cold, start with Although. ## Make: your own starts

In your own document, write a paragraph of at least four sentences about an animal, a place or a game. Make each sentence start with something the reader already knows.

Here is one example: "Our cat **Pepper** sleeps all day. **She** wakes up at dinner time. **Her favourite food** is tuna. **After dinner**, she goes out to hunt moths." The starts all pick up the cat or what the cat does.

Then read only the first few words of each sentence aloud, in a row, to a partner. Can your partner say what the paragraph is about from the starts alone?

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Writing a paragraph and then reading only its starts turns the check into a habit for their own writing. Hints, in order: Choose an animal, a place or a game you know well and write four sentences about it. / Read only the start of each sentence. Does each one pick up something from before? -->

## Exit ticket

**Which version fits after 'Our class grew sunflowers.': 'Taller than the fence they grew by March.' or 'By March, they were taller than the fence.'? Why?**

* By March, they were taller than the fence. The start puts the time first and picks up the sunflowers (they); the new fact comes at the end.
