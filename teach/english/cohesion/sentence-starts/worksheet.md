# Where a sentence starts

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Two sentences say exactly the same thing in different orders. Why does one fit the paragraph and the other make you stop?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn that the start of a sentence tells the reader what it is about, and the rest adds something new. When each start picks up something known, a paragraph flows. Moving a time, reason or contrast to the start puts it in the spotlight. Students choose between versions of the same sentence, find a start that jumps, and write a paragraph of their own and read its starts to a partner.

**Students should be able to say:** I can say what the start of a sentence tells the reader. I can choose the version of a sentence that flows from the one before. I can read the starts of my own sentences to check that my paragraph flows.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

**'Wombats dig long burrows under the ground.' Which sentence fits best next?**

☐ Both fit equally well

☐ Their burrows can be up to 30 metres long.

☐ Up to 30 metres is how long their burrows can be.

☐ Neither fits

My prediction, and why:

{{lines-3}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**What happens:** Their burrows can be up to 30 metres long. It starts with what you just read about (burrows) and adds the new fact (30 metres). The other version starts with the new fact, so you have to hold it until the end to see what it is about.

:::

## Worked example: follow the starts


:::keep-together

### Reading the starts

1. $\text{Underline each start}$ — Mark the first few words of every sentence, up to the main doing word.
2. $\text{Read only the starts}$ — Read them in a row. Do they follow a thread (wombats, their burrows, they)?
3. $\text{Find a jump}$ — A start that names something new breaks the thread. The reader has to stop.
4. $\text{Move it}$ — Rewrite so the start picks up something known, and the new thing comes at the end.

Now look at what a start can do when the writer moves a time to the front.

**'After the storm, the creek flooded the walking track.' What does putting After the storm first do?**

☐ Nothing: the start of a sentence doesn't matter

☐ It shows the storm was the most important thing in the whole text

☐ It puts the time in the spotlight, so the reader knows when before what

☐ It makes the sentence a question

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** It puts the time in the spotlight, so the reader knows when before what

Moving the time to the start gives it the spotlight: the reader learns when first, then what happened.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find Nib's jump

**Here is Nib's paragraph, one sentence per line. Click the sentence whose start jumps to something the reader hasn't met yet.**

```
 1  Bees live together in a hive.
 2  They build the hive from wax.
 3  A sticky substance called propolis is used by them to seal gaps.
 4  Inside the hive, the bees store honey for winter.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3 starts with 'A sticky substance called propolis', which is brand new. Starting with 'They' (the bees) and ending with propolis would keep the thread: They seal gaps with a sticky substance called propolis.

:::


:::keep-together

### Why known first?

**Why does starting with something the reader knows make writing easier to follow?**

☐ It makes sentences shorter

☐ It means you never repeat a word

☐ The reader connects the new information to what they already have

☐ Teachers prefer it

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** The reader connects the new information to what they already have

Known first gives the reader a hook to hang the new information on.

## A paragraph that flows


## Make: your own starts

In your own document, write a paragraph of at least four sentences about an animal, a place or a game. Make each sentence start with something the reader already knows.

Here is one example: "Our cat **Pepper** sleeps all day. **She** wakes up at dinner time. **Her favourite food** is tuna. **After dinner**, she goes out to hunt moths." The starts all pick up the cat or what the cat does.

Then read only the first few words of each sentence aloud, in a row, to a partner. Can your partner say what the paragraph is about from the starts alone?

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?

## Exit ticket

:::keep-together

**Which version fits after 'Our class grew sunflowers.': 'Taller than the fence they grew by March.' or 'By March, they were taller than the fence.'? Why?**

{{lines-3}}

:::

**Answer:** By March, they were taller than the fence. The start puts the time first and picks up the sunflowers (they); the new fact comes at the end.
