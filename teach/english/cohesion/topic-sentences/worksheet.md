# Tell the reader what's coming

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** You have one minute to find out what a two-page report says. Which sentences do you read?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn that a topic sentence tells the reader what a paragraph is about, and the other sentences are details that belong to it. Topic sentences are often first, but not always. Students label topic sentences and details, find one at the end of a paragraph, move it to the start, and write and label their own paragraph.

**Students should be able to say:** I can find the topic sentence of a paragraph, wherever it is. I can say how the details belong to the topic sentence. I can write a paragraph with a topic sentence and details that belong to it.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: find the topic sentence


:::keep-together

### Finding the topic sentence

1. $\text{Read the whole paragraph}$ — Read every sentence before deciding. The topic sentence may not be first.
2. $\text{Ask what they share}$ — What are most of the sentences about? Say it in a few words.
3. $\text{Find the sentence that says it}$ — The sentence that says what they share is the topic sentence.
4. $\text{Check the details belong}$ — Every other sentence should be an example, fact or reason for it.

Now: what makes a sentence a topic sentence?

**What makes a sentence a topic sentence?**

☐ It says what the whole paragraph is about, and the other sentences belong to it

☐ It is always the first sentence

☐ It has the most interesting fact

☐ It is the longest sentence

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** It says what the whole paragraph is about, and the other sentences belong to it

A topic sentence is the one the other sentences belong to. Its place in the paragraph can vary.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find Nib's slip

**Here is Nib's paragraph, one sentence per line. Click the sentence that doesn't belong to the topic sentence.**

```
 1  Dogs need exercise every day.
 2  A short walk helps them stay at a healthy weight.
 3  Running at the park lets them use up energy.
 4  My favourite colour is blue.
 5  Dogs that don't get exercise can become bored and chew things.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 4

Line 4: Nib's favourite colour has nothing to do with dogs needing exercise. Every detail should belong to the topic sentence. It may belong in a different paragraph, or nowhere.

:::


:::keep-together

### At the end?

**A paragraph ends with 'All of this shows that our school wastes a lot of water.' Could that be its topic sentence?**

☐ Only if the paragraph is short

☐ No: topic sentences must come first

☐ Yes: it says what the paragraph is about, even though it comes last

☐ No: it starts with All

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Yes: it says what the paragraph is about, even though it comes last

A topic sentence at the end sums up what the details have been building to. It still tells the reader what the paragraph is about.
