# Open and close

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** A friend gives you directions: turn left, go past three houses, turn right. Would it help if they first said 'It's the blue house at the end of the next street'?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn that an overview (introduction) tells the reader where a whole text is going, body paragraphs each take one part in that order, and a conclusion pulls it together with nothing new. They label paragraphs by their job, reorder a shuffled text by following its overview, and write an overview and a conclusion for given body paragraphs.

**Students should be able to say:** I can say what an overview and a conclusion do for a reader. I can check that the body paragraphs follow the overview's order. I can write an overview and a conclusion that guide a reader through my text.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: what each part does


:::keep-together

### Checking a whole text

1. $\text{Read the overview}$ — What is the text about? What parts does it promise to cover, and in what order?
2. $\text{Match the body}$ — Does each body paragraph take one of those parts, in that order?
3. $\text{Read the conclusion}$ — Does it pull the parts together, with nothing new?
4. $\text{Fix what's missing}$ — Add a missing overview, reorder the body, or move a new fact out of the conclusion.

Now: what does an overview do for the reader?

**What does an overview do for the reader?**

☐ Says what the reader should think at the end

☐ Repeats the title

☐ Says what the text is about and what it will cover, often in order

☐ Gives the most interesting fact first

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Says what the text is about and what it will cover, often in order

The overview is a map: what the text is about and the parts it will cover.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find Nib's slip

**Here is the plan for Nib's report, one paragraph per line. Click the paragraph that does the wrong job for its place.**

```
 1  Overview: Wombats are strong diggers with a clever way to protect themselves. This report covers their burrows and their tough back end.
 2  Body: Wombats dig burrows up to 30 metres long, with tunnels and rooms.
 3  Body: A wombat's rump is tough, so it can block its burrow with its back end.
 4  Body: When a predator chases it, the wombat dives into its burrow and plugs the entrance.
 5  Conclusion: Wombats also have cube-shaped droppings, which is very strange.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 5

Line 5: the conclusion brings in a new fact (wombats have cube-shaped droppings) that no paragraph covered. A conclusion pulls together what the text has already said. A new fact belongs in a body paragraph.

:::


:::keep-together

### Echo the overview

**Why might a conclusion repeat some words from the overview?**

☐ So the reader sees the text has come back to where it started

☐ Because the writer ran out of ideas

☐ Conclusions should never repeat the overview

☐ To make the text longer

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** So the reader sees the text has come back to where it started

Echoing the overview tells the reader the journey is complete: this is the point the text set out to make.
