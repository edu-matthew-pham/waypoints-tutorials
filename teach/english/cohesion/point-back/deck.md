# What does it point back to?

Holding writing together · Warm-up: pointing and joining

<!-- Speaker note: Students learn that pointing words (she, it, they, this, these) name nothing on their own: they point back to something the reader has met, and readers follow them back by meaning, not just by distance. They trace links with arrows on a short text, find the links themselves, then write their own sentences and check the links with a partner. About 40 minutes (Predict and Try 8 min · Worked example 10 min · Vary 5 min · Practise 7 min · Make and own writing 10 min). -->

---

## Today

- I can say what a pointing word such as it or they points back to.
- I can check my answer by swapping it in.
- I can make sure a reader can follow the pointing words in my own writing.

## Why it matters

> Read this: 'It was enormous. It moved slowly towards them.' What was enormous, and who is them? You cannot tell, because nothing came before.

<!-- Speaker note: Some words don't name anything themselves. **She**, **we**, **it**, **they**, **them**, **this** and **these** point back to something the reader has already met. They save the writer from repeating the same words, but only if the reader can follow them back. Nib is learning to write. Nib's notes in this course have a few slips for you to find. -->

## Following a pointing word

Now try the three questions on a word that points to a whole idea.

$$\text{Find the word}$$

1) $\text{Ask who or what}$ — She is a person, it is one thing, they and these are more than one.
2) $\text{Look back}$ — Read back for something that fits. The closest one is a good first guess, not the answer.
3) $\text{Swap it in}$ — Put your answer in place of the word. If the sentence still makes sense, you found it.

**Nib traced 'it' in this sentence: 'Zara put the cake on the bench because it was too hot to hold.' Click the line where Nib first goes wrong.**

```
 1  Nib finds the pointing word: it.
 2  Nib asks what it could be: one thing.
 3  Nib picks the closest thing before it: the bench.
 4  Nib writes: it means the bench.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: Nib picked the closest thing without swapping it back in. 'The bench was too hot to hold' does not make sense. The cake was too hot. -->

## Why point back?

**Why do writers use words like she, it and they at all?**

- To make the reader guess
- Because names are not allowed twice
- So they don't have to repeat the same name in every sentence
- Because they are shorter to spell

* Answer: So they don't have to repeat the same name in every sentence

<!-- Speaker note: Pointing words keep the text moving without repeating the same name over and over. They only work when the reader can tell what they point to. -->

## Make: your own

In your own document, write three sentences about something your class did. Use *she*, *he*, *it*, *they* or *this* at least twice.

Then use the highlighter in your document. Colour each pointing word, and colour what it points back to the same colour.

Here is one example: "Our class went to the museum on Tuesday. **It** had a whale skeleton. **We** drew **it** for art." Here *It* and the second *it* point back to the museum, and *We* points back to our class.

Then swap with a partner. Can your partner say what every pointing word points to without asking you?

If a partner hesitated at one of your pointing words, change it to the name.

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Writing their own sentences and tracing their own pointing words moves the skill from reading into writing. Hints, in order: Think of a trip, a game or a project your class did. Write three sentences about it. / Highlight a pointing word and what it points back to in the same colour. If you can't find the second one, a reader can't either. -->
