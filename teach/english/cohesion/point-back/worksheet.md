# What does it point back to?

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Read this: 'It was enormous. It moved slowly towards them.' What was enormous, and who is them? You cannot tell, because nothing came before.


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn that pointing words (she, it, they, this, these) name nothing on their own: they point back to something the reader has met, and readers follow them back by meaning, not just by distance. They trace links with arrows on a short text, find the links themselves, then write their own sentences and check the links with a partner.

**Students should be able to say:** I can say what a pointing word such as it or they points back to. I can check my answer by swapping it in. I can make sure a reader can follow the pointing words in my own writing.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: follow it back


:::keep-together

### Following a pointing word

1. $\text{Find the word}$ — Spot a word that does not name anything itself: she, he, we, it, they, them, this, these, that.
2. $\text{Ask who or what}$ — She is a person, it is one thing, they and these are more than one.
3. $\text{Look back}$ — Read back for something that fits. The closest one is a good first guess, not the answer.
4. $\text{Swap it in}$ — Put your answer in place of the word. If the sentence still makes sense, you found it.

Now try the three questions on a word that points to a whole idea.

**'Our team lost the final. This made the coach very quiet on the bus.' What does This point back to?**

☐ The final

☐ The bus

☐ The whole idea: our team losing the final

☐ Our team

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** The whole idea: our team losing the final

This can point back to a whole idea, not just one word. Losing the final is what made the coach quiet.

:::


:::keep-together

### Why point back?

**Why do writers use words like she, it and they at all?**

☐ To make the reader guess

☐ Because names are not allowed twice

☐ So they don't have to repeat the same name in every sentence

☐ Because they are shorter to spell

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** So they don't have to repeat the same name in every sentence

Pointing words keep the text moving without repeating the same name over and over. They only work when the reader can tell what they point to.

## Make: your own

In your own document, write three sentences about something your class did. Use *she*, *he*, *it*, *they* or *this* at least twice.

Then use the highlighter in your document. Colour each pointing word, and colour what it points back to the same colour.

Here is one example: "Our class went to the museum on Tuesday. **It** had a whale skeleton. **We** drew **it** for art." Here *It* and the second *it* point back to the museum, and *We* points back to our class.

Then swap with a partner. Can your partner say what every pointing word points to without asking you?

If a partner hesitated at one of your pointing words, change it to the name.

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?
