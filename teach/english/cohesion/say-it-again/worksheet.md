# Say it again, on purpose

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** You were told not to repeat words. So why does a good report say volcano five times?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn that repeating a key word on purpose, or swapping in a synonym or antonym, makes a chain that keeps the reader on the same thing. Repetition is a tool: it is clearer than it or they when those could point to two things. Students colour chains in short texts and write their own paragraph and highlight its chain.

**Students should be able to say:** I can find the chain of repeats and synonyms for a key word. I can say why a writer repeated a key word. I can repeat a key word on purpose in my own writing.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: find the chains


:::keep-together

### Finding a chain

1. $\text{Find the key word}$ — What is the text about? That word, such as volcano, is the start of a chain.
2. $\text{Follow the repeats}$ — Find each place it is repeated, swapped for a word that means the same, or set against its opposite.
3. $\text{Check each gap}$ — Where the chain goes quiet, can the reader still tell what the text is about?
4. $\text{Repeat where needed}$ — If it or they could point to two things, repeat the key word instead.

Now see how a word with the same meaning carries a chain.

**In 'The drought lasted three years. During the dry spell, farmers sold their sheep.', what is dry spell doing?**

☐ It starts a new topic

☐ It means the same as drought, so it continues the chain

☐ It points forward to the sheep

☐ It is a mistake: the writer should not change the word

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** It means the same as drought, so it continues the chain

Dry spell is a synonym for drought, so the chain continues with a little variety.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find Nib's slip

**Here is Nib's paragraph, one sentence per line. Click the first line where a reader can't tell what Nib means, because Nib should have repeated a key word.**

```
 1  Kelp forests grow along the coast of Tasmania.
 2  Sea urchins and rock lobsters live in the kelp forests.
 3  They eat it.
 4  This keeps the forest healthy.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: 'They eat it' could mean the urchins eat the kelp, or the rock lobsters eat the urchins. Repeating the key words ('The urchins eat the kelp') makes it clear.

### Repeat or point?

**When is repeating a key word better than using it or they?**

☐ When it or they could point to more than one thing

☐ Never: always use it or they

☐ Only in the first sentence

☐ Always: never use it or they

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** When it or they could point to more than one thing

Use a pointing word when only one thing fits. Repeat the key word when two things could fit.

## Three chains


## Make: your own chain

In your own document, write four sentences about one thing, such as a sport, an animal or a place. Repeat its name at least once, and use a word that means the same at least once.

Then use the highlighter in your document to colour the chain: every word that stands for that one thing.

Here is one example: "**Netball** is my favourite sport. I play **the game** every Saturday. Our **netball** team is called the Comets. We won every match this term." The chain is *Netball*, *the game* and *netball*.

Then swap with a partner. Highlight each other's chain without asking. Do you find the same words the writer meant?

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?
