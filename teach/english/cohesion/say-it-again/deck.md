# Say it again, on purpose

Holding writing together · Words that hold it together

<!-- Speaker note: Students learn that repeating a key word on purpose, or swapping in a synonym or antonym, makes a chain that keeps the reader on the same thing. Repetition is a tool: it is clearer than it or they when those could point to two things. Students colour chains in short texts and write their own paragraph and highlight its chain. About 40 minutes (Predict and Try 8 min · Worked example 10 min · Vary 6 min · Practise 6 min · Make and own writing 10 min). -->

- I can find the chain of repeats and synonyms for a key word.
- I can say why a writer repeated a key word.
- I can repeat a key word on purpose in my own writing.

## Why it matters

> You were told not to repeat words. So why does a good report say volcano five times?

<!-- Speaker note: You may have been told not to repeat words. But writers repeat **key words** on purpose: each repeat tells the reader "still the same thing". A word that means the same (a **synonym**) does the same job with a little variety. Together, the repeats make a **chain** that runs through the text. A word that means the **opposite** (an **antonym**) starts a second chain that runs against the first, which ties them together. -->

## Predict

**Version A: 'The kelp forest is home to many animals. Sea urchins eat it. Rock lobsters eat them, which protects it.' Version B: 'The kelp forest is home to many animals. Sea urchins eat the kelp. Rock lobsters eat the urchins, which protects the forest.' Which is easier to follow?**

- Version A, which avoids repeating
- Neither makes sense
- Both are just as easy
- Version B, which repeats the key words

## Finding a chain

Now see how a word with the same meaning carries a chain.

$$\text{Find the key word}$$

1) $\text{Follow the repeats}$ — Find each place it is repeated, swapped for a word that means the same, or set against its opposite.
2) $\text{Check each gap}$ — Where the chain goes quiet, can the reader still tell what the text is about?
3) $\text{Repeat where needed}$ — If it or they could point to two things, repeat the key word instead.

## Finding a chain

**In 'The drought lasted three years. During the dry spell, farmers sold their sheep.', what is dry spell doing?**

- It starts a new topic
- It means the same as drought, so it continues the chain
- It points forward to the sheep
- It is a mistake: the writer should not change the word

* Answer: It means the same as drought, so it continues the chain

<!-- Speaker note: Dry spell is a synonym for drought, so the chain continues with a little variety. -->

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Changing what counts as a chain (synonyms and opposites, not just exact repeats) widens the move. Hints, in order: Chain 1 is the dry words: drought, dry spell, dry. Chain 2 is the wet words, which are the opposite. / Rain, rainfall and floods all belong to the wet chain. For students who need more support: A card: Key word. Same word, same-meaning word, opposite word. Repeat when it could mean two things. For early finishers: Find a speech (a famous one, or a school captain's) that repeats a phrase on purpose. Why does the speaker repeat it? When students get stuck: A student colours every noun. Ask: Is this word about the same thing as the rest of the chain? Fix: One chain, one thing. Leave other words uncoloured. / A student thinks repeating is always bad style. Ask: Read Version A of the kelp paragraph aloud. Where did you get lost? Fix: Repeat where the reader needs it; use it where only one thing fits. / A student colours a word the board calls a stray, and argues it belongs. Ask: Is it a word for the same thing, or something linked to it? Fix: Good argument either way. Chains are about the reader staying on track; the board only knows the words that were planted. -->

## Make: your own chain

In your own document, write four sentences about one thing, such as a sport, an animal or a place. Repeat its name at least once, and use a word that means the same at least once.

Then use the highlighter in your document to colour the chain: every word that stands for that one thing.

Here is one example: "**Netball** is my favourite sport. I play **the game** every Saturday. Our **netball** team is called the Comets. We won every match this term." The chain is *Netball*, *the game* and *netball*.

Then swap with a partner. Highlight each other's chain without asking. Do you find the same words the writer meant?

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Writing a paragraph with a deliberate chain and then colouring it is the production half of the node. Hints, in order: Choose one thing you know well and write four sentences about it. / Say its name more than once, and use another word for it once. Where an it or they could mean two things, which would be clearer? -->
