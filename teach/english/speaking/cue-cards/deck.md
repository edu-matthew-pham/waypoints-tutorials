# From script to cue cards

Speaking to an audience · Prepare: cards and register

<!-- Speaker note: Students pick the key words that bring each part of a script back to mind on the cue board, find the card that is really a script, cut three scripts down with a card limit and a quarter-of-the-script limit, and print cards for a talk of their own. About 45 minutes (Predict and Try 8 min · Worked example 10 min · Vary and Practise 12 min · Make 10 min · Own class 5 min). -->

## Today

- I can pick the key words that bring a part of my talk back to mind.
- I can keep each cue card short enough to take in at a glance.
- I can print cue cards and say my talk from them.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Try board yourself: click the words you would need, then read the cards. The one idea: a cue card is a reminder, not a copy. The lesson is tagged AC9E6LY07 node 1 (organise ideas and choose vocabulary for a presentation), because choosing key words is choosing the vocabulary that carries each part. This is partial coverage: the node also asks for a plan with three supporting points (lesson 1 and the challenge) and for naming topic vocabulary choices, which students do here only as key words. Delivery from the cards happens in class (node 3 of the same descriptor is tagged on the challenge). What the board counts: a key word is any word the student clicks; neighbouring key words stay together on a card, and a full stop, comma, colon, semicolon, question mark or exclamation mark after a key word is dropped. A card is "over" when it holds more than the limit named in the step (6 in Try, 5 in Vary, 8 in Practise and Make). The percentage is key words as a share of the script, rounded to a whole number, so 25.4% shows as 25 and passes. The board never judges which words are the right ones; a student tests that by covering the script and speaking. Print cue cards makes index-card pages two across (92 by 58 mm cards). Print highlighted script prints the whole script with the key words marked, for a speaker who wants more on the page. Both open the browser's print dialog. The lost property box, garden club, quiet room and bike rack scripts are made up for the lesson. Teacher: Try: ask what a card is, if the script is the talk. Teacher: Worked example: ask what Nib would do with card 3 in front of an audience. Teacher: Vary: with five words allowed, ask which words had to go. -->

## Why it matters

> If you read your talk word for word, you look at the page. If you say it, you look at the people. What would you put on a card to say it?

<!-- Speaker note: Once the script is written, you do not read it out. You say it, and a **cue card** reminds you what comes next. A good cue card: - holds only the **key words**: the few words that bring the whole part back to mind, - is short enough to take in at a glance, so you can look up and keep speaking, - has one card for each part of the talk (the opening, each point and the close). The talk is no shorter. The words are still yours. The card is just the cue. -->

## Think, pair, share

**Is it cheating to use cards?**

- What does an audience notice if the speaker reads from a page?
- What does a speaker lose if they have nothing at all?

<!-- Speaker note: Cards are a tool, like a map. The goal is to look at the listeners. Some speakers use none, some use a highlighted script. The choice depends on the talk and the speaker. ## Predict

**Nib has a 90-word script for a one-minute talk, in four parts. How many words does Nib really need on the cue cards to say it all?**

- All 90, in case Nib forgets a word
- About half, so Nib can follow it closely
- None, because Nib wrote the script
- About 15 to 20, a few for each part

## What goes on a card

Now: what is a cue card for?

$$\text{Cut the script into its parts}$$

1) $\text{Find the key words}$ — The few words that bring the part back.
2) $\text{Keep the exact facts}$ — Numbers, names and hard words.
3) $\text{Keep a card short}$ — A glance, not a read.
4) $\text{Test it}$ — Cover the script. Can you say each part?

## What goes on a card

**What is a cue card for?**

- Reminding the speaker what comes next, so they can look up and speak
- Proving the speaker wrote the talk
- Holding the whole script so the speaker can read it
- Showing the audience the main points

* Answer: Reminding the speaker what comes next, so they can look up and speak

<!-- Speaker note: A cue card gives the speaker the next idea, so their eyes and voice can stay with the listeners. -->

## Find the card that is a script

**Here are Nib's four cue cards, one per line. Click the card that is really a script.**

```
 1  Card 1: lost property box
 2  Card 2: First, no name, jumper
 3  Card 3: Second, nobody checks the box. Things sit there for months and then get thrown out. It is a waste of good jumpers.
 4  Card 4: name, check, Fridays
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Card 3 is the script with almost nothing cut. Nib will read it, not say it. It needs only the key words, such as: Second, nobody checks, months. -->

## What has to be on the card?

**Nib's talk says 17 drink bottles were handed in last term. What should go on the card for that part?**

- 17 bottles, since Nib needs to get that exactly right
- The word 'bottles'
- Nothing, because Nib will remember
- The whole sentence about the bottles

* Answer: 17 bottles, since Nib needs to get that exactly right

## How many cards?

**A talk has an opening, two points and a close. The speaker makes three cards: the opening, point one and point two. What is the problem?**

- Three cards is too many for a short talk
- The close has no card, and it is the part the speaker most needs to get right
- Cards should only be made for the points
- Nothing, because the speaker will remember the close

* Answer: The close has no card, and it is the part the speaker most needs to get right

<!-- Speaker note: Every part of the talk needs a card, and the close most of all. It is the last thing listeners hear. Ask on mini whiteboards or by show of hands before the reveal. -->

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Cutting a different script with a lower limit on words per card shows that a card can be shorter than students first expect. Hints, in order: Which words would you need to start each part? / Which words are the ones you could not afford to forget? For students who need more support: A card: Cover the script. Say the part. Which word did you need? Keep only those. For early finishers: Make a second set of cards with half as many words. Say the talk from them, and decide which set worked better and why. The hard part, on purpose: Students copy the script onto the cards 'just in case'. Ask them to cover the script and say a part from the card: the words they did not need are the ones to cut. When students get stuck: A student clicks nearly every word. Ask: If you could only keep one word from this sentence, which would you keep? Fix: Cut to the word or phrase that carries the idea. / A student's cards are empty for one part. Ask: How will you remember this part? Fix: Add one or two key words for it. Every part needs a card. / A student picks only the first words of each sentence. Ask: Do those words bring the idea back, or just the start? Fix: Pick the words that name the idea, not the ones that start the sentence. -->

## Exit ticket

**Nib's card for a part of the talk has three whole sentences on it. What is wrong, and what should be on it?**

* It is the script. Nib will read it instead of speaking. The card should hold only the key words, and any number or name that has to be right.
