# Write it to be heard

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** A sentence can be perfect on paper and impossible to follow out loud. What changes when no one can read it again?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students find signpost words in a script, see why a key idea is said twice, spot the sentence a listener would lose, choose the version of a sentence that can be said aloud, rework one long sentence into short ones, and write the start of a script.

**Students should be able to say:** I can explain why a script is written differently from a report. I can use short sentences, signpost words and a repeated main idea. I can explain a special word at the moment I use it.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: what a script does


:::keep-together

### Write for the ear

1. $\text{Short sentences}$ — One idea, one breath.
2. $\text{Signpost words}$ — First, second, so, in short.
3. $\text{Say it twice}$ — Once near the start, once near the end.
4. $\text{Explain special words}$ — Use the word, then say what it means.
5. $\text{Speak to them}$ — Use you, ask a question, then answer it.

Now: why say the main idea twice?

**A speaker says the main idea near the start and again in the last sentence. Why?**

☐ It makes the talk longer

☐ The speaker has run out of ideas

☐ A talk has to use the same words twice by rule

☐ Listeners cannot reread, so a second chance helps them keep it

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Listeners cannot reread, so a second chance helps them keep it

On paper, a reader can look back. In a talk, hearing the main idea twice gives the listener a second chance to catch it.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find the sentence a listener would lose

**Here is part of Nib's script about the school bubblers, one sentence per line. Click the sentence a listener would lose.**

```
 1  Good morning, Year 5. Today I want to talk about our school bubblers.
 2  Three of them are broken, and that is a problem.
 3  The bubblers by the oval, which were fitted a long time ago before the new courts were built, are among the three that we cannot use, so students line up at the single working tap.
 4  So here is my idea: fix them this term.
 5  In short, three broken bubblers means a long queue for water.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: it packs three ideas into one sentence, with a clause tucked into the middle. Written down, a reader could manage it. Out loud, the listener has lost the first idea before the speaker finishes. Nib should say it as three short sentences.

:::


:::keep-together

### What can be said in a breath?

**Which of these can a speaker say naturally in one breath?**

☐ The broken bubbler, which was installed last year after a request from the student council, will be replaced next term, which the principal confirmed.

☐ None of them, because every sentence is too hard to say

☐ Because the bubbler is broken, we are using the tap.

☐ Replacement of the bubbler, subject to approval, is anticipated.

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Because the bubbler is broken, we are using the tap.

A sentence with two parts joined by because is fine to speak: it has one idea and a breath's length. What goes wrong is length and clauses buried in the middle.

:::

## From the page to the ear

Here is one long written sentence. Copy it into your own document.

> The oval, which has been wet since the storm last week and which the groundskeeper says will not be usable until the drains are cleared, is why all training has moved to the gym, where space is limited.

In your own document, rewrite it as three or more sentences for a speaker to say. Keep every sentence under 15 words. Use at least one joining word such as *because*, *when* or *until*.

Here is one example, for a different sentence. Written: "The pool, which closed on Monday after a leak was found under the deep end and which the council says will stay shut until the repairs finish, is why lessons have moved to the hall." Spoken: "The pool closed on Monday. A leak was found under the deep end. It will stay shut **until** the repairs finish. So lessons have moved to the hall."

Then say your sentences aloud to a partner. Can they follow it without looking? Where did either of you run out of breath?

A short sentence with a joining word is a complex sentence you can say in one breath. Listen for where the breath runs out.
