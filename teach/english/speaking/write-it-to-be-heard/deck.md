# Write it to be heard

Speaking to an audience · Warm-up: a talk with a point

<!-- Speaker note: Students find signpost words in a script, see why a key idea is said twice, spot the sentence a listener would lose, choose the version of a sentence that can be said aloud, rework one long sentence into short ones, and write the start of a script. ## Today

- I can explain why a script is written differently from a report.
- I can use short sentences, signpost words and a repeated main idea.
- I can explain a special word at the moment I use it.

## Why it matters

> A sentence can be perfect on paper and impossible to follow out loud. What changes when no one can read it again?

<!-- Speaker note: A reader can slow down, look back and read a hard sentence twice. A listener cannot. So a script is written for the **ear**, not the eye. Writing for the ear means: - **Short sentences**, each one a breath long, with one idea in it. - **Signpost words** (first, second, so, in short) that tell listeners where they are. - **Saying the key idea twice**, in different words. - **Explaining a special word** when you use it, not before and not after. A longer sentence is fine if it is easy to say. Two ideas joined by a word like *because* can sound natural, as long as the speaker can say it in a single breath. -->

## Predict

**Nib reads this sentence aloud once: 'The reduction in lunchtime seating, which was caused by the building of the new library wing, has led to crowding in the quad.' What will most listeners have by the end?**

- They lose track partway, because they hear it once, in order
- More than a reader would, because speech is slower
- The same as a reader, because the words are the same
- The whole idea, because the sentence is complete

## Write for the ear

Now: why say the main idea twice?

$$\text{Short sentences}$$

1) $\text{Signpost words}$ — First, second, so, in short.
2) $\text{Say it twice}$ — Once near the start, once near the end.
3) $\text{Explain special words}$ — Use the word, then say what it means.
4) $\text{Speak to them}$ — Use you, ask a question, then answer it.

## Write for the ear

**A speaker says the main idea near the start and again in the last sentence. Why?**

- It makes the talk longer
- The speaker has run out of ideas
- A talk has to use the same words twice by rule
- Listeners cannot reread, so a second chance helps them keep it

* Answer: Listeners cannot reread, so a second chance helps them keep it

<!-- Speaker note: On paper, a reader can look back. In a talk, hearing the main idea twice gives the listener a second chance to catch it. -->

## Find the sentence a listener would lose

**Here is part of Nib's script about the school bubblers, one sentence per line. Click the sentence a listener would lose.**

```
 1  Good morning, Year 5. Today I want to talk about our school bubblers.
 2  Three of them are broken, and that is a problem.
 3  The bubblers by the oval, which were fitted a long time ago before the new courts were built, are among the three that we cannot use, so students line up at the single working tap.
 4  So here is my idea: fix them this term.
 5  In short, three broken bubblers means a long queue for water.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: it packs three ideas into one sentence, with a clause tucked into the middle. Written down, a reader could manage it. Out loud, the listener has lost the first idea before the speaker finishes. Nib should say it as three short sentences. -->

## What can be said in a breath?

**Which of these can a speaker say naturally in one breath?**

- The broken bubbler, which was installed last year after a request from the student council, will be replaced next term, which the principal confirmed.
- None of them, because every sentence is too hard to say
- Because the bubbler is broken, we are using the tap.
- Replacement of the bubbler, subject to approval, is anticipated.

* Answer: Because the bubbler is broken, we are using the tap.

<!-- Speaker note: A sentence with two parts joined by because is fine to speak: it has one idea and a breath's length. What goes wrong is length and clauses buried in the middle. -->

## When to explain a word

**A speaker uses the word 'habitat' in a talk to Year 5. When is the best moment to say what it means?**

- Right after using it, in the same breath
- Never, because listeners will look it up
- In the close, so everyone remembers
- Before the talk starts, in the title

* Answer: Right after using it, in the same breath

<!-- Speaker note: The listener needs the meaning at the moment of the word. Say it, then say what it means. Ask on mini whiteboards or by show of hands before the reveal. -->

## Exit ticket

**A speaker says: 'The reduction in lunchtime seating, which was caused by construction, has led to crowding.' Say it so a listener can follow.**

* Any version in short sentences: 'There is less lunchtime seating. The building work caused it. So the quad is crowded.'
