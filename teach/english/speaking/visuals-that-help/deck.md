## Why it matters

> You have a slide up and you are talking. Where do your listeners look, and what do they take in?

<!-- Speaker note: A **visual** is anything the listeners see while you speak: a slide, a chart, a drawing, a photo, an object. A good visual does one of three jobs. - It **adds** something the words cannot: a shape, a number pattern, a picture of the thing. - It **repeats** the words on the screen. This usually hurts: listeners read ahead and stop listening. - It **distracts**: it moves, flashes or has nothing to do with the point, so listeners watch it instead of you. The speaker is the main show. The visual is there to help the speaker, not to speak for them. -->

**Does a talk need slides at all?**

- What can a slide show that a speaker cannot say?

## Predict

**Nib puts every sentence of the talk on a slide and reads them out. What will most of the listeners do?**

- Look at Nib the whole time
- Read ahead on their own and stop listening to Nib
- Remember more than if there were no slide
- Listen closely, because they hear and read the same words twice

<!-- Speaker note: What happens: Read ahead on their own and stop listening. People read faster than a speaker talks, so they finish early and turn off. A slide that repeats the words gives them no reason to listen. Take two or three predictions before anyone runs it. -->

## A visual with a job

Now: what job should a visual have?

$$\text{Name the point}$$

1) $\text{Ask what words cannot do}$ — Show a shape, a pattern, a picture, a layout.
2) $\text{Choose the visual}$ — A chart, a map, a photo, a drawing.
3) $\text{Keep the writing short}$ — A title or a label, not the script.
4) $\text{Say what is on it}$ — Point to it, then explain what it shows.

<!-- Speaker note: Model this live on the board before revealing anything: write each line and say why, pausing to ask the class what comes next. Then click to reveal the lines and compare. Script. ## A visual with a job

**What is the best job for a visual in a talk?**

- Holding the whole script so the speaker does not forget it
- Showing something the words cannot, such as a shape, a pattern or a picture
- Filling the screen so there is no empty space
- Making the talk last longer

* Answer: Showing something the words cannot, such as a shape, a pattern or a picture

<!-- Speaker note: A visual earns its place by showing what words cannot. Otherwise the listener is better off watching the speaker. -->

## Find the slide that repeats

**Here is Nib's slide plan for a talk about the bike rack, one slide per line. Click the slide that repeats the talk.**

```
 1  Slide 1: A photo of bikes lying on the ground by the fence.
 2  Slide 2: Every sentence Nib will say about the problem, typed out in full.
 3  Slide 3: A bar chart: 40 students ride, 8 bike spots.
 4  Slide 4: A drawing of how the new rack would hold the bikes in two rows.
```

* Answer: line 2

## How much writing?

**How much writing should a slide have?**

- A few words at most, such as a title or a label
- None at all, ever
- As much as fits, so listeners can read the talk
- Whatever the speaker says next, word for word

* Answer: A few words at most, such as a title or a label

<!-- Speaker note: A few words, such as a title or a label, can orient listeners. Anything more, and they read instead of listen. -->

## Add, repeat or distract?

**A speaker says, 'This tap has been broken for a month.' The slide shows a photo of the broken tap. What does the photo do?**

- Repeats the words, so it should go
- Distracts, because photos always do
- Adds something the words cannot: what the broken tap looks like
- Nothing, because the speaker said it already

* Answer: Adds something the words cannot: what the broken tap looks like

<!-- Speaker note: A photo of the thing shows what words cannot. The speaker's words and the photo do different jobs. Ask on mini whiteboards or by show of hands before the reveal. -->

## Exit ticket

**A slide has the exact sentence the speaker is about to say. What happens, and what could go on the slide instead?**

* Listeners read it first and stop listening. A picture, chart or map that shows the point could go there instead.
