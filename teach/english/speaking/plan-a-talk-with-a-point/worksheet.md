# Plan a talk that has a point

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** You have two minutes and a room full of people. What do you decide before you write a single word?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students decide the purpose and audience of a talk, label a plan's opening, points, details and close, put a shuffled plan in order, find the line that does not belong in a close, and plan a short talk of their own.

**Students should be able to say:** I can say who a talk is for and what I want listeners to know, think or do afterwards. I can plan a talk with an opening, two or three points, a detail for each, and a close. I can check that every part of my plan serves my purpose.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::


## Purpose and audience: which talk sticks?


:::keep-together

**Nib has two minutes to talk to Year 5 about the school library. Plan A lists everything Nib knows about the library. Plan B starts with one sentence: 'Year 5 should leave wanting to join the book club.' Which talk will Year 5 remember better?**

☐ Both the same, because the topic is the same

☐ Plan B, because every part of it has a job

☐ Plan A, because it gives them more facts

☐ Neither, because it depends on Nib's voice

My answer, and why:

{{lines-3}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**What happens:** Plan B. When every point works towards one aim, listeners can tell what matters. A heap of facts leaves them with no way to tell which one to keep.

:::

## Worked example: what a plan is for


:::keep-together

### Aim first

1. $\text{Decide the purpose}$ — What should listeners know, think or do afterwards?
2. $\text{Decide the audience}$ — Who are they, and what do they already know?
3. $\text{Choose two or three points}$ — Keep only the ideas that serve the purpose.
4. $\text{Back each point}$ — Add a fact or example for each one.
5. $\text{Open and close}$ — Say where you are going, then say where you have been.

Now: why is a topic not enough?

**Nib wrote the aim 'Tell Year 5 about the library.' Why is that not enough to plan from?**

☐ It says nothing about what listeners should know, think or do afterwards

☐ It is too short to be a sentence

☐ Aims should always name three points

☐ Year 5 already know all about the library

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** It says nothing about what listeners should know, think or do afterwards

A topic says what the talk is about. An aim says what the listeners will do or think because of it. Without an aim, Nib cannot tell which facts belong.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find Nib's slip

**Here is Nib's plan for a talk about the new beanbags, one line each. Click the line that does not belong in a close.**

```
 1  Opening: Ask who has ever read in a really uncomfortable chair.
 2  Point 1: The new beanbags are for reading, not for sleeping.
 3  Detail: There are twelve, and they sit in the quiet corner.
 4  Point 2: You can book a beanbag for lunchtime.
 5  Detail: The booking sheet is on the library door.
 6  Close: Say it again: read, book, relax. Then ask them to try one this week.
 7  Close: Finish by telling them the library now has a new printer for homework.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 7

Line 7: a close says the main idea again and what to do. The new printer is a fresh idea, and listeners have no time left to take it in. It belongs in a point, or nowhere.

:::


:::keep-together

### How many points?

**A two-minute talk has eight main points, each with a detail. What is the likely problem?**

☐ The talk needs more details for each point

☐ Eight is an unlucky number of points

☐ Listeners will not hold that many, and each point gets only a few seconds

☐ The opening should come last

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Listeners will not hold that many, and each point gets only a few seconds

People hold only a few ideas from a talk they hear once. Two or three points, each with a detail, is what two minutes can carry.

:::


:::keep-together

### Which way to present?

**Nib wants Year 5 to learn how to fold a paper plane. Which way of presenting suits the listeners and the purpose?**

☐ A list of steps read out without stopping

☐ A poster only, because Year 5 can read

☐ A talk with Nib folding a plane in front of them as each step is said

☐ A talk alone, because talks are quicker

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** A talk with Nib folding a plane in front of them as each step is said

The purpose is to show how, so the best choice shows it. A talk with a live demonstration lets listeners see each step as they hear it. Choosing how to present is part of planning.

:::
