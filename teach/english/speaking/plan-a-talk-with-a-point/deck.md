# Plan a talk that has a point

Speaking to an audience · Warm-up: a talk with a point

<!-- Speaker note: Students decide the purpose and audience of a talk, label a plan's opening, points, details and close, put a shuffled plan in order, find the line that does not belong in a close, and plan a short talk of their own. About 45 minutes (Predict and Try 8 min · Worked example 12 min · Vary 7 min · Make 12 min · Own class 8 min). -->

## Today

- I can say who a talk is for and what I want listeners to know, think or do afterwards.
- I can plan a talk with an opening, two or three points, a detail for each, and a close.
- I can check that every part of my plan serves my purpose.

## Why it matters

> You have two minutes and a room full of people. What do you decide before you write a single word?

<!-- Speaker note: A talk is heard once, at the speed the speaker chooses. Listeners cannot go back and read it again, so a talk needs a plan that is easy to follow. Two choices come before the plan: - **Purpose**: what you want the listeners to know, think or do afterwards. - **Audience**: who is listening, and what they already know. Then comes the plan itself: - **Opening**: tells listeners what the talk is about and why they should listen. - **Points**: the two or three main ideas, in an order that makes sense. - **Details**: a fact or example that backs up each point. - **Close**: says the main idea again and what listeners should do. -->

## Think, pair, share

**How much of a talk can a listener keep?**

- Think of a talk you heard at assembly. What can you still say from it?
- Why do you think that part stayed?

<!-- Speaker note: Use assembly, not a named person's talk. Listen for one idea, not a list. That is the point of an aim. Give 30 seconds alone, 2 minutes in pairs, then take answers from both sides. There is no single right answer: ask for a reason, and let students change their minds. -->

## Purpose and audience: which talk sticks?

**Nib has two minutes to talk to Year 5 about the school library. Plan A lists everything Nib knows about the library. Plan B starts with one sentence: 'Year 5 should leave wanting to join the book club.' Which talk will Year 5 remember better?**

- Both the same, because the topic is the same
- Plan B, because every part of it has a job
- Plan A, because it gives them more facts
- Neither, because it depends on Nib's voice

<!-- Speaker note: What happens: Plan B. When every point works towards one aim, listeners can tell what matters. A heap of facts leaves them with no way to tell which one to keep. Take two or three answers from the class before moving on. -->

<!-- Speaker note: Teacher: ask what Year 5 would repeat to a friend afterwards. -->

## Aim first

Now: why is a topic not enough?

$$\text{Decide the purpose}$$

1) $\text{Decide the audience}$ — Who are they, and what do they already know?
2) $\text{Choose two or three points}$ — Keep only the ideas that serve the purpose.
3) $\text{Back each point}$ — Add a fact or example for each one.
4) $\text{Open and close}$ — Say where you are going, then say where you have been.

## Aim first

**Nib wrote the aim 'Tell Year 5 about the library.' Why is that not enough to plan from?**

- It says nothing about what listeners should know, think or do afterwards
- It is too short to be a sentence
- Aims should always name three points
- Year 5 already know all about the library

* Answer: It says nothing about what listeners should know, think or do afterwards

<!-- Speaker note: A topic says what the talk is about. An aim says what the listeners will do or think because of it. Without an aim, Nib cannot tell which facts belong. -->

## Find Nib's slip

**Here is Nib's plan for a talk about the new beanbags, one line each. Click the line that does not belong in a close.**

```
 1  Opening: Ask who has ever read in a really uncomfortable chair.
 2  Point 1: The new beanbags are for reading, not for sleeping.
 3  Detail: There are twelve, and they sit in the quiet corner.
 4  Point 2: You can book a beanbag for lunchtime.
 5  Detail: The booking sheet is on the library door.
 6  Close: Say it again: read, book, relax. Then ask them to try one this week.
 7  Close: Finish by telling them the library now has a new printer for homework.
```

* Answer: line 7

<!-- Speaker note: Line 7: a close says the main idea again and what to do. The new printer is a fresh idea, and listeners have no time left to take it in. It belongs in a point, or nowhere. -->

## How many points?

**A two-minute talk has eight main points, each with a detail. What is the likely problem?**

- The talk needs more details for each point
- Eight is an unlucky number of points
- Listeners will not hold that many, and each point gets only a few seconds
- The opening should come last

* Answer: Listeners will not hold that many, and each point gets only a few seconds

<!-- Speaker note: People hold only a few ideas from a talk they hear once. Two or three points, each with a detail, is what two minutes can carry. -->

## Which way to present?

**Nib wants Year 5 to learn how to fold a paper plane. Which way of presenting suits the listeners and the purpose?**

- A list of steps read out without stopping
- A poster only, because Year 5 can read
- A talk with Nib folding a plane in front of them as each step is said
- A talk alone, because talks are quicker

* Answer: A talk with Nib folding a plane in front of them as each step is said

<!-- Speaker note: The purpose is to show how, so the best choice shows it. A talk with a live demonstration lets listeners see each step as they hear it. Choosing how to present is part of planning. -->

## What is missing?

**A plan has an opening, three points and a close, but no details. What will listeners most likely miss?**

- The opening, because it comes first
- Anything that makes each point believable, such as a fact or an example
- The close, because it is short
- Nothing, because points are enough

* Answer: Anything that makes each point believable, such as a fact or an example

<!-- Speaker note: A detail is the fact or example that makes a point believable. Without one, each point is just a claim. Ask on mini whiteboards or by show of hands before the reveal. -->

## Exit ticket

**A plan has an opening, two points with details and a close. The close says 'Also, the canteen has new chips.' What is wrong?**

* The close adds a new idea. A close says the main idea again and what to do; the chips belong in a point or nowhere.
