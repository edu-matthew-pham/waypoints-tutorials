- I can plan, script and cut down a short talk for a real audience.
- I can rehearse it with a partner and change one thing because of their feedback.
- I can compare what I meant my voice to do with what it did.

## Why it matters

> A talk is a few minutes of speaking and a lot of choices behind it. Can you make every one of them on purpose?

<!-- Speaker note: You will prepare a talk of about one minute for your class. Choose one change you would like to see in your school, such as the length of lunch, a new place to sit, or a club. Your listeners are your class, and your aim is for them to agree to try it. You will use what you have learned in this course: a plan with a point, a script written for the ear, cue cards, a register that suits the listeners, a voice and visuals on purpose, and feedback from a rehearsal. The site helps you plan, script and check. **You give the talk in class**, to your teacher and your classmates. Nobody here can hear it, so a partner or your teacher judges the delivery. -->

## Script: write it to be heard

In your own document, write the opening and your first point as a script. Use four or more sentences, none of them 15 words or longer. Use at least one joining word such as *because*, *when* or *until*. Choose words and a register that suit your class.

Here is one example, from Nib's lunch talk: "Who here has ever wished lunch were a bit longer? Today I want to show you how to get ten more minutes. **First**, the queue wastes time. Last week the last student was served after nine minutes. That is nine minutes of lunch gone, **because** the line is slow."

Then say your script aloud to a partner. Could you say each sentence in one breath? Did your partner follow it the first time? Does your register suit your class?

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Writing the opening and first point of the script in their own document, then saying it aloud, shows whether the script is built for the ear, which only a spoken test can tell. Hints, in order: Could you say each sentence in one breath? / Which signpost word will tell your listeners where they are? For students who need more support: The cue board's sample script, a checklist card, and a partner who listens first. Rehearse to a partner before the class. For early finishers: Give the talk a second time to a different audience, such as Year 5, and change the register and one visual. The hard part, on purpose: Students prepare well and then read the script. Ask them to put the script away before they present; the cards are the only page in their hand. When students get stuck: A student reads the script aloud, word for word, when presenting. Ask: What are the cards for? Fix: Put the script away. Cards only. Pause and look up between parts. / A student's talk runs well over the time. Ask: Which point would your listeners miss least? Fix: Cut a point or a detail. A shorter talk said well is better. / A student freezes when presenting. Ask: What is the first key word on your first card? Fix: Offer a second go, perhaps to a partner first. Do not press. -->

## Features: choose a voice and a visual

**Choose one voice setting (pace, volume, pitch or tone) and one visual for your talk. For each, say where in your talk you will use it, and why it suits your purpose and your listeners.**

* Answer: A strong answer: Voice: I will slow down and get a little louder on 'one more window', because it is the request and I want my class to catch it. Visual: a bar chart of how long the queue takes each day, shown in point one. It adds the pattern, which my words would take too long to say, and it helps my class see that the problem is real.; A different plan that also works: Voice: I will use a warm, steady tone in my opening, because I want my class to feel I am on their side. ## Rehearse and change one thing <span style="font-size:.6em;color:#64748b">(12 min)</span>

Pairs rehearse from their cards. The listener uses the checklist and writes one thing to keep and one to change. The speaker changes one thing and says it again. Then the pair swap.

<!-- Speaker note: Stop any pair whose feedback is only 'good'. Ask 'what did you hear, and where?'. -->

## Which part failed?

**A listener says, 'Your talk was well planned, but I couldn't tell what you wanted us to do.' Which part of the plan most needs fixing?**

- The opening, because it came first
- The details, because there were too few
- The close, because it should say what listeners should do
- The register, because it was too formal

* Answer: The close, because it should say what listeners should do

## Exit ticket

**Name one choice you made in your talk and say what you meant it to do for your listeners.**

* Any choice with its reason: 'I slowed down at the close so the request would be heard.'
