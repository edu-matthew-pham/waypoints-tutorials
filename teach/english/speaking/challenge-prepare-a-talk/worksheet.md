# Challenge: prepare a talk

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** A talk is a few minutes of speaking and a lot of choices behind it. Can you make every one of them on purpose?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students plan, script and cut down a one-minute talk on a change they would like to see in the school, rehearse it with a partner in class, give it in class, and check what they did against what they planned. The site plans, scripts and checks; delivery is yours to see.

**Students should be able to say:** I can plan, script and cut down a short talk for a real audience. I can rehearse it with a partner and change one thing because of their feedback. I can compare what I meant my voice to do with what it did.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Plan: opening, points, details and close

The board checks that every line has words and a label. Whether the plan suits your class is for a partner or your teacher to judge.

## Script: write it to be heard

In your own document, write the opening and your first point as a script. Use four or more sentences, none of them 15 words or longer. Use at least one joining word such as *because*, *when* or *until*. Choose words and a register that suit your class.

Here is one example, from Nib's lunch talk: "Who here has ever wished lunch were a bit longer? Today I want to show you how to get ten more minutes. **First**, the queue wastes time. Last week the last student was served after nine minutes. That is nine minutes of lunch gone, **because** the line is slow."

Then say your script aloud to a partner. Could you say each sentence in one breath? Did your partner follow it the first time? Does your register suit your class?

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?

Visual: a bar chart of how long the queue takes each day, shown in point one. It adds the pattern, which my words would take too long to say, and it helps my class see that the problem is real.; A different plan that also works: Voice: I will use a warm, steady tone in my opening, because I want my class to feel I am on their side. Visual: a simple drawing of the canteen with the spare window marked, shown in point two. It adds the layout, so listeners can picture where the window is.

There is no one right plan. What matters is that each choice is made on purpose and has a reason that links it to the listeners.

## Rehearse: with a partner, in class

Use the same checklist as in the rehearsal lesson. Your partner ticks what they heard and saw, and writes one thing to keep and one to change.

- I could hear every word, including the last sentence.
- The speaker looked up from the cards for most of the talk.
- The speaker slowed down or paused on the most important part.
- The speaker's tone matched the words.
- One thing to keep: …
- One thing to change: …

Your teacher can tell you what is allowed in your class.
