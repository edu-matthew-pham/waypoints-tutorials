# Features that shape meaning

Judging and persuading · Words that nudge

<!-- Speaker note: Students name four language features, match each to its effect, find a wrongly named feature, see how features change with audience and purpose, and write a short poster in their own document. ## Today

- I can name four language features.
- I can say what each one does to a reader.
- I can explain why features change with the audience.
- I can use features on purpose in a poster.

## Why it matters

> A poster asks, 'Would you leave a puppy out in the cold?' Does the writer really want an answer?

<!-- Speaker note: Writers choose **language features** on purpose. Four common ones: - **Speaking to the reader**: *your*, *we need you*. - **A question with an obvious answer**: *Would you leave a puppy out in the cold?* - **Repetition**: *read more, learn more, be more*. - **A strong verb**: *crushes* instead of *hurts*. Each feature does a job for the reader. You will work out which job in the Worked example. The features change with the **audience** and the **purpose**. A poster to students and a letter to the council do different jobs. -->

## Name it, then say what it does

**Match each feature to what it does.**

| | |
|---|---|
| `Speaking to 'you'` | A. Nudges the reader to agree |
| `A question with an obvious answer` | B. Makes a problem sound serious |
| `Repeating a phrase` | C. Makes the idea stick |
| `A strong verb such as strangles` | D. Makes the reader feel part of it |

* Answer: Speaking to 'you' → D. Makes the reader feel part of it; A question with an obvious answer → A. Nudges the reader to agree; Repeating a phrase → C. Makes the idea stick; A strong verb such as strangles → B. Makes a problem sound serious

<!-- Speaker note: Each feature has a different job in shaping what the reader feels or remembers. -->

## Spot the wrongly named feature

**Here is Nib explaining a feature of a poster, one point per line. One line gets the feature wrong. Click it.**

```
 1  The poster says 'Join us this Saturday.'
 2  This speaks straight to the reader.
 3  It is a question, so it asks for an answer.
 4  It makes the reader feel part of the team.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3 calls 'Join us this Saturday' a question. It is an invitation that speaks to the reader, not a question. -->

## Why do features change?

**Why would a letter to the council use fewer 'Join us!' lines than a poster for students?**

- A letter reasons with an official, while a poster rallies students
- Letters are never allowed to use any features at all
- Students are not able to follow any reasons at all

* Answer: A letter reasons with an official, while a poster rallies students

<!-- Speaker note: The purpose and audience decide the features. A poster rallies. A letter reasons. -->

## Make: a short poster

In your own document, write a poster of at least four sentences about keeping a school garden tidy. Include at least two short sentences (five words or fewer). Use at least two language features on purpose, such as a question to the reader, repetition, a direct invitation or strong verbs.

Here is one example: "Our garden needs you. **Who wants weeds?** Pick up your litter. A tidy garden grows better beans and a happier school. **Join us on Friday.**"

Then swap with a partner. Name two features in the poster and say what each does to the reader.
