# Who says so?

Judging and persuading · Warm-up: fact, opinion and evidence

<!-- Speaker note: Students see that a named source who knows the topic is stronger than a vague one, sort sources by whether they know the claim, find a vague source, and write a claim with a fitting source in their own document. About 45 minutes (Predict and Try 8 min · Worked example 12 min · Vary and Practise 10 min · Make and own writing 15 min). -->

---

## Today

- I can tell a vague source from a specific one.
- I can say whether a source knows about a claim.
- I can say why a named source persuades.
- I can write a claim and name a fitting source.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Practise board yourself: six sources, three bands. The one idea: a named source helps most when it knows about the claim. This lesson covers AC9E5LA02 node 3: why a specific, authoritative source persuades more than a general one, and judging authority in context. The terms **attribution** and **authority** are for you. The Practise board has one answer per line. 'A famous chef says the bus is usually late' is named but does not fit; a chef is not a bus expert. The Make step has no board: students write in their own document, and a partner or you check that the named source really knows about the claim. The Vary step uses gaps, not the swap board, so the board does not show the answer. All sources and people are made up for the lesson. Teacher: Try: ask 'could you look this up?' of each. -->

## Why it matters

> 'Word is the canteen is closing.' Who said so? Could you check?

<!-- Speaker note: Who says so matters. A claim is stronger when the writer names **a specific source**: a person, a record or a count that you could find. - **Vague**: *Some people say… Experts say… They say…* - **Named**: *Ms Iqbal, the pool supervisor who checks the water each morning, says…* A named source helps only if the source knows about this claim. A baker may know about bread. A baker does not know whether a bridge is safe. -->

## Vague or specific?

$$\text{Look at who is named}$$

1) $\text{Ask: could I check it?}$ — A named source can be found. A vague one cannot.
2) $\text{Ask: do they know this?}$ — A source helps only on what it knows about.

## Spot the vague source

**Here is Nib's paragraph about the reef, one sentence per line. One sentence uses a vague source. Click it.**

```
 1  Reef Watch counted 31 healthy corals at the north site.
 2  Marine biologist Dr Lina Ortiz says warm water bleaches coral.
 3  A friend of my cousin says the reef is dying fast.
 4  The reef guide's logbook records the water at 29 degrees.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3 says 'a friend of my cousin'. You cannot check who that is or what they saw. The other lines name a team, a person or a record. -->

## Why name the source?

**Why does 'Dr Ortiz, who has counted the reef for ten years, says…' persuade more than 'someone says'?**

- It is longer, so it sounds more important
- It uses a title, and titles always win arguments
- It names a person who knows the reef, so a reader can check

* Answer: It names a person who knows the reef, so a reader can check

<!-- Speaker note: A named person with a reason to know gives a reader something to check. -->

## Make: add a source

In your own document, write at least three sentences (at least 30 words in all): a claim about the school, and a named source who knows about it. Say what the source knows or has seen.

Here is one example: "The canteen queue is too long at lunch. **Ms Reid, who supervises the canteen**, says about sixty students arrive at once. She thinks a second serving window would help the queue move faster at lunch."

Then swap with a partner. Does the person you named really know about the claim? Could a reader find them and ask?
