# Who says so?

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** 'Word is the canteen is closing.' Who said so? Could you check?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students see that a named source who knows the topic is stronger than a vague one, sort sources by whether they know the claim, find a vague source, and write a claim with a fitting source in their own document.

**Students should be able to say:** I can tell a vague source from a specific one. I can say whether a source knows about a claim. I can say why a named source persuades. I can write a claim and name a fitting source.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: does the source know?


:::keep-together

### Vague or specific?

1. $\text{Look at who is named}$ — A person, a record or a count, or 'some people'?
2. $\text{Ask: could I check it?}$ — A named source can be found. A vague one cannot.
3. $\text{Ask: do they know this?}$ — A source helps only on what it knows about.

**Does the source know about this claim?**

Groups: Knows about it, Does not know about it

- A swimming coach says goggles help young swimmers see the wall.: {{field-15}}
- A famous singer says this cough medicine works.: {{field-15}}
- A footballer says this phone is the fastest.: {{field-15}}
- A bus driver says the 8:10 bus is often full.: {{field-15}}
- A chef says the bridge is safe to cross.: {{field-15}}
- A librarian says this book is a good start for mystery readers.: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** A swimming coach says goggles help young swimmers see the wall. → Knows about it; A famous singer says this cough medicine works. → Does not know about it; A footballer says this phone is the fastest. → Does not know about it; A bus driver says the 8:10 bus is often full. → Knows about it; A chef says the bridge is safe to cross. → Does not know about it; A librarian says this book is a good start for mystery readers. → Knows about it

:::

### Spot the vague source

**Here is Nib's paragraph about the reef, one sentence per line. One sentence uses a vague source. Click it.**

```
 1  Reef Watch counted 31 healthy corals at the north site.
 2  Marine biologist Dr Lina Ortiz says warm water bleaches coral.
 3  A friend of my cousin says the reef is dying fast.
 4  The reef guide's logbook records the water at 29 degrees.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3 says 'a friend of my cousin'. You cannot check who that is or what they saw. The other lines name a team, a person or a record.

:::


:::keep-together

### Why name the source?

**Why does 'Dr Ortiz, who has counted the reef for ten years, says…' persuade more than 'someone says'?**

☐ It is longer, so it sounds more important

☐ It uses a title, and titles always win arguments

☐ It names a person who knows the reef, so a reader can check

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** It names a person who knows the reef, so a reader can check

A named person with a reason to know gives a reader something to check.

:::

## Make: add a source

In your own document, write at least three sentences (at least 30 words in all): a claim about the school, and a named source who knows about it. Say what the source knows or has seen.

Here is one example: "The canteen queue is too long at lunch. **Ms Reid, who supervises the canteen**, says about sixty students arrive at once. She thinks a second serving window would help the queue move faster at lunch."

Then swap with a partner. Does the person you named really know about the claim? Could a reader find them and ask?

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?
