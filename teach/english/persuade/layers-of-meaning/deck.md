# Layers of meaning

Judging and persuading · Words that nudge

<!-- Speaker note: Students name irony, exaggeration and understatement, separate irony from sarcasm, find a flipped effect, label new lines, and write a short scene with two devices in their own document. - I can name irony, exaggeration and understatement.
- I can say what extra meaning a device adds.
- I can tell irony from sarcasm.
- I can use a device in a short scene.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Try board yourself: six lines, three devices. The one idea: a device says one thing and means another, and the gap is the extra layer. This lesson covers AC9E8LA02 nodes 1 and 2: rhetorical devices (irony, hyperbole, understatement) and how they create meaning beyond the literal. The terms **irony**, **hyperbole** and **understatement** are used in the lesson because they are the metalanguage in node 2. Kit's hinge: students who conflate irony with sarcasm, or cannot identify understatement, cannot explain layers of meaning. The sort and the bug task return to it. Irony is taught as the opposite of what is said or expected: verbal irony in the Try lines, and simple situational irony in the sarcasm sort. Years 9 and 10 extend it. Each Try and Practise line has one defensible device. 'I'd die for a sandwich right now' is an everyday exaggeration. The Make step has no board: students write the scene in their own document, and partners name the device in each spoken line and what it adds. All lines are made up. Teacher: Try: ask students to say the plain version aloud. -->

## Why it matters

> 'Super speedy,' said Dad, watching the snail cross the path. Does he mean it?

<!-- Speaker note: Some writing says one thing and means another. Three devices do this: - **Irony**: saying the opposite of what is meant, so the reader sees the gap. *'Super speedy,' said Dad, watching the snail cross the path.* - **Hyperbole**: exaggerating. *The soup was hot enough to melt the sun.* - **Understatement**: playing down. *'Not my best day,' said the pilot, as the plane lost a wing.* Each adds a layer beyond the literal words. **Sarcasm** is a sharp kind of irony used to mock someone. Irony does not have to mock anyone, and it can be a situation that turns out the opposite of what you expect. -->

## Which device, and what layer?

**Match each device to what it does.**

| | |
|---|---|
| `Hyperbole (exaggeration)` | A. Makes a big thing sound small |
| `Understatement (playing down)` | B. Irony used to mock someone |
| `Irony (the opposite of what is said or expected)` | C. Shows the reverse of the surface meaning |
| `Sarcasm` | D. Makes a small thing feel huge |

* Answer: Hyperbole (exaggeration) → D. Makes a small thing feel huge; Understatement (playing down) → A. Makes a big thing sound small; Irony (the opposite of what is said or expected) → C. Shows the reverse of the surface meaning; Sarcasm → B. Irony used to mock someone

## Spot the flipped effect

**Here is Nib's explanation of a line, one point per line. One point gets the effect wrong. Click it.**

```
 1  The captain says, 'A small hiccup,' as the engine explodes.
 2  This is understatement.
 3  It makes the problem sound huge.
 4  The captain stays calm, which is funny.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3 says understatement makes the problem sound huge. It does the opposite: it plays the problem down, which is why the calm captain is funny. -->

## Make: a scene with devices

In your own document, write a short scene of at least four sentences. Include two spoken lines in quotation marks: one that plays something down (understatement) and one that blows something up (exaggeration).

Here is one example: The bus broke down on the hill. "**A minor delay**," said the driver, as smoke poured from the engine. "**We will be here until the end of time**," groaned Mia. The sun set slowly over the stopped bus.

Then swap with a partner. Name the device in each spoken line and say what it adds to the scene.
