# Spot the bias

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** 'The coach strolled in.' 'The coach slunk in.' Same person, same door. Which writer likes the coach?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students see that bias is word choice that favours one side, label sentences by the side they favour, find a biased line in a report that names both sides, choose plain wording, and write a fair report in their own document.

**Students should be able to say:** I can name the word that takes a side. I can say that a text can be biased without false facts. I can say that naming both sides is not enough. I can write a report that stays fair.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: whose side is the word on?


:::keep-together

### Whose side is the word on?

1. $\text{Find the word that judges}$ — Praise or blame, not just a fact.
2. $\text{Ask: who benefits?}$ — Which side looks better because of it?
3. $\text{Try a plain version}$ — Could the same fact be said without a judging word?

**Which side does each phrase favour?**

Groups: Favours the council, Favours the students

- The sensible rule protects learning.: {{field-15}}
- The council showed leadership.: {{field-15}}
- The harsh rule punishes everyone.: {{field-15}}
- A fair decision at last.: {{field-15}}
- The council ignored student voices.: {{field-15}}
- A heavy-handed decision.: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** The sensible rule protects learning. → Favours the council; The council showed leadership. → Favours the council; The harsh rule punishes everyone. → Favours the students; A fair decision at last. → Favours the council; The council ignored student voices. → Favours the students; A heavy-handed decision. → Favours the students

:::


:::keep-together

### Spot the line that takes a side

**Nib says this report is balanced because it names both sides. One sentence still takes a side. Click it.**

```
 1  The council met to discuss the phone rule.
 2  Parents spoke for and against the rule.
 3  Reasonable parents backed the rule, while others complained noisily.
 4  The vote was 6 to 3.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3 names parents on both sides, but 'reasonable' praises one group and 'complained noisily' criticises the other. Naming both sides is not enough.

:::


:::keep-together

### Same fact, different lean

**Two reports give the same number, 40 people. One says 'a mere 40' and one says 'a passionate 40'. What does this show?**

☐ One report has the wrong number

☐ The same fact can be worded to favour different sides

☐ Numbers cannot be biased

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** The same fact can be worded to favour different sides

Mere makes 40 sound small. Passionate makes 40 sound strong. The fact is the same.

:::

## Make: a fair report

In your own document, write at least four sentences reporting a disagreement about the school canteen. Name both sides, use plain words that take no side, and use no exclamation marks.

Here is one example: "Some students want the canteen to open earlier. They say many arrive hungry. The canteen manager says there is not enough staff before nine. The school council will discuss both views next week."

Then swap with a partner. Circle any word that takes a side. Does each side get a fair say?

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?
