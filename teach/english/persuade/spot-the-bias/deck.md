# Spot the bias

Judging and persuading · Words that nudge

<!-- Speaker note: Students see that bias is word choice that favours one side, label sentences by the side they favour, find a biased line in a report that names both sides, choose plain wording, and write a fair report in their own document. About 45 minutes (Predict and Try 8 min · Worked example 12 min · Vary and Practise 10 min · Make and own writing 15 min). -->

---

## Today

- I can name the word that takes a side.
- I can say that a text can be biased without false facts.
- I can say that naming both sides is not enough.
- I can write a report that stays fair.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Try board yourself: six sentences, three labels. The one idea: bias lives in word choices, and true facts can lean. ## Why it matters

> 'The coach strolled in.' 'The coach slunk in.' Same person, same door. Which writer likes the coach?

<!-- Speaker note: **Bias** means language that favours one side. A text can be biased even when every fact in it is true, because the **word choices** decide who looks good. A text can talk about several groups, so look at the words used for each one. To weigh a text, ask: which words favour whom? A fair report picks plain words for every side. -->

## Whose side is the word on?

$$\text{Find the word that judges}$$

1) $\text{Ask: who benefits?}$ — Which side looks better because of it?
2) $\text{Try a plain version}$ — Could the same fact be said without a judging word?

## Spot the line that takes a side

**Nib says this report is balanced because it names both sides. One sentence still takes a side. Click it.**

```
 1  The council met to discuss the phone rule.
 2  Parents spoke for and against the rule.
 3  Reasonable parents backed the rule, while others complained noisily.
 4  The vote was 6 to 3.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3 names parents on both sides, but 'reasonable' praises one group and 'complained noisily' criticises the other. Naming both sides is not enough. -->

## Same fact, different lean

**Two reports give the same number, 40 people. One says 'a mere 40' and one says 'a passionate 40'. What does this show?**

- One report has the wrong number
- The same fact can be worded to favour different sides
- Numbers cannot be biased

* Answer: The same fact can be worded to favour different sides

<!-- Speaker note: Mere makes 40 sound small. Passionate makes 40 sound strong. The fact is the same. -->

## Make: a fair report

In your own document, write at least four sentences reporting a disagreement about the school canteen. Name both sides, use plain words that take no side, and use no exclamation marks.

Here is one example: "Some students want the canteen to open earlier. They say many arrive hungry. The canteen manager says there is not enough staff before nine. The school council will discuss both views next week."

Then swap with a partner. Circle any word that takes a side. Does each side get a fair say?

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Writing a fair report that names both sides puts the idea to use. Hints, in order: What did each side want? / Which plain words describe it? -->
