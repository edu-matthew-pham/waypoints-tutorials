# Who is it for?

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Two messages ask for the same favour, one to a cousin and one to a stranger who runs the hall. Would the words match?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn that familiarity (how well you know someone) and power (their authority) both shape how formal to be. They place situations on a scale, sort cues, find reasoning that ignores power, choose an email opening, choose greetings and write three openings.

**Students should be able to say:** I can say how well I know someone and whether they have authority. I can choose words that fit both. I can explain why a coach I know well still gets respectful words. I can write openings for three different readers.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: familiarity or power?


:::keep-together

### Familiarity or power?

1. $\text{Ask: how well do I know them?}$ — Close means more relaxed words.
2. $\text{Ask: who decides?}$ — The one with authority gets respect.
3. $\text{Combine them}$ — A warm request can be both relaxed and respectful.

**Is each cue about familiarity (how well you know them) or power (their authority)?**

Groups: Familiarity, Power

- They are your best friend.: {{field-15}}
- They are the principal.: {{field-15}}
- You have never met them.: {{field-15}}
- They mark your work.: {{field-15}}
- You have known them for years.: {{field-15}}
- They decide who joins the team.: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** They are your best friend. → Familiarity; They are the principal. → Power; You have never met them. → Familiarity; They mark your work. → Power; You have known them for years. → Familiarity; They decide who joins the team. → Power

:::

### Spot the missing step

**Here is Nib's reasoning before writing to Ms Rossi, one step at a time. In one step Nib decides how to word the request using only one cue. Click it.**

```
 1  I need an extension on my history essay.
 2  I know Ms Rossi really well, and we chat about footy every lunchtime.
 3  So I will word my request exactly as I would for a friend.
 4  I will write my message now.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Step 3 decides using only how well Nib knows Ms Rossi (familiarity). She also has power over Nib's extension, so the words need to be respectful too.

### Choose the opening

**Ava has played for her coach for three years and they joke often. She emails him to ask to miss training. Which opening fits?**

☐ Hey Coach, I'm skipping Thursday.

☐ Hi Coach, I hope you're well. Could I please miss training on Thursday?

☐ Dear Mr Wilson, I respectfully request permission to be absent from Thursday's training session.

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Hi Coach, I hope you're well. Could I please miss training on Thursday?

Warm because Ava knows the coach well, and a request because the coach has the authority to say yes or no.

## Make: three openings

In your own document, write three openings for three readers: a friend, a coach you know well and the principal. Write two sentences for each, at least six sentences in all. Think about how well you know each person and whether they have authority.

Here is one example, for a friend: "Hey Sam, are you free after school? I found a good spot for the project." Your coach and the principal need different words.

Then swap with a partner. For each opening, say how well the writer knows the person and whether the person has authority.

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?
