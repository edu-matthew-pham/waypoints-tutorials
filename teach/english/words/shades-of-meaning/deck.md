# Shades of meaning

Language for people and situations · Warm-up: same message, different words

<!-- Speaker note: Students learn that synonyms are not interchangeable: they sit at different strengths and carry different feelings, and an exact or specialist word says more than a general one. They place words on a scale, sort synonym and antonym pairs, catch a word with the wrong shade, choose words that match what happened and write a description. About 45 minutes (Predict and Try 8 min · Worked example 12 min · Vary and Practise 10 min · Make and own writing 15 min). -->

## Today

- I can tell synonyms from antonyms.
- I can say how two similar words differ in strength or feeling.
- I can choose an exact word instead of a plain one.
- I can explain what a specialist word adds.

## Why it matters

> 'Ate' and 'gobbled' both mean eating. Would you say your dinner was gobbled at a polite dinner party?

<!-- Speaker note: **Synonyms** are words with a similar meaning. **Antonyms** are words with opposite meanings. Synonyms are rarely identical. *Ate*, *nibbled* and *gobbled* all mean eating, but each pictures a different way of eating. The shade of meaning is part of what the writer says. An **exact word** or a **specialist word** can say more than a general one. *Bird* is general; *kookaburra* is exact. -->

## Synonym or antonym?

$$\text{Compare the two words}$$

1) $\text{If similar}$ — Check the strength and the feeling. Are they interchangeable?
2) $\text{If opposite}$ — The pair makes a contrast that sharpens meaning.

Groups: Synonyms (similar meaning) · Antonyms (opposite meaning)

happy / glad · happy / miserable · begin / start · begin / finish · brave / cowardly · huge / enormous

* Answer: happy / glad → Synonyms (similar meaning); happy / miserable → Antonyms (opposite meaning); begin / start → Synonyms (similar meaning); begin / finish → Antonyms (opposite meaning); brave / cowardly → Antonyms (opposite meaning); huge / enormous → Synonyms (similar meaning)

## Spot the word with the wrong shade

**Here is Nib's lake diary, one sentence per line. One line uses a word whose shade does not fit. Click it.**

```
 1  The class walked to the lake.
 2  A gentle breeze drifted across the water.
 3  Mia shrieked softly to Ben.
 4  We ate our lunch under a tree.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3 has 'shrieked softly'. Shrieked means a loud, sharp cry, so it cannot be soft. Nib swapped in a synonym for 'said' without checking its shade. -->

## What does the exact word add?

**A science note says: 'The reptile basked on a warm rock.' What does 'reptile' tell the reader that 'animal' does not?**

- It means the animal is dangerous
- It names a particular group of animals
- It is just a harder word for the same thing

* Answer: It names a particular group of animals

<!-- Speaker note: Reptile narrows the animal to a particular group, so the reader knows more. Specialist words add precision. -->

## Make: describe a stormy day

In your own document, write a description of a stormy day, at least four sentences and about 40 words. Picture it at a beach. Choose exact words for what you see, hear and feel, and swap any plain word such as *big* or *bad* for a stronger, more exact one.

Here is one example: "The storm **roared** over the bay, and the waves **crashed** against the jetty. A **fierce** wind **whipped** the sand into our faces."

Then swap with a partner. Point to three exact words and say what each adds that a plainer word would not.

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Describing a stormy day with exact words puts shades of meaning to use in a real piece of writing. Hints, in order: Picture the storm at a beach. What do you see, hear and feel? / Where could you swap a plain word like 'big' or 'bad' for an exact one? -->

## Exit ticket

**Which is stronger, 'upset' or 'devastated'? Say how you know.**

* Devastated is stronger. Upset is a mild or medium feeling; devastated is the strongest of the two.
