# Shades of meaning

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** 'Ate' and 'gobbled' both mean eating. Would you say your dinner was gobbled at a polite dinner party?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn that synonyms are not interchangeable: they sit at different strengths and carry different feelings, and an exact or specialist word says more than a general one. They place words on a scale, sort synonym and antonym pairs, catch a word with the wrong shade, choose words that match what happened and write a description.

**Students should be able to say:** I can tell synonyms from antonyms. I can say how two similar words differ in strength or feeling. I can choose an exact word instead of a plain one. I can explain what a specialist word adds.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: similar or opposite, and how strong?


:::keep-together

### Synonym or antonym?

1. $\text{Compare the two words}$ — Same direction of meaning, or opposite?
2. $\text{If similar}$ — Check the strength and the feeling. Are they interchangeable?
3. $\text{If opposite}$ — The pair makes a contrast that sharpens meaning.

**Is each pair of words synonyms or antonyms?**

Groups: Synonyms (similar meaning), Antonyms (opposite meaning)

- happy / glad: {{field-15}}
- happy / miserable: {{field-15}}
- begin / start: {{field-15}}
- begin / finish: {{field-15}}
- brave / cowardly: {{field-15}}
- huge / enormous: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** happy / glad → Synonyms (similar meaning); happy / miserable → Antonyms (opposite meaning); begin / start → Synonyms (similar meaning); begin / finish → Antonyms (opposite meaning); brave / cowardly → Antonyms (opposite meaning); huge / enormous → Synonyms (similar meaning)

### Spot the word with the wrong shade

**Here is Nib's lake diary, one sentence per line. One line uses a word whose shade does not fit. Click it.**

```
 1  The class walked to the lake.
 2  A gentle breeze drifted across the water.
 3  Mia shrieked softly to Ben.
 4  We ate our lunch under a tree.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3 has 'shrieked softly'. Shrieked means a loud, sharp cry, so it cannot be soft. Nib swapped in a synonym for 'said' without checking its shade.

### What does the exact word add?

**A science note says: 'The reptile basked on a warm rock.' What does 'reptile' tell the reader that 'animal' does not?**

☐ It means the animal is dangerous

☐ It names a particular group of animals

☐ It is just a harder word for the same thing

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** It names a particular group of animals

Reptile narrows the animal to a particular group, so the reader knows more. Specialist words add precision.

## Make: describe a stormy day

In your own document, write a description of a stormy day, at least four sentences and about 40 words. Picture it at a beach. Choose exact words for what you see, hear and feel, and swap any plain word such as *big* or *bad* for a stronger, more exact one.

Here is one example: "The storm **roared** over the bay, and the waves **crashed** against the jetty. A **fierce** wind **whipped** the sand into our faces."

Then swap with a partner. Point to three exact words and say what each adds that a plainer word would not.
