# Same message, different words

Language for people and situations · Warm-up: same message, different words

<!-- Speaker note: Students learn that the same message can be worded in many ways and that the words that fit depend on the reader and the purpose. They place ways of asking on a scale, sort messages by reader, spot wording pitched for the wrong reader, choose openings, choose inclusive wording and write two messages about one event. About 45 minutes (Predict and Try 8 min · Worked example 12 min · Vary and Practise 10 min · Make and own writing 15 min). -->

---

## Today

- I can tell informal wording from formal wording.
- I can choose words that fit who is reading.
- I can explain why formal is not simply more correct.
- I can choose wording that includes every reader.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Try board yourself: six ways to ask for a pen, three bands. The one idea: the words that fit depend on the reader and the purpose. This lesson covers AC9E4LA01 nodes 1 to 3: register and formality, factors that shape language choices (who is reading, how well they know each other), and inclusive language. The term **register** is named here for you; the lesson says 'how formal the words are'. Kit's hinge for node 1: students who treat formal as 'more correct' go wrong later. The Predict, the sort and the bug task all return to this. Node 3 criteria: identify a non-inclusive phrasing, suggest an alternative with a reason, and see inclusion as a choice about the reader, not just a rule. The Practise board names the choice. ## Why it matters

> You text your friend 'On my way!' Would you send those words to the principal?

<!-- Speaker note: The same message can be said in many ways. Which words fit depends on **who is reading** and **what the message is for**: - **Informal** words suit people you know well: *Thanks, mate!* They are short and relaxed. - **Formal** words suit a reader you do not know well, or who has authority: *Thank you for your help.* They are careful and complete. Which words fit depends on the reader and the job. **Inclusive** wording is a choice to make every reader feel the message is for them. -->

## Predict

**Max texts a friend, 'Cheers for the lift!' and writes to a guest speaker, 'Thank you for visiting our school.' Is either one wrong?**

- Both are wrong, because a message should read the same for everyone
- The letter is wrong, because it is too stiff for a school
- Neither is wrong: each fits the person who reads it
- The text is wrong, because it is not formal

<!-- Speaker note: What happens: Neither is wrong. 'Cheers for the lift!' suits a friend, and 'Thank you for visiting our school.' suits a guest. ## Spot the words for the wrong reader

**Here is Nib's request to the principal, one sentence per line. One line is pitched for a friend, not the principal. Click it.**

```
 1  Dear Ms Tran,
 2  I am writing to ask whether our class may use the hall on Friday.
 3  We reckon it'll be heaps good for everyone.
 4  Thank you for considering my request.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3 uses 'We reckon it'll be heaps good', which is relaxed wording for a friend. The other lines are careful and complete. Nib wrote the same way to everyone. -->

## Choose the opening

**Nib wants to ask the mayor for a new bin in the park. Which opening fits best?**

- Mayor Rossi, you need to put a bin in Elm Park now.
- Hey Mayor, put a bin in the park.
- Dear Mayor Rossi, I am writing to ask for a bin in Elm Park.

* Answer: Dear Mayor Rossi, I am writing to ask for a bin in Elm Park.

<!-- Speaker note: Nib is writing to someone with authority whom Nib does not know. Careful words and a polite request fit that reader and that job. -->

## Make: two messages about one event

In your own document, pick one event, such as an excursion or a match. Write two short messages about it: a text to a friend and a note to a teacher. Write at least four sentences in all. Use at least one short form, such as *I'm* or *can't*, in the text to your friend.

Here is one example: "Hey Zoe, our excursion to the museum was awesome! We **didn't** even have to wait in line." and "Dear Mr Lee, thank you for organising the excursion to the museum. The class learned a great deal about the dinosaur display."

Then swap with a partner. Point to the words that fit each reader. What changed between the two messages?

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Writing a text to a friend and a note to a teacher about the same event puts two registers to use in one task. Hints, in order: Pick one event, such as an excursion or a match. / Write the message to your friend first, then the note to your teacher. How do the words change? -->
