# Words and roles

Language for people and situations · Warm-up: same message, different words

<!-- Speaker note: Students learn that words signal roles and relationships: orders, requests and shared ideas show who has authority. They place ways of asking on a five-step formality scale, sort lines by speaker, spot an order to someone in charge, choose words for a favour, and write a request to the principal. About 45 minutes (Predict and Try 8 min · Worked example 12 min · Vary and Practise 10 min · Make and own writing 15 min). -->

## Why it matters

> A teacher says 'Take out your books.' A student says 'May I take out my book?' What do the words tell you about who is in charge?

<!-- Speaker note: Our words do more than carry a message. They also show **our role** and **our relationship** to the person we are talking to. - A person in charge often gives **orders**: *Stand behind the line.* - A person asking for something often makes a **request**: *Could I please leave early?* - A person working alongside others often **shares an idea**: *Shall we start with the map?* Formality is a **scale**, not a switch. Between the most casual words and the most formal words there are many steps. To choose words, think about three things: **who** is reading, **what** the message is for, and **where** it will be read. -->

## Predict

**A coach says, 'Everyone, run two laps.' A player says, 'Coach, could we please stop for a drink?' What do the words tell you?**

- The coach gives orders and the player asks, so the coach is in charge
- The player is in charge, because the player sounds more polite
- Neither is in charge, because they are both talking
- The coach and player are close friends, because they are chatting

## Choose the opening for the job

**Nib wants to ask the librarian if the club can use the quiet room. Which opening fits best?**

- Give us the quiet room on Thursday.
- Hey Mrs Ng, the quiet room's ours on Thursday, yeah?
- Hi Mrs Ng, could our club please use the quiet room on Thursday?

* Answer: Hi Mrs Ng, could our club please use the quiet room on Thursday?

## Make: ask the principal

In your own document, write an email to the principal asking for something, such as a lunchtime club or a change to a rule. Write at least four sentences. Use full forms, such as *do not* and not *don't*, and leave out exclamation marks. Say what you want and why the principal might agree.

Here is one example: "Dear Ms Tran, I am writing to ask whether our class may start a chess club at lunchtime. We would meet in the library on Tuesdays. Thank you for considering this request."

Then swap with a partner. Find the word or phrase that makes it a request and not an order.

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Writing an email that asks the principal for something puts the words for a person in charge to use. Hints, in order: What do you want to ask for, and why would the principal agree? / How would you word it so it sounds like a request, not an order? -->

## Exit ticket

**Which role does 'Please sit down.' signal, and which does 'May I sit down?' signal?**

* 'Please sit down.' signals the person in charge, giving an instruction. 'May I sit down?' signals the person asking for permission.
