# Words and roles

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** A teacher says 'Take out your books.' A student says 'May I take out my book?' What do the words tell you about who is in charge?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn that words signal roles and relationships: orders, requests and shared ideas show who has authority. They place ways of asking on a five-step formality scale, sort lines by speaker, spot an order to someone in charge, choose words for a favour, and write a request to the principal.

**Students should be able to say:** I can show that formality is a scale, not a switch. I can say what orders, requests and titles show about roles. I can name three things that decide which words to use. I can write a request that fits the person in charge.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: who is speaking?


:::keep-together

### Who is speaking?

1. $\text{Look at the verbs}$ — Is it an order, a request, or an idea to share?
2. $\text{Look at the names}$ — Does anyone use a title, a first name or no name?
3. $\text{Decide the role}$ — Who has the authority, and who is asking?

**Who is probably speaking: a teacher or a student?**

### Spot the order to the coach

**Here is Nib's message to the coach, one line at a time. One line gives the coach an order. Click it.**

```
 1  Hi Coach,
 2  Thanks for the extra training on Wednesday.
 3  Tell me the time of Saturday's match.
 4  See you there.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3 says 'Tell me the time', which is an order. Nib is asking the coach, so a request fits: 'Could you tell me the time of Saturday's match?'

:::


:::keep-together

### Choose the opening for the job

**Nib wants to ask the librarian if the club can use the quiet room. Which opening fits best?**

☐ Give us the quiet room on Thursday.

☐ Hey Mrs Ng, the quiet room's ours on Thursday, yeah?

☐ Hi Mrs Ng, could our club please use the quiet room on Thursday?

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Hi Mrs Ng, could our club please use the quiet room on Thursday?

Three things decide the words: the reader (the librarian, who is in charge of the room), the purpose (asking for something) and the place (a message the librarian will read). A polite request fits all three.

:::

## Make: ask the principal

In your own document, write an email to the principal asking for something, such as a lunchtime club or a change to a rule. Write at least four sentences. Use full forms, such as *do not* and not *don't*, and leave out exclamation marks. Say what you want and why the principal might agree.

Here is one example: "Dear Ms Tran, I am writing to ask whether our class may start a chess club at lunchtime. We would meet in the library on Tuesdays. Thank you for considering this request."

Then swap with a partner. Find the word or phrase that makes it a request and not an order.
