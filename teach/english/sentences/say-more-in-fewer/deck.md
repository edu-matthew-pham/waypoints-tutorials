# Say more in fewer words

Building sentences · Shaping sentences

<!-- Speaker note: Students fold two choppy sentences about the same thing into one by turning the second into an embedded clause with who, which or that. ## Today

- I can fold a second sentence into the first with who, which or that.
- I can say which noun an embedded clause tells about.
- I can say what is lost if the clause is taken out.
- I can write a review that carries its details in embedded clauses.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Try board yourself: three pairs, three folded forms. The one idea: an embedded clause tells about a noun, sits right after it, and lets a writer say more in fewer words. This lesson covers AC9E8LA05 nodes 1 and 2 (what embedded clauses are and how they function; embedded clauses expanding ideas). An **embedded clause** (also called a relative clause) is a dependent clause inside a noun group, starting with who, which, that or whose. Lesson 5 introduced the idea; this lesson uses it to tighten writing, so concision is the focus. Commas: a clause that adds extra detail takes commas (My sister, who plays the cello, practises daily); a clause that says which one needs none (The student who won smiled). The boards treat only clear cases. Punctuation for meaning teaches the choice. The sentences are made up for the lesson. Teacher: Try: ask which noun each folded clause tells about. Teacher: Worked example: ask which fact would disappear if the clause went. -->

## Why it matters

> 'The pool is old. The pool is closed.' Can you say both in one sentence without repeating 'the pool'?

<!-- Speaker note: Two short sentences about the same thing can fold into one. - **Before:** *The cafe sells hot pies. The cafe closes at four.* - **After:** *The cafe, which sells hot pies, closes at four.* The second sentence becomes an **embedded clause** (*which sells hot pies*). It sits right after the noun it tells about. Take it out and the reader loses a fact. -->

## Fold in three steps

$$\text{Find the shared noun}$$

1) $\text{Fold the second in}$ — Put it right after the noun, starting with who, which or that.
2) $\text{Check it}$ — Is the noun named once? Are both facts still there?

## Fold in three steps

**Match each embedded clause to the noun it tells about.**

| | |
|---|---|
| `The cod that Mia caught was huge.` | A. cousin |
| `My cousin, who lives in Perth, visits in June.` | B. student |
| `The oval, which floods every winter, is closed.` | C. oval |
| `The student who won the prize smiled.` | D. cod |

* Answer: The cod that Mia caught was huge. → D. cod; My cousin, who lives in Perth, visits in June. → A. cousin; The oval, which floods every winter, is closed. → C. oval; The student who won the prize smiled. → B. student

<!-- Speaker note: An embedded clause sits right after the noun it tells about, so the noun is the word just before the joining word. -->

## Make: a review

In your own document, write a short review of a book, game or place. Write at least four sentences. Say what it is, then fold details in with *who*, *which* or *whose* at least twice. Fold two sentences into one wherever you repeat a noun.

Here is one example: "The Lost Lighthouse is a book that I could not put down. Its hero, **who is only twelve**, finds a map **which leads to a hidden cave**. The cave, **whose walls glitter**, holds the secret of the wrecked ship."

Then swap with a partner. Underline each embedded clause and the noun it follows. Does each clause sit right next to the noun it tells about?

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Writing a short review that folds details into embedded clauses puts the move to work in a real text type. Hints, in order: Review a book, game or place.
