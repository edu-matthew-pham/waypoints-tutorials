# Simple, compound, complex

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Is a long sentence always a complicated one?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn the four sentence shapes: simple, compound, complex and compound-complex. They label whole sentences, sort sentences, catch a wrong label, build a compound-complex sentence, colour dependent clauses and write a short note home in their own document.

**Students should be able to say:** I can find the main and dependent clauses in a sentence. I can name a sentence as simple, compound, complex or compound-complex. I can build a sentence of each shape. I can use a mix of shapes in a short note.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: how the shapes differ


:::keep-together

### Four steps to name a shape

1. $\text{Count the verbs}$ — Each verb belongs to a clause. How many are there?
2. $\text{Test each clause}$ — Could it stand alone as a sentence? Then it is a main clause.
3. $\text{Name the shape}$ — One main is simple, two mains compound, a main with a dependent complex, two mains with a dependent compound-complex.

**Sort each sentence by its shape.**

Groups: Simple, Compound, Complex

- The tide came in.: {{field-15}}
- The tide came in, so we left.: {{field-15}}
- We left because the tide came in.: {{field-15}}
- Ava swam and Ben watched.: {{field-15}}
- Ben watched while Ava swam.: {{field-15}}
- Everyone cheered.: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** The tide came in. → Simple; The tide came in, so we left. → Compound; We left because the tide came in. → Complex; Ava swam and Ben watched. → Compound; Ben watched while Ava swam. → Complex; Everyone cheered. → Simple

:::


:::keep-together

### Count the clauses

**Nib labelled each sentence on its own line. One label is wrong. Click the line with the wrong label.**

```
 1  The ferry sailed. (simple)
 2  The ferry sailed, but the storm grew. (compound)
 3  We waited because the storm grew. (compound)
 4  Mia rowed and Ravi steered. (compound)
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3 is complex, not compound: 'because the storm grew' is a dependent clause, not a second main clause.

:::


:::keep-together

### Which shape?

**'The dog barked, and the cat ran.' Why is this compound and not complex?**

☐ It is a long sentence

☐ Both parts could stand alone as sentences

☐ It has a comma

☐ It has the word and

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Both parts could stand alone as sentences

Both 'the dog barked' and 'the cat ran' could stand alone, so they are both main clauses. Two main clauses make a compound sentence.

## Make: a note home

In your own document, write a short note to a parent about a school event. Write at least four sentences. Include at least one compound sentence and one complex sentence. Try one compound-complex sentence for the most important news.

Here is one example: "On Friday our class visited the museum. We left early **because** the bus came at eight. The guide showed us a model ship, **and** she explained how it sailed **when** the wind was strong."

Then swap with a partner. Label each sentence simple, compound, complex or compound-complex. Do you agree with each label?

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?
