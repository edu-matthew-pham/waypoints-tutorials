# Build a noun group

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** 'The racket' or 'our rusty old tennis racket'. Which one could you draw?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn that a noun group is a noun and the words that tell more about it, in the order determiner, describer, classifier, head noun. They label three noun groups, match each part to its job, catch a noun group too thin to picture, ask what a longer one does, build a sentence around one, colour describers and classifiers, and write four sentences of an information report in their own document.

**Students should be able to say:** I can find the head noun of a noun group. I can name the determiner, describer and classifier, and put them in order. I can tell when a noun group is too thin and rebuild it. I can use precise noun groups in an information report.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: what does each part do?


:::keep-together

### Noun group parts

1. $\text{Find the head noun}$ — What is the main thing? Everything else tells you more about it.
2. $\text{Check the order}$ — Determiner, describer, classifier, head noun.
3. $\text{Ask what it lets you see}$ — Does the group help the reader picture exactly what is meant?

Now match the parts.

**Match each set of words to the part of a noun group they are.**

A. Describer: what it is like

B. Head noun

C. Classifier: what kind it is

D. Determiner: points out or counts

{{columns 60% 40%}}

{{rows 1}}

| Part | Letter |
|---|---|
| `the, a, my, those` | |
| `tiny, shiny, cracked` | |
| `paper, kitchen, sports` | |
| `The word all the others tell you more about` | |

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** the, a, my, those → D. Determiner: points out or counts; tiny, shiny, cracked → A. Describer: what it is like; paper, kitchen, sports → C. Classifier: what kind it is; The word all the others tell you more about → B. Head noun

Each part has its own job. A determiner points out the thing, a describer says what it is like, a classifier says what kind, and the head noun is the thing itself.

:::

### Find the thin noun group

**Here is Nib's information report, one sentence per line. One line has a noun group so thin that you cannot picture what it names. Click it.**

```
 1  The platypus is a small, shy animal.
 2  It finds food with a thing on its face.
 3  Its dense brown fur keeps it warm in cold water.
 4  It lays eggs in a riverbank burrow.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 2

Line 2: 'a thing on its face' could be anything. The report needs a precise noun group, such as 'its soft rubbery bill'.

:::


:::keep-together

### What does the longer group do?

**A hiking shop writes 'our lightweight waterproof hiking backpack' instead of 'our bag'. What does the longer noun group do?**

☐ Changes the head noun into a describer

☐ Makes the sentence shorter

☐ Tells the buyer exactly what it is and why it suits them

☐ Means the sentence no longer needs a verb

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Tells the buyer exactly what it is and why it suits them

Lightweight and waterproof are describers, and hiking is a classifier. Together they let the buyer picture exactly what they are getting.

## Describers and classifiers


## Make: a report with noun groups

In your own document, write four sentences of an information report about an animal, a place or an object. Make it at least 40 words. Build each noun group with a determiner, at least one describer or classifier, and a head noun.

Here is one example: "The Murray cod is **a large freshwater fish**. It lives in **slow, muddy rivers** across the southern inland. **Its broad, mottled body** helps it hide among fallen logs."

Then swap with a partner. Circle each noun group in each other's report. Does each one have a head noun and at least one describer or classifier?

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?
