# A clause inside a clause

Building sentences · Detail inside a sentence

<!-- Speaker note: Students learn that an embedded clause sits inside a main clause, straight after the noun it tells about, and starts with who, which, that or whose. They label five sentences, sort sentences by clause type, catch an embedded clause that drifted from its noun, choose the construction that keeps the main news main, build a sentence with one, colour embedded clauses and write a short biography in their own document. About 50 minutes (Predict and Try 10 min · Worked example 12 min · Vary 5 min · Practise 5 min · Make (in their own document) and own writing 18 min). -->

## Today

- I can find an embedded clause and the noun it tells about.
- I can tell an embedded clause from a clause joined at the end.
- I can use an embedded clause to add detail without a new sentence.
- I can write a short biography with embedded clauses.

## Why it matters

> 'The boat, which Eli loved, sank.' Where did 'which Eli loved' come from?

<!-- Speaker note: An **embedded clause** (also called a relative clause) sits **inside** a main clause, right after the noun it tells about. It usually starts with *who*, *which*, *that* or *whose*. - *My cousin, **who lives in Perth**, visits in June.* A clause joined at the **end** is different. It starts with a joining word such as *because* or *after* and says something about the whole main clause. - *The track is closed **because the creek is high**.* Both are dependent clauses, but they do different jobs. -->

## Think, pair, share

**Why tuck detail inside a sentence instead of adding another sentence?**

- Read 'My aunt runs a bakery. She sells pies.' Then 'My aunt, who runs a bakery, sells pies.' What changed?
- Which part is now the main news?

<!-- Speaker note: The embedded clause keeps the extra detail in the background so the reader's eye stays on the main clause. Two short sentences give both ideas the same weight. ## Embedded or joined at the end?

Now sort the sentences.

$$\text{Find the noun}$$

2) $\text{Take it out}$ — The main clause should still stand on its own.

## Embedded or joined at the end?

**Sort each sentence by the kind of dependent clause it has.**

Groups: Embedded clause · Clause joined at the end

The cod that Mia caught was huge. · My cousin, who lives in Perth, visits in June. · The oval that we use is flooded. · We stayed inside because it was raining. · The match stopped after the siren sounded. · Ava smiled while she waited.

* Answer: The cod that Mia caught was huge. → Embedded clause; My cousin, who lives in Perth, visits in June. → Embedded clause; The oval that we use is flooded. → Embedded clause; We stayed inside because it was raining. → Clause joined at the end; The match stopped after the siren sounded. → Clause joined at the end; Ava smiled while she waited. → Clause joined at the end

## Find the clause that drifted

**Here is Nib's biography, one sentence per line. One embedded clause has drifted away from the noun it tells about, so the sentence says something silly. Click it.**

```
 1  Eli Brandt grew up in a small town in Queensland.
 2  He became a sailor and later a writer.
 3  Eli wrote about the boat in a book that sank.
 4  The book, which sold well, is still in print.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: 'in a book that sank' makes the book sink. The clause should sit right after the noun it tells about: 'Eli wrote a book about the boat that sank.' -->

## Which keeps the main news main?

**'The bridge is closed. It crosses the creek.' A writer wants the closure to stay the main news and the creek to be a side detail. Which sentence does that?**

- The bridge, which crosses the creek, is closed.
- The bridge crosses the creek and is closed.
- The bridge crosses the creek, so it is closed.
- The bridge is closed because it crosses the creek.

* Answer: The bridge, which crosses the creek, is closed.

<!-- Speaker note: An embedded clause tucks the detail inside, so the main clause (the bridge is closed) stays the main news, without changing the facts. -->

## Make: a short biography

In your own document, write four sentences of a short biography of a person you know or have made up. Use *who*, *which* or *whose* at least twice to add detail inside a sentence, right after the noun it tells about.

Here is one example: "My grandfather, **who was born in Hobart**, worked on the ferries for forty years. His ferry, **whose engine was loud**, crossed the river twice an hour."

Then swap with a partner. Point to each embedded clause and the noun it tells about. Does each clause sit right after its noun?
