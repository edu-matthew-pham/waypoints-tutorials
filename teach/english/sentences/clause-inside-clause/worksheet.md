# A clause inside a clause

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** 'The boat, which Eli loved, sank.' Where did 'which Eli loved' come from?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn that an embedded clause sits inside a main clause, straight after the noun it tells about, and starts with who, which, that or whose. They label five sentences, sort sentences by clause type, catch an embedded clause that drifted from its noun, choose the construction that keeps the main news main, build a sentence with one, colour embedded clauses and write a short biography in their own document.

**Students should be able to say:** I can find an embedded clause and the noun it tells about. I can tell an embedded clause from a clause joined at the end. I can use an embedded clause to add detail without a new sentence. I can write a short biography with embedded clauses.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: where does it go, and why?


:::keep-together

### Embedded or joined at the end?

1. $\text{Find the noun}$ — Which noun is the clause telling you about?
2. $\text{Check where it sits}$ — Right after that noun, inside the main clause.
3. $\text{Take it out}$ — The main clause should still stand on its own.

Now sort the sentences.

**Sort each sentence by the kind of dependent clause it has.**

Groups: Embedded clause, Clause joined at the end

- The cod that Mia caught was huge.: {{field-15}}
- My cousin, who lives in Perth, visits in June.: {{field-15}}
- The oval that we use is flooded.: {{field-15}}
- We stayed inside because it was raining.: {{field-15}}
- The match stopped after the siren sounded.: {{field-15}}
- Ava smiled while she waited.: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** The cod that Mia caught was huge. → Embedded clause; My cousin, who lives in Perth, visits in June. → Embedded clause; The oval that we use is flooded. → Embedded clause; We stayed inside because it was raining. → Clause joined at the end; The match stopped after the siren sounded. → Clause joined at the end; Ava smiled while she waited. → Clause joined at the end

:::

### Find the clause that drifted

**Here is Nib's biography, one sentence per line. One embedded clause has drifted away from the noun it tells about, so the sentence says something silly. Click it.**

```
 1  Eli Brandt grew up in a small town in Queensland.
 2  He became a sailor and later a writer.
 3  Eli wrote about the boat in a book that sank.
 4  The book, which sold well, is still in print.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: 'in a book that sank' makes the book sink. The clause should sit right after the noun it tells about: 'Eli wrote a book about the boat that sank.'

:::


:::keep-together

### Which keeps the main news main?

**'The bridge is closed. It crosses the creek.' A writer wants the closure to stay the main news and the creek to be a side detail. Which sentence does that?**

☐ The bridge, which crosses the creek, is closed.

☐ The bridge crosses the creek and is closed.

☐ The bridge crosses the creek, so it is closed.

☐ The bridge is closed because it crosses the creek.

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** The bridge, which crosses the creek, is closed.

An embedded clause tucks the detail inside, so the main clause (the bridge is closed) stays the main news, without changing the facts.

## Make: a short biography

In your own document, write four sentences of a short biography of a person you know or have made up. Use *who*, *which* or *whose* at least twice to add detail inside a sentence, right after the noun it tells about.

Here is one example: "My grandfather, **who was born in Hobart**, worked on the ferries for forty years. His ferry, **whose engine was loud**, crossed the river twice an hour."

Then swap with a partner. Point to each embedded clause and the noun it tells about. Does each clause sit right after its noun?

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?
