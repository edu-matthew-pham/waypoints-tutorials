# Said or reported

Building sentences · Warm-up: clauses, speech and noun groups

<!-- Speaker note: Students learn two ways to tell what someone said. They label six sentences as direct or indirect, sort the features of each, catch a report that kept the speaker's own words, choose which suits a job, build a reported sentence, choose punctuation for speech, and write a newsletter item in their own document that quotes one person and reports another. About 45 minutes (Predict and Try 8 min · Worked example 12 min · Vary 5 min · Practise 5 min · Make (in their own document) and own writing 15 min). -->

## Today

- I can tell direct speech from indirect speech.
- I can report what someone said, changing the tense and the pronouns.
- I can punctuate direct speech.
- I can choose which kind of speech suits what I am writing.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Try board yourself: six sentences, two kinds of speech. The one idea: direct speech quotes, indirect speech reports and changes the words. This lesson covers AC9E4LA07 nodes 1 to 3 (direct speech features, indirect speech features, choosing between them). The reports in this lesson are made after the event, so the tense moves back (am becomes was). If a statement is still true when it is reported, writers sometimes keep the present tense ('Ava said she is tired' about someone still tired). The lesson teaches the shift because that is the usual pattern, and the board never asks about a case where both would work. Quotation marks: the lesson uses single marks, as many Australian schools do; double marks are also correct. The punctuation board plants the marks and asks only for the mark before the closing mark and before the opening mark. All the speech is made up for the lesson. Teacher: Try: after labelling, ask which sentences could swap kind, and which words would change. Teacher: Worked example: Nib's recount. Ask who 'I' is in line 3. Teacher: Vary: ask why the two decoys each fail. -->

## Why it matters

> 'I am starving,' said Ravi. Ravi said he was starving. Which one lets you hear Ravi?

<!-- Speaker note: There are two ways to tell the reader what someone said. - **Direct speech** gives the exact words, inside quotation marks, with a saying verb and the speaker: *'The soup is hot,' said Dad.* - **Indirect speech** reports what was said, with no quotation marks: *Dad said the soup was hot.* The tense usually moves back (*is* becomes *was*) and the pronouns change (*I* becomes *he*, *we* becomes *they*). -->

## Think, pair, share

**When would a writer rather quote someone than report what they said?**

- Read a short quote from a news story aloud, then read the report of it. Which would you remember?
- Which kind would a writer use for a long meeting? For a heated argument?

<!-- Speaker note: Quote when the exact words or the voice matter. Report when only the information matters. News and recounts usually use both. ## Two ways to tell what was said

**Sort each feature by the kind of speech it belongs to.**

Groups: Direct speech · Indirect speech

The speaker's exact words · Quotation marks around the speech · No quotation marks · 'I' changes to 'she' or 'he' · The tense usually moves back, as in 'am' to 'was'

* Answer: The speaker's exact words → Direct speech; Quotation marks around the speech → Direct speech; No quotation marks → Indirect speech; 'I' changes to 'she' or 'he' → Indirect speech; The tense usually moves back, as in 'am' to 'was' → Indirect speech

## Which suits the job?

**A news report must sum up what four people said at a long meeting in two lines. Which kind of speech fits better?**

- Neither, because speech can't be summed up
- Direct speech, which quotes every person's exact words
- Direct speech, which needs no saying verb
- Indirect speech, which sums up what each person said

* Answer: Indirect speech, which sums up what each person said

<!-- Speaker note: Indirect speech is the writer's summary, so it is quick. Direct speech is for moments when the reader should hear the exact words. -->

## Quote or report?

**'We have won!' cried the captain. Which is the reported version?**

- The captain cried that they had won.
- The captain cried, 'We have won.'

* Answer: The captain cried that they had won.

## Make: a newsletter item

In your own document, write a short newsletter item (at least three sentences) about a school event, such as a sports day, a concert or a visit. Quote one person's exact words inside quotation marks. Report what a second person said, with no quotation marks.

Here is one example: On Friday our school held its swimming carnival. 'I have never heard such cheering,' said the principal. The captain told the crowd that the Blue team **had won**.

Then swap with a partner. Find the quoted sentence and the reported sentence. Does the quote have its marks and its punctuation? Has the report lost its marks, and moved its tense and pronouns?

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Writing a newsletter item that quotes one person and reports another puts both kinds of speech to work for a reader.
