# What the extra clause adds

Building sentences · Warm-up: clauses, speech and noun groups

<!-- Speaker note: Students learn that a dependent clause adds one of five kinds of information (reason, purpose, condition, concession, time) and that the clause that comes first gets noticed first. They label five dependent clauses, sort joining words, catch a joining word that makes the wrong relationship, build one sentence in both orders, choose joining words in context and write a letter to the principal in their own document. About 50 minutes (Predict and Try 8 min · Worked example 12 min · Vary 7 min · Practise 5 min · Make (in their own document) and own writing 18 min). -->

## Today

- I can name what a dependent clause adds: reason, purpose, condition, concession or time.
- I can choose the joining word that makes the relationship I mean.
- I can put a dependent clause first or last, and say what changes.
- I can use complex sentences to argue in a letter.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Try board yourself: five dependent clauses, five relationships. The one idea: the joining word decides the relationship, and the order decides what is noticed first. This lesson covers AC9E5LA05 nodes 1 to 3 (types of logical relationship, clause ordering and prominence, using complex sentences for effect). ACARA names the relationships reason, purpose, condition, concession and time. **Concession** is a clause that admits a point against the main idea (although, even though); the lesson says so in plain words. Reason and purpose can feel close. A reason says why something happened (because students were getting sunburnt); a purpose says what it is for (so that everyone can sit down). The board only plants clear examples. The clause-order effects are tendencies, not rules: a fronted dependent clause tends to set the scene or build to the main idea, and a trailing one tends to give the news first. The Make step is written in the student's own document and judged by a partner, not the site. Teacher: Try: after labelling, ask which clause could swap to the other end, and what the reader would notice first. Teacher: Worked example: Nib's letter. Ask what Nib actually means, then which joining word says that. Teacher: Vary: build both orders. Ask which one a news headline would pick. -->

## Why it matters

> 'Although the canteen costs more, it serves healthier food.' Why does the writer start with the downside?

<!-- Speaker note: A dependent clause adds one of five kinds of information: - **Reason:** *because* the filter broke - **Purpose:** *so that* everyone can see - **Condition:** *if* the creek rises - **Concession:** *although* the fare was high (a point that goes against the main idea) - **Time:** *after* the bell rang It can come before or after the main clause. What comes **first** gets the reader's attention first. -->

## Find the wrong relationship

**Here is Nib's letter to the principal, one sentence per line. One joining word gives a different relationship from the one Nib means. Click that line.**

```
 1  Our school library closes at 1 pm.
 2  I think it should stay open at lunch because that is when students are free.
 3  It should stay open although many students need a quiet place to read.
 4  I hope you agree, since it would help everyone.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: Nib means 'because many students need a quiet place to read', a reason. Although admits a point against the main idea, so the sentence says the opposite of what Nib means. -->

## What does the order put first?

**'Although the walk was long, the group finished before dark.' Compared with 'The group finished before dark although the walk was long,' what does the first put first?**

- The difficulty, so the finish comes as a relief
- Nothing, because the order makes no difference
- The finish, so the difficulty is only an afterthought
- Both ideas at once

* Answer: The difficulty, so the finish comes as a relief

<!-- Speaker note: The first version makes the reader meet the long walk first, so the finish lands as a relief. The second leads with the good news. -->

## Make: a letter to the principal

In your own document, write a letter to the principal asking for something you would like the school to change. Write at least four sentences. Use at least three different joining words from this lesson, such as *because*, *so that*, *unless*, *although* or *until*. Start at least one sentence with its dependent clause.

Here is one example: "Dear Mrs Hart, I am writing to ask for a longer lunch break. Students need time to eat and play **because** we study hard all morning. **If** lunch were half an hour longer, more of us could join a club. **Although** the timetable is full, a longer lunch would help everyone concentrate in the afternoon."

Then swap with a partner. Point to each joining word and say what its clause adds: a reason, a purpose, a condition, a concession or a time. Which clause does each sentence put first, and why?

## Exit ticket

**'Unless the rain stops, the picnic will move inside.' What does the dependent clause add, and what does it put first?**

* It adds a condition, and it puts the condition first, so the reader meets what has to be true before the result.
