# Challenge: rework the report

Building sentences · Challenge: rework the report

<!-- Speaker note: The challenge draws the course together. Students rework Nib's school garden report: they fold two sentences into one, keep the tense steady, choose joining words that fit the job, and then rework a choppy paragraph in their own document by combining, embedding and varying length. About 50 minutes (Predict 3 min · Parts 1 to 3 18 min · Part 4 20 min · Own writing 9 min). -->

---

## Today

- I can fold two sentences about the same thing into one.
- I can find and fix a verb that slips out of the tense.
- I can choose a joining word that fits the job.
- I can rework a choppy paragraph without changing its facts.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do each part yourself first: one fold, one tense check, one shape choice, then the paragraph. Part 4 starts with a choppy paragraph that students copy into their own document and rework there. The site does not take, store or count their writing. The moves to look for are one folded clause (who, which or whose), at least two joining words, and a short and a long sentence; these are rough aids to show the moves were tried, not proof the paragraph is good. ## Why it matters

> A report has all the right facts but reads like a list. Can you rework it without changing a single fact?

## Think, pair, share

**How do we know a rewrite is better and not just different?**

- Read the choppy and the reworked version aloud. What does the listener notice?
- Did any fact change?

## Predict

**A reader says: 'Every sentence is about the same length, some repeat the same noun, and a few verbs jump to a different time.' Which moves fix that?**

- Add more facts to each sentence
- Make every sentence longer
- Fold sentences, fix the tense and mix the shapes
- Use bigger words everywhere

<!-- Speaker note: What happens: Folding removes the repeated noun, steady tense fixes the jump in time, and a mix of shapes stops every sentence sounding the same. That is the plan for this challenge. Take two or three predictions before anyone runs it. -->

## Make: Part 4: rework a choppy paragraph

Copy this choppy paragraph into your own document:

> The garden was behind the hall. The garden had four beds. We planted beans in the first bed. The beans grew slowly. We watered the plants every day. The plants were healthy.

Rework it without changing a fact. Write at least four sentences. Use *who*, *which* or *whose* at least once. Use at least two joining words, such as *because* or *until*. Keep one short sentence of five words or fewer to land the end, and make one sentence long, fifteen words or more.

Here is a different choppy paragraph, reworked: "The kitchen, **which** smelled of toast, was full. We waited **until** the bell rang **because** nobody wanted to be late. Then we ran."

Then swap with a partner. Tick off every fact from the choppy paragraph in the new one. Did any fact change or go missing? Does it read well?

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Reworking a choppy paragraph with all the moves is the course's main transfer task. Hints, in order: Fold two sentences about the same thing with which or who. / Join two ideas with a word like because or until, and keep a short sentence to land the end. For students who need more support: A fold frame: [noun], which/who [detail], [rest of the sentence]. For early finishers: Rework a paragraph from a class novel the other way: unpack a long sentence into short ones. What changes? The hard part, on purpose: Students make every sentence long. Ask 'which sentence should the reader see at a glance?' and keep that one short. When students get stuck: A student's reworked paragraph is one very long sentence. Ask: Which sentence should the reader notice first? Fix: Keep it short, and fold the detail into the sentences around it. / A student drops a fact when folding. Ask: Can you find every fact from the choppy version? Fix: Tick each fact off in the new version. -->
