# One main idea, one extra

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** 'If you want to win.' Is that a whole sentence?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn that a sentence can have a main clause, which stands alone, and a dependent clause, which adds when, why, if or how and cannot. They label both halves of four sentences, match joining words to what they add, catch a dependent clause left as a sentence, choose the joining word that fits, colour joining words, and write three sentences of a recount.

**Students should be able to say:** I can tell a main clause from a dependent clause. I can name the joining word and what it adds (when, why, what must be true, how). I can spot a dependent clause left on its own as a sentence. I can write a recount that uses complex sentences.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: what does the extra add?


:::keep-together

### Three steps for any complex sentence

1. $\text{Find the joining word}$ — Look for because, until, if, after, while, although.
2. $\text{Test each part}$ — Could this part be a whole sentence on its own?
3. $\text{Name what it adds}$ — When? Why? What must be true? How?

Now match the joining words.

**Match each joining word to what its clause adds.**

A. Why: reason

B. How: manner

C. What must be true: condition

D. When: time

:::teacher-note

**Answer:** after → D. When: time; because → A. Why: reason; unless → C. What must be true: condition; as if → B. How: manner

The joining word tells the reader how the extra idea connects to the main one: when it happens, why, what has to be true, or how.

### Find the clause that cannot stand alone

**Here is Nib's recount, one sentence per line. One line is only a dependent clause, so it cannot stand alone. Click it.**

```
 1  On Friday our class walked to the creek.
 2  We stopped when we reached the old bridge.
 3  Because the water was high.
 4  Ms Okafor told us to stay back.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: 'Because the water was high.' leaves you waiting for what happened. Nib needs a main clause: 'Because the water was high, Ms Okafor told us to stay back.'

:::


:::keep-together

### Which one gives the reason?

**Which sentence tells you why the group packed up?**

☐ We will pack up if the storm arrives.

☐ We packed up when the storm arrived.

☐ We packed up because the storm was coming.

☐ We packed up before the storm arrived.

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** We packed up because the storm was coming.

Because gives the reason. The joining word decides what kind of extra information the dependent clause adds.

## Make: a recount with extras

In your own document, write at least three sentences of a recount, such as a school excursion or a day out. Use at least two different joining words, such as *when*, *because*, *after*, *until* or *while*.

Here is one example: "On Tuesday our class visited the wetlands. We wore boots **because** the ground was muddy. **After** we ate lunch, we counted the birds."

Then swap with a partner. Point to each dependent clause and say what it adds: when, why, what must be true or how. Could it stand alone?

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?
