# One main idea, one extra

Building sentences · Warm-up: clauses, speech and noun groups

<!-- Speaker note: Students learn that a sentence can have a main clause, which stands alone, and a dependent clause, which adds when, why, if or how and cannot. ## Today

- I can tell a main clause from a dependent clause.
- I can name the joining word and what it adds (when, why, what must be true, how).
- I can spot a dependent clause left on its own as a sentence.
- I can write a recount that uses complex sentences.

## Why it matters

> 'If you want to win.' Is that a whole sentence?

<!-- Speaker note: A **clause** is a group of words built around a verb. There are two kinds: - A **main clause** makes sense on its own. It can be a whole sentence. - A **dependent clause** adds extra information (when, why, if) and cannot stand alone. It starts with a **joining word** (also called a subordinating conjunction) such as *because*, *until*, *if*, *after* or *while*. A sentence with a main clause and a dependent clause is a **complex sentence**. -->

## Think, pair, share

**Why do writers use dependent clauses instead of lots of short sentences?**

- Say 'The match was cancelled. The oval was flooded.' Now say it with because. What does the joining word do that the full stop did not?
- Which version would you rather read in a long recount?

<!-- Speaker note: The joining word tells the reader how the two ideas connect. Two short sentences leave the reader to work that out. A complex sentence says it, and a long text reads more smoothly. Give 30 seconds alone, 2 minutes in pairs, then take answers from both sides. There is no single right answer: ask for a reason, and let students change their minds. -->

## Three steps for any complex sentence

Now match the joining words.

$$\text{Find the joining word}$$

1) $\text{Test each part}$ — Could this part be a whole sentence on its own?
2) $\text{Name what it adds}$ — When? Why? What must be true? How?

## Three steps for any complex sentence

**Match each joining word to what its clause adds.**

| | |
|---|---|
| `after` | A. Why: reason |
| `because` | B. How: manner |
| `unless` | C. What must be true: condition |
| `as if` | D. When: time |

* Answer: after → D. When: time; because → A. Why: reason; unless → C. What must be true: condition; as if → B. How: manner

<!-- Speaker note: The joining word tells the reader how the extra idea connects to the main one: when it happens, why, what has to be true, or how. -->

## Find the clause that cannot stand alone

**Here is Nib's recount, one sentence per line. One line is only a dependent clause, so it cannot stand alone. Click it.**

```
 1  On Friday our class walked to the creek.
 2  We stopped when we reached the old bridge.
 3  Because the water was high.
 4  Ms Okafor told us to stay back.
```

* Answer: line 3

<!-- Speaker note: Line 3: 'Because the water was high.' leaves you waiting for what happened. Nib needs a main clause: 'Because the water was high, Ms Okafor told us to stay back.' -->

## Which one gives the reason?

**Which sentence tells you why the group packed up?**

- We will pack up if the storm arrives.
- We packed up when the storm arrived.
- We packed up because the storm was coming.
- We packed up before the storm arrived.

* Answer: We packed up because the storm was coming.

<!-- Speaker note: Because gives the reason. The joining word decides what kind of extra information the dependent clause adds. -->

## Make: a recount with extras

In your own document, write at least three sentences of a recount, such as a school excursion or a day out. Use at least two different joining words, such as *when*, *because*, *after*, *until* or *while*.

Here is one example: "On Tuesday our class visited the wetlands. We wore boots **because** the ground was muddy. **After** we ate lunch, we counted the birds."

Then swap with a partner. Point to each dependent clause and say what it adds: when, why, what must be true or how. Could it stand alone?

## Exit ticket

**'We stayed inside until the storm passed.' Which part is the dependent clause, and what does it add?**

* 'Until the storm passed' is the dependent clause. It adds a time: how long they stayed inside.
