# Choose the shape for the job

Building sentences · Shaping sentences

<!-- Speaker note: Students learn that a dependent clause can elaborate, extend or explain, and that a writer chooses short or long, simple, compound or complex sentences for the job. They label what clauses add, match jobs to shapes, catch a sentence too long for a notice, build an extending sentence, colour short and long sentences and write a school fair notice in their own document. About 50 minutes (Predict and Try 10 min · Worked example 12 min · Vary 5 min · Practise 5 min · Make (in their own document) and own writing 18 min). -->

## Today

- I can say whether a dependent clause elaborates, extends or explains.
- I can choose a short or long sentence for the job.
- I can mix sentence lengths so the key point stands out.
- I can write a notice with a deliberate mix of shapes.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Try board yourself: six clauses, three labels. The one idea: the job decides the shape. This lesson covers AC9E7LA05 nodes 3 and 4 (elaborating, extending and explaining; sentence structure as a deliberate choice). Elaborating gives more detail about something already named, extending adds a related new fact, and explaining gives a reason. Items on the board were chosen so one label clearly fits; a few real sentences can sit between labels, and students can argue them in discussion. The Make step gives rough lengths (short is five words or fewer, long is fifteen or more). Students count in their own document and check with a partner; the site counts nothing. A mix existing does not make it a good mix. The three jobs are ACARA's broad view of a dependent clause. Time, condition and concession clauses (from What the clause adds) fall under them: a time clause usually extends, and a condition or concession qualifies the main idea. The board keeps to the clear cases and lets students argue the rest in discussion. The sentences are made up for the lesson. Teacher: Try: ask three questions in turn: does it describe a noun, does it say what happened as a result, does it say why? Teacher: Worked example: ask where a driver's eyes go first. -->

## Why it matters

> 'Stop.' or 'Please stop, because there is a hole in the road ahead.' Which would you put on a sign?

<!-- Speaker note: A dependent clause adds one of three things. - **Elaborates:** describes a noun in more detail. *Ava bought a kite that had a long red tail.* - **Extends:** adds what happened as a result or next. *The rain stopped, which meant we could play outside.* - **Explains:** says why. *We stayed in because the oval was flooded.* Then a writer chooses the shape for the job: a short sentence for a warning, a longer one when the reader needs the detail or the reason. -->

## Predict

**A sign at a building site must be read in one glance. Which would you put on it?**

- Anyone who is entering this site is required to wear a hard hat at all times, since falling objects are a danger.
- Although it is not always raining, a hard hat is needed because there is danger.
- Hard hats must be worn.
- Hard hats, which are important, should perhaps be worn.

## Pick the shape for the job

**Match each job to the kind of sentence that suits it.**

| | |
|---|---|
| `A warning that must be read in a glance` | A. A complex sentence with because |
| `Two facts of equal weight` | B. A compound sentence |
| `A fact with the reason for it` | C. A short simple sentence |

* Answer: A warning that must be read in a glance → C. A short simple sentence; Two facts of equal weight → B. A compound sentence; A fact with the reason for it → A. A complex sentence with because

## Why mix lengths?

**Why might a writer follow a long sentence with a very short one?**

- Short sentences are easier to spell
- It makes the text longer
- Short sentences are always correct
- The short one stands out and lands the point

* Answer: The short one stands out and lands the point

<!-- Speaker note: After a long sentence full of detail, a short one catches attention and lands the main point. -->

## Make: a notice for the school fair

In your own document, write a notice for the school fair. Write at least five sentences (not headings). Say what, when and what to bring. Put the most important instruction in a short sentence of five words or fewer. Use at least one long sentence of fifteen words or more that gives detail or a reason.

Here is one example: "The school fair is on Saturday. It will run from nine until three, and every class has built a stall that sells something they made themselves. **Come early.** The cake stall always sells out before ten, since the baking is so popular. **Bring a bag for everything you buy.**"

Then swap with a partner. Do the short sentences carry the key instructions? Do the long ones carry the detail and the reasons?

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Writing a notice with some short and some long sentences puts the choice of shape to work for a reader. Hints, in order: Write what, when and what to bring.
