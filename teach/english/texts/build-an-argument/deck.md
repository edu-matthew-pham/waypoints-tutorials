# Challenge: build an argument

How texts are built · Challenge: build an argument

<!-- Speaker note: The course challenge, built on the genre teaching and learning cycle. Students take a model argument apart (position, reasons, restated position), put Nib's jumbled argument back in order, choose the joining words that carry an argument from reason to reason, and build their own argument on the board, then check it with a partner against a short checklist. About 55 minutes (Predict 4 min · Part 1 8 min · Part 2 7 min · Part 3 6 min · Part 4 20 min · Own writing 10 min). -->

---

## Today

- I can name the stages of an argument and what each one does.
- I can put an argument's stages in order and link its reasons.
- I can build my own argument with a position, reasons with evidence and a restated position.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Try Part 1 yourself, then write a quick argument in Part 4 to feel how long it takes. The challenge follows the **genre teaching and learning cycle**: deconstruction (Part 1, take a model apart), then Parts 2 and 3 as a scaffolded middle step (fixing Nib's order and choosing joining words), and independent construction (Part 4). On screen students work alone, so the real **joint construction** happens in class: build one argument together on the board (the class plan's opening activity) before Part 4. It brings together AC9E6LA03 node 2 (characteristic stages of a text type), AC9E7LA03 node 1 (purpose drives structure) and AC9E8LA03 node 1 (structure and language serve the purpose). The argument's stages are often called thesis, arguments and reinforcement of thesis; the lesson says position, reasons and restated position. Claim and support (Patterns for ideas) is the shape of each reason paragraph, and the Holding writing together course's Inside a paragraph goes deeper into evidence and explanation. The board only checks that the stages are there and labelled. The quality (clear position, evidence, a restatement with nothing new) is judged with a partner or by you, with the checklist on the page. Nothing is marked by AI. Teacher: Part 1: ask where the evidence is in each reason paragraph. Teacher: Part 2: ask which word decides the order of the two reasons. (Also.) Teacher: Part 3: ask what Therefore promises the reader. Teacher: Part 4: partners swap and use the checklist. One fix each before the lesson ends. -->

## Why it matters

> You want your school to change one thing. You get one page to convince the principal. How do you build it?

<!-- Speaker note: An **argument** tries to convince the reader. It has three stages: - **Position:** what you want, in one clear sentence. - **Reasons:** each one in its own paragraph, with evidence. - **Restated position:** the point again, so the reader leaves with it. In this challenge you take a model apart, fix Nib's, then build your own. -->

## Think, pair, share

**What makes an argument convincing: the reasons, the evidence, or the order?**

- Would the tree argument work with no evidence at all?
- Would it work with the restated position first?

<!-- Speaker note: All three matter. Reasons give the case, evidence makes it believable, and the order lets the reader follow it and remember the point. Give 30 seconds alone, 2 minutes in pairs, then take answers from both sides. There is no single right answer: ask for a reason, and let students change their minds. -->

## Predict

**You're writing to the principal to ask for more trees in the playground. Which plan will convince them best?**

- A story about a hot day, and nothing else
- Facts about trees, with no request
- What you want, your reasons with evidence, then what you want again
- Your request, said three times in a row

<!-- Speaker note: What happens: What you want, your reasons with evidence, then what you want again: the stages of an argument. A story might help as an opening, but on its own it doesn't ask for anything. Take two or three predictions before anyone runs it. -->

## Make: Part 4: build your own

The board checks that each stage is there and labelled. With a partner, check the rest:

- Is the position one clear sentence?
- Does each reason have a piece of evidence?
- Does the restated position sum up the reasons, without adding a new one?

[Open this step](https://tutorials.learningwaypoints.com/english/texts/build-an-argument/?full=step-5#step-5)

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Writing their own argument on the board is the independent construction step: the stages are theirs to use. Hints, in order: Choose one change you'd like at your school. Click the first box and write your position in one sentence. / Write two reasons, each with a piece of evidence, then a restated position. Label each part. Add a box if you need one. For students who need more support: A planning frame with four boxes: I want... · Reason 1 + evidence · Reason 2 + evidence · So, we should... For early finishers: Add a short story at the start of your argument, as in Mixing text types. Does it make the argument stronger? Check that it links to your position. The hard part, on purpose: Part 4: students write reasons with no evidence ('Trees are good. Trees are nice.'). The checklist asks for evidence in every reason. Ask: how do you know? What would show the principal it's true? When students get stuck: A student labels the restated position as a reason. Ask: Does it give a new reason, or sum up the ones before? Fix: The restated position sums up and asks again. It adds nothing new. / A student's position is a question ('Should we have more trees?'). Ask: What do you want the reader to do or agree to? Fix: Make it a statement: 'Our school should...' / A student's reasons have no evidence. Ask: How do you know? What happened, or what could you measure? Fix: Add one fact or example to each reason: a time, a number, something students said. -->

## Exit ticket

**An argument ends: 'Also, trees give homes to possums.' What's wrong with this as an ending?**

* It adds a new reason instead of restating the position. The ending should sum up the reasons and press the point again.
