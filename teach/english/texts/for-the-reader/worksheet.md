# Built for a reader

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** A sign says 'Bathers are advised to remain within the designated flagged area.' Would a seven-year-old follow it?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn that structure and language are chosen together for a particular reader, and that judging a text means asking whether those choices work for that reader. They colour commands and direct address in a beach poster for children, match features to effects, catch an adult sentence in a children's poster, label a poster's hook, steps and reminder, sort sun safety paragraphs by reader, and rewrite an adult text for a Year 2 student.

**Students should be able to say:** I can name structure and language features chosen for a reader. I can judge whether a text's choices work for the reader it was written for. I can rewrite a text for a different reader.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: does it work for the reader?


:::keep-together

### Choices for a reader

1. $\text{Picture the reader}$ — Who are they? How old? What do they already know?
2. $\text{Choose the structure}$ — A hook, then steps? Or reasons and detail?
3. $\text{Choose the language}$ — Short commands and you, or technical words and full explanations?
4. $\text{Judge it}$ — Would this reader understand it and act on it?

Now match each feature to what it does.

**Match each feature to what it does for a young reader.**

A. Say exactly what to do

B. Catch the eye from a distance

C. Speak straight to the reader

D. Easy to read quickly

**Answer:** Short sentences → D. Easy to read quickly; Commands such as Swim and Stay → A. Say exactly what to do; You and your → C. Speak straight to the reader; A big heading → B. Catch the eye from a distance

Each feature is chosen for what it does for the reader, not because posters always have it.

### Find Nib's slip

**Here is Nib's beach poster for Year 2, one line each. Click the line that doesn't suit its reader.**

```
 1  Beach safety for Year 2
 2  Swim between the red and yellow flags.
 3  Rip currents are concentrated channels of water flowing seaward through the surf zone.
 4  If you get tired, float on your back and raise one arm.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3 is written for adults: long, technical (concentrated, seaward, surf zone) and with no instruction. A Year 2 reader needs something like 'If the water pulls you out, don't fight it.'

:::


:::keep-together

### Why more for parents?

**A beach safety guide for parents adds a paragraph on how to spot a rip current, with a diagram. Why include it for parents but not on the Year 2 poster?**

☐ Parents like longer texts with more pictures in them

☐ Year 2 students don't usually go to the beach

☐ Diagrams are too hard to draw for young children

☐ Parents can act on the detail; a young child needs one simple rule

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Parents can act on the detail; a young child needs one simple rule

The structure grows or shrinks for the reader. Parents can act on the detail; a young child needs a rule they can remember in the water.

## Make: rewrite for a younger reader

Here is a sun safety text written for adults. Copy it into your own document.

> Ultraviolet radiation from the sun can damage unprotected skin in as little as fifteen minutes. Individuals are advised to wear broad-brimmed hats, apply sunscreen and seek shade during periods of high UV.

In your own document, rewrite it for a Year 2 student. Use at least three short sentences. Try commands (*Wear*, *Put on*, *Play*) and *you* or *your*. Use no word a seven-year-old wouldn't know.

Here is one example: "The sun can burn **your** skin very fast. **Wear** a big hat. **Put on** sunscreen. **Play** in the shade when it is hot."

Then swap with a partner and read your version to them as if they were seven. Use this checklist: short sentences, commands, *you* or *your*, and no hard words. Would they know what to do?

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?

## Exit ticket

:::keep-together

**A text for parents says 'Wear a hat!' and nothing else. Does it work for its reader? Why or why not?**

{{lines-3}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Probably not well: adults can use reasons and detail (why hats matter, which kind), so a single command undersells them. It would suit a young child better.
