# Built for a reader

How texts are built · Built for a reader

<!-- Speaker note: Students learn that structure and language are chosen together for a particular reader, and that judging a text means asking whether those choices work for that reader. - I can name structure and language features chosen for a reader.
- I can judge whether a text's choices work for the reader it was written for.
- I can rewrite a text for a different reader.

## Why it matters

> A sign says 'Bathers are advised to remain within the designated flagged area.' Would a seven-year-old follow it?

<!-- Speaker note: Writers choose **structure** and **language** together, for a particular **reader**. For young readers, a writer might use: - a **question** or big heading to hook them; - **short commands** that say exactly what to do (Swim, Stay, Ask); - **you** and **your**, to speak straight to the reader. For adults, the same writer might add **reasons**, **technical words** and more detail. A text works when its choices fit its reader. -->

## Choices for a reader

Now match each feature to what it does.

$$\text{Picture the reader}$$

1) $\text{Choose the structure}$ — A hook, then steps? Or reasons and detail?
2) $\text{Choose the language}$ — Short commands and you, or technical words and full explanations?
3) $\text{Judge it}$ — Would this reader understand it and act on it?

## Choices for a reader

**Match each feature to what it does for a young reader.**

| | |
|---|---|
| `Short sentences` | A. Say exactly what to do |
| `Commands such as Swim and Stay` | B. Catch the eye from a distance |
| `You and your` | C. Speak straight to the reader |
| `A big heading` | D. Easy to read quickly |

* Answer: Short sentences → D. Easy to read quickly; Commands such as Swim and Stay → A. Say exactly what to do; You and your → C. Speak straight to the reader; A big heading → B. Catch the eye from a distance

## Why more for parents?

**A beach safety guide for parents adds a paragraph on how to spot a rip current, with a diagram. Why include it for parents but not on the Year 2 poster?**

- Parents like longer texts with more pictures in them
- Year 2 students don't usually go to the beach
- Diagrams are too hard to draw for young children
- Parents can act on the detail; a young child needs one simple rule

* Answer: Parents can act on the detail; a young child needs one simple rule

<!-- Speaker note: The structure grows or shrinks for the reader. Parents can act on the detail; a young child needs a rule they can remember in the water. -->

## Make: rewrite for a younger reader

Here is a sun safety text written for adults. Copy it into your own document.

> Ultraviolet radiation from the sun can damage unprotected skin in as little as fifteen minutes. Individuals are advised to wear broad-brimmed hats, apply sunscreen and seek shade during periods of high UV.

In your own document, rewrite it for a Year 2 student. Use at least three short sentences. Try commands (*Wear*, *Put on*, *Play*) and *you* or *your*. Use no word a seven-year-old wouldn't know.

Here is one example: "The sun can burn **your** skin very fast. **Wear** a big hat. **Put on** sunscreen. **Play** in the shade when it is hot."

Then swap with a partner and read your version to them as if they were seven. Use this checklist: short sentences, commands, *you* or *your*, and no hard words. Would they know what to do?

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Rewriting an adult text for a child makes students choose structure and language for a reader, which they then judge. Hints, in order: Who will read this? Imagine a seven-year-old. What should they do after reading it? / Cut long words first (ultraviolet, individuals). Then try commands: Wear, Put on, Play. -->

## Exit ticket

**A text for parents says 'Wear a hat!' and nothing else. Does it work for its reader? Why or why not?**

* Probably not well: adults can use reasons and detail (why hats matter, which kind), so a single command undersells them. It would suit a young child better.
