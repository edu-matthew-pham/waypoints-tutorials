# Purpose shapes the text

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** You know ten facts about frogs. Would you put them in the same order to teach Year 3, to win a debate, or to tell your family about an excursion?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn that a text's purpose decides its stages and its language. Using the same frog facts, they label a report's stages, match purposes to stages, sort language by purpose, catch a report that drifts into a recount, label an argument built from the same facts, colour time words and technical words, and write their own report.

**Students should be able to say:** I can say how a text's purpose decides its stages. I can spot language that fits a recount, a report or an argument. I can notice when a text drifts away from its purpose. I can write a report with a general statement and descriptions.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: purpose to stages to language


:::keep-together

### Purpose decides the stages

1. $\text{Ask what it is for}$ — Retell, describe, explain or persuade?
2. $\text{Choose the stages}$ — Each purpose has its own stages (see the list above).
3. $\text{Choose the language}$ — Past tense and time words, present tense and technical words, or should and because.
4. $\text{Check for drift}$ — Every sentence should serve the one purpose.

Now match each purpose to its stages.

**Match each purpose to the stages that fit it.**

A. General statement, descriptions

B. Position, reasons, position restated

C. Opening statement, steps in order

D. Orientation, events, ending

:::teacher-note

**Answer:** To retell what happened → D. Orientation, events, ending; To describe what something is like → A. General statement, descriptions; To explain how or why → C. Opening statement, steps in order; To persuade → B. Position, reasons, position restated

Each purpose needs different stages. A writer chooses the stages by asking what the text is for.

### Sort the language

**Which kind of text does each sentence's language belong to?**

Groups: Recount, Report, Argument

- On Friday, we visited the museum.: {{field-15}}
- After that, we ate lunch in the park.: {{field-15}}
- Wombats dig burrows with their front claws.: {{field-15}}
- Eucalypts are evergreen trees.: {{field-15}}
- Our school should build a frog pond.: {{field-15}}
- A pond would let every class study frogs.: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** On Friday, we visited the museum. → Recount; After that, we ate lunch in the park. → Recount; Wombats dig burrows with their front claws. → Report; Eucalypts are evergreen trees. → Report; Our school should build a frog pond. → Argument; A pond would let every class study frogs. → Argument

Recounts use the past tense and time words, reports the present tense and technical words, and arguments words such as should and would.

:::

### Find Nib's drift

**Here is Nib's report on frogs, one sentence per line. Click the line where Nib drifts away from the report's purpose.**

```
 1  Frogs are amphibians that live in water and on land.
 2  Many frogs have long back legs for jumping.
 3  Last week our class went to the creek, and Mr Ali slipped in the mud.
 4  Most frogs lay their eggs in water.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3 retells the excursion: past tense, our class, a funny moment. That belongs in a recount. A report describes frogs in general, so Nib should cut it or move it to a recount of the trip.
