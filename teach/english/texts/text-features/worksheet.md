# Features that fit the job

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** 'Thunder shook the windows.' Is that the start of a story, a science report or an advert? How can you tell from one sentence?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** An optional warm-up from Years 4 and 5. Students learn that imaginative, informative and persuasive texts each have features that fit their purpose, and that informative texts include reports (what something is like) and explanations (how or why something happens). They label three paragraphs about one gum tree, sort features, colour persuasive words, and write the same topic three ways.

**Students should be able to say:** I can tell imaginative, informative and persuasive texts apart by their features. I can tell a report from an explanation. I can say why a persuasive text might use a character. I can write about one topic for three different purposes.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: features as clues


:::keep-together

### What fits which?

1. $\text{Ask what the text is for}$ — To entertain, to inform, or to persuade?
2. $\text{Look for features}$ — Characters and dialogue, facts and technical words, or opinions and reasons?
3. $\text{Check they fit}$ — Every feature should help the text do its job.
4. $\text{Report or explanation?}$ — What something is like, or how and why it happens?

Now sort the features.

**Which kind of text does each feature belong to?**

Groups: Imaginative, Informative, Persuasive

- characters and a problem to solve: {{field-15}}
- dialogue: what characters say: {{field-15}}
- technical words, such as eucalypt: {{field-15}}
- headings and facts: {{field-15}}
- the writer's opinion, with reasons: {{field-15}}
- a call to act: Write to the council today: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** characters and a problem to solve → Imaginative; dialogue: what characters say → Imaginative; technical words, such as eucalypt → Informative; headings and facts → Informative; the writer's opinion, with reasons → Persuasive; a call to act: Write to the council today → Persuasive

Each feature helps a text do its job: stories need characters, information needs facts, and persuasion needs an opinion the reader can be won over to.

:::


:::keep-together

### Report or explanation?

**Is each sentence from a report (what something is like) or an explanation (how or why something happens)?**

Groups: Report, Explanation

- Volcanoes are openings in the Earth's crust where melted rock comes out.: {{field-15}}
- Some volcanoes are tall cones, and others are wide and low.: {{field-15}}
- When gas builds up underground, it pushes melted rock upwards.: {{field-15}}
- As a result, the volcano erupts.: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Volcanoes are openings in the Earth's crust where melted rock comes out. → Report; Some volcanoes are tall cones, and others are wide and low. → Report; When gas builds up underground, it pushes melted rock upwards. → Explanation; As a result, the volcano erupts. → Explanation

A report describes and classifies: what something is and what it is like. An explanation follows a process or a cause: how or why something happens.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find Nib's slip

**Here is Nib's information report, one sentence per line. Click the line that doesn't fit an informative text.**

```
 1  Koalas are marsupials that live in eucalypt forests.
 2  They eat eucalyptus leaves and sleep for most of the day.
 3  I think koalas are the cutest animals in the world.
 4  A mother koala carries her joey in a pouch for about six months.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: 'I think koalas are the cutest' is an opinion. A report gives facts, so the reader can trust it. Opinions belong in persuasive texts or reviews.

:::
