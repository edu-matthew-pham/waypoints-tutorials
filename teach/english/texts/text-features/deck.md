# Features that fit the job

How texts are built · Warm-up: stages and features

<!-- Speaker note: An optional warm-up from Years 4 and 5. Students learn that imaginative, informative and persuasive texts each have features that fit their purpose, and that informative texts include reports (what something is like) and explanations (how or why something happens). They label three paragraphs about one gum tree, sort features, colour persuasive words, and write the same topic three ways. About 45 minutes (Predict and Try 8 min · Worked example 12 min · Vary 10 min · Practise 6 min · Make and own writing 9 min). -->

## Today

- I can tell imaginative, informative and persuasive texts apart by their features.
- I can tell a report from an explanation.
- I can say why a persuasive text might use a character.
- I can write about one topic for three different purposes.

## Why it matters

> 'Thunder shook the windows.' Is that the start of a story, a science report or an advert? How can you tell from one sentence?

<!-- Speaker note: Texts are written for different **purposes**, and each kind has **features** that fit its job. - **Imaginative** texts entertain: characters, a setting, a problem, describing words and dialogue. - **Informative** texts inform: facts, technical words and a calm tone. A **report** says what something is like; an **explanation** says how or why something happens. - **Persuasive** texts try to change your mind: an opinion, reasons, strong words such as **must** and **best**, and a call to act. -->

## What fits which?

Now sort the features.

$$\text{Ask what the text is for}$$

1) $\text{Look for features}$ — Characters and dialogue, facts and technical words, or opinions and reasons?
2) $\text{Check they fit}$ — Every feature should help the text do its job.
3) $\text{Report or explanation?}$ — What something is like, or how and why it happens?

## What fits which?

**Which kind of text does each feature belong to?**

Groups: Imaginative · Informative · Persuasive

characters and a problem to solve · dialogue: what characters say · technical words, such as eucalypt · headings and facts · the writer's opinion, with reasons · a call to act: Write to the council today

* Answer: characters and a problem to solve → Imaginative; dialogue: what characters say → Imaginative; technical words, such as eucalypt → Informative; headings and facts → Informative; the writer's opinion, with reasons → Persuasive; a call to act: Write to the council today → Persuasive

<!-- Speaker note: Each feature helps a text do its job: stories need characters, information needs facts, and persuasion needs an opinion the reader can be won over to. -->

## Report or explanation?

**Is each sentence from a report (what something is like) or an explanation (how or why something happens)?**

Groups: Report · Explanation

Volcanoes are openings in the Earth's crust where melted rock comes out. · Some volcanoes are tall cones, and others are wide and low. · When gas builds up underground, it pushes melted rock upwards. · As a result, the volcano erupts.

* Answer: Volcanoes are openings in the Earth's crust where melted rock comes out. → Report; Some volcanoes are tall cones, and others are wide and low. → Report; When gas builds up underground, it pushes melted rock upwards. → Explanation; As a result, the volcano erupts. → Explanation

<!-- Speaker note: A report describes and classifies: what something is and what it is like. An explanation follows a process or a cause: how or why something happens. -->

* Answer: line 3

## Exit ticket

**'Lightning is about five times hotter than the surface of the sun.' Imaginative, informative or persuasive, and how do you know?**

* Informative. It's a fact with no opinion or story: no characters, no should or must.
