# Every text has a plan

How texts are built · Warm-up: stages and features

<!-- Speaker note: An optional warm-up from Years 4 and 5. Students learn that a text is built in stages that each do a job, using the recount: orientation (who, where, when), events in time order, and an ending. They label the stages, put a jumbled recount back in order, find the stages inside an email, and write and label their own recount. About 40 minutes (Predict and Try 8 min · Worked example 10 min · Vary 8 min · Practise 5 min · Make and own writing 9 min). -->

## Today

- I can name the stages of a recount and say what each one does.
- I can put a recount's events in time order.
- I can tell a text's form (an email, a speech) from its stages.
- I can write a recount with all three stages.

## Why it matters

> A friend starts telling you about their weekend with 'Then we went home.' What's missing?

<!-- Speaker note: A text is built in **stages**, and each stage does a job for the reader. A **recount** retells something that happened, in three stages: - **Orientation:** who, where and when. - **Events:** what happened, in time order. - **Ending:** how it finished, or what the writer thought of it. -->

## Think, pair, share

**Why do so many stories start by saying who, where and when?**

- What happens to a listener who doesn't know where a story took place?
- Can you think of a story that starts in the middle? Does it explain the scene later?

<!-- Speaker note: The orientation lets the reader picture the events. Some stories start in the middle on purpose to hook the reader, but they still give the orientation soon after. Give 30 seconds alone, 2 minutes in pairs, then take answers from both sides. There is no single right answer: ask for a reason, and let students change their minds. -->

## Finding the stages

Now: what is the orientation's job?

$$\text{Ask what the text is for}$$

1) $\text{Find the orientation}$ — Who was there, where and when. It sets the scene.
2) $\text{Find the events}$ — What happened, in time order. Time words (First, Next, After lunch) mark them.
3) $\text{Find the ending}$ — How it finished, or what the writer thought of it.

## Finding the stages

**What is the orientation's job?**

- To list everything that happened
- To say how it all ended
- To give the writer's opinion of the day
- To tell the reader who, where and when

* Answer: To tell the reader who, where and when

<!-- Speaker note: The orientation sets the scene, so the reader can picture the events that follow. -->

## Find Nib's slip

**Here is Nib's recount, one sentence per line. Click the line where the reader needed an orientation but didn't get one.**

```
 1  Then we ate our lunch next to the lake.
 2  After that, we fed the ducks some oats.
 3  Later, it started to rain, so we ran to the car.
 4  It was a great day, even with the rain.
```

* Answer: line 1

<!-- Speaker note: Line 1: Nib starts with Then, in the middle of the day. The reader doesn't know who went, where the lake is or when it happened. Nib needs an orientation first, such as 'On Saturday, my class went to the lake for a picnic.' -->

## Form or stage?

**Is each one a form (what the text looks like) or a stage (a part with a job)?**

Groups: Form · Stage

an email · a speech · a poster · the orientation · the events · the ending

* Answer: an email → Form; a speech → Form; a poster → Form; the orientation → Stage; the events → Stage; the ending → Stage

<!-- Speaker note: The form is what a text looks like or how it is delivered. The stages are the parts inside it, each with a job. A recount keeps its stages whether it's an email, a speech or a story told at dinner. -->

**'During the interval, we bought ice creams in the foyer.' Which stage is this most likely to be?**

## Exit ticket

**Which stage is this, and how do you know? 'On Monday morning, our class planted twenty trees along the creek.'**

* The orientation. It tells you who (our class), where (along the creek) and when (Monday morning).
