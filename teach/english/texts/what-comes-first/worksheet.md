# What comes first

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Most people read only the first line or two of a news story. If you wrote the story, what would you put there?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn that where an idea sits shapes how much it matters, and that a structure can also hide things. They put a news report's main point first and background last, catch Nib burying the main point, ask what a one-sided newsletter leaves out, label and reorder news items, and judge which order of the same facts suits which reader (an unmarked answer compared with models).

**Students should be able to say:** I can put the main point first for a reader in a hurry. I can say how the order of a text changes what a reader notices. I can ask what a text leaves out.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

☐ The number with the dollar sign

☐ Both facts equally

☐ Whichever fact comes first

☐ Whichever fact comes last

My prediction, and why:

:::teacher-note

**What happens:** Whichever comes first. Readers give the opening the most attention, and many stop reading soon after. The writer's choice of what goes first shapes what readers take away.

## Worked example: what the order does


:::keep-together

### The order is a choice

1. $\text{Find what comes first}$ — That is what the writer wants you to notice most.
2. $\text{Find what comes last}$ — That gets the least attention, and quick readers may miss it.
3. $\text{Ask what is left out}$ — What would someone on the other side want you to know?
4. $\text{Ask who it suits}$ — Who gains from this order?

Now: why does news lead with the main point?

**Why do news reports usually put the main point first?**

☐ Background is boring, so it goes last

☐ It is a rule that every text must follow

☐ The main point is always the shortest sentence

☐ Many readers stop early, so they still get the news

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Many readers stop early, so they still get the news

News is built for readers who may stop at any point. Putting the main point first means even a quick reader leaves with it.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find Nib's slip

**Here is Nib's report on the school fete, one sentence per line. Click the line a reader in a hurry needs first, but Nib left to the end.**

```
 1  Our fete committee met six times this year to plan the day.
 2  There were 25 stalls, including a cake stall and a dunk tank.
 3  Mrs Patel's lemon slice sold out by ten o'clock.
 4  In total, the fete raised $8,000 for new playground equipment.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 4

Line 4: the $8,000 raised is the news, but a reader who stops after two lines never reaches it. Nib should open with it, then the stalls and the lemon slice as details.

:::


:::keep-together

### What's left out?

**A canteen newsletter lists three good things about the new menu: more salads, less sugar and shorter queues. It doesn't mention that prices went up. What does this structure do?**

☐ It gives a fair and balanced report of the new menu

☐ It shows one side, so readers may miss the price rise

☐ Nothing, because every fact in it is true

☐ It retells the canteen's year as a recount

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** It shows one side, so readers may miss the price rise

What a structure leaves out shapes meaning as much as what it puts first. A careful reader asks: what isn't here?
