# What comes first

How texts are built · Organising ideas

<!-- Speaker note: Students learn that where an idea sits shapes how much it matters, and that a structure can also hide things. They put a news report's main point first and background last, catch Nib burying the main point, ask what a one-sided newsletter leaves out, label and reorder news items, and judge which order of the same facts suits which reader (an unmarked answer compared with models). ## Why it matters

> Most people read only the first line or two of a news story. If you wrote the story, what would you put there?

<!-- Speaker note: Where a writer puts an idea changes how much it **matters** to the reader. What comes first gets the spotlight. A **news report** is built for readers in a hurry: - **Main point** first: what happened, or what is changing. - **Details** next: when, where, how many. - **Background** last: how it came about. The order can also hide things. What comes last, or is left out, gets less attention. -->

## Predict

**Two news reports use the same facts about a new school bus. One starts with the 15 minutes it saves. The other starts with the $40,000 a year it costs. Which fact will most readers of each report remember?**

- The number with the dollar sign
- Both facts equally
- Whichever fact comes first
- Whichever fact comes last

<!-- Speaker note: What happens: Whichever comes first. Readers give the opening the most attention, and many stop reading soon after. The writer's choice of what goes first shapes what readers take away. Take two or three predictions before anyone runs it. -->

## The order is a choice

Now: why does news lead with the main point?

$$\text{Find what comes first}$$

1) $\text{Find what comes last}$ — That gets the least attention, and quick readers may miss it.
2) $\text{Ask what is left out}$ — What would someone on the other side want you to know?
3) $\text{Ask who it suits}$ — Who gains from this order?

## The order is a choice

**Why do news reports usually put the main point first?**

- Background is boring, so it goes last
- It is a rule that every text must follow
- The main point is always the shortest sentence
- Many readers stop early, so they still get the news

* Answer: Many readers stop early, so they still get the news

<!-- Speaker note: News is built for readers who may stop at any point. Putting the main point first means even a quick reader leaves with it. -->

## Find Nib's slip

**Here is Nib's report on the school fete, one sentence per line. Click the line a reader in a hurry needs first, but Nib left to the end.**

```
 1  Our fete committee met six times this year to plan the day.
 2  There were 25 stalls, including a cake stall and a dunk tank.
 3  Mrs Patel's lemon slice sold out by ten o'clock.
 4  In total, the fete raised $8,000 for new playground equipment.
```

* Answer: line 4

<!-- Speaker note: Line 4: the $8,000 raised is the news, but a reader who stops after two lines never reaches it. Nib should open with it, then the stalls and the lemon slice as details. -->

## What's left out?

**A canteen newsletter lists three good things about the new menu: more salads, less sugar and shorter queues. It doesn't mention that prices went up. What does this structure do?**

- It gives a fair and balanced report of the new menu
- It shows one side, so readers may miss the price rise
- Nothing, because every fact in it is true
- It retells the canteen's year as a recount

* Answer: It shows one side, so readers may miss the price rise

<!-- Speaker note: What a structure leaves out shapes meaning as much as what it puts first. A careful reader asks: what isn't here? -->

## Main point?

**Which sentence should lead a news item? A: 'The hall was built in 1975.' B: 'The hall will be closed next week for repairs.'**

- A

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Labelling a news item that is already in news order shows the three parts and why each sits where it does. Hints, in order: Click a sentence, then choose Main point, Detail or Background. / Background says how it came about. Details add facts about the news. For students who need more support: Three labels on a card: Main point (the news) · Details (when, where, how many) · Background (how it came about). For early finishers: Find a real news story. Rewrite its first sentence so a different fact leads. How does the story feel now? The hard part, on purpose: Students accept the order a text gives them. The canteen newsletter task is the turning point: every fact is true, yet the reader is steered. Ask: who gains from this order? When students get stuck: A student puts the background first because 'that's where it started'. Ask: Is this a story told in time order, or news for a reader in a hurry? Fix: A recount goes in time order. News goes in order of importance. / A student labels a detail as the main point because it has a number in it. Ask: Would the sentence make sense to someone who hadn't heard the news yet? Fix: The main point is the news itself. Details only make sense after it. / A student's Make answer only says which version is better. Ask: Better for whom? And what does it push into the background? Fix: Use the self-check: a version for each reader, why, and what each one hides. -->
