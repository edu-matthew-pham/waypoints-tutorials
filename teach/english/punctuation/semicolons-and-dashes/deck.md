# Semicolons and dashes

Punctuation for meaning · Marks that change meaning

<!-- Speaker note: Students learn that a semicolon joins two closely related main clauses, that a pair of dashes sets off an aside and that a single dash adds a pause or a surprise. They label each mark's job, sort joins that work from joins that need fixing, spot two sentences stuck together with a comma, build a sentence with a semicolon, choose marks and write a short description. About 45 minutes (Predict and Try 8 min · Worked example 12 min · Vary 5 min · Practise 6 min · Make in their own document 14 min). -->

## Today

- I can join two related main clauses with a semicolon.
- I can tell when a comma alone is not enough.
- I can use a pair of dashes for an aside and a single dash for a pause or surprise.
- I can check my own semicolons and dashes.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Try board yourself: six sentences, label the mark. The one idea: each mark depends on what stands on each side, and each sets a tone. This lesson covers AC9E8LA09 nodes 1 and 2. The usual name for this fault is a **comma splice**; the lesson says 'two sentences stuck together with a comma' and the misconception is filed as comma-splice. A full stop is also correct for two main clauses. The boards avoid it as an option, so no item depends on it. A semicolon is a choice about how closely the ideas belong. Dashes: the lesson uses a spaced en dash (–), as the Australian Style Manual does. Typing it takes a shortcut on most devices; a spaced hyphen ( - ) is fine in the Make step, which students write in their own document and a partner or you check. A pair of dashes, a pair of brackets and a pair of commas can all hold an aside. The last lesson taught brackets as the quiet one, dashes as the emphatic one. The sentences are made up for the lesson. Teacher: Try: read a sentence aloud with each mark and listen to the difference. Teacher: Worked example: for each sort item, say each side aloud as its own sentence. Teacher: Vary: say what the comma version does to the sentence. -->

## Why it matters

> 'It was quiet – too quiet.' What does the dash make you do as you read?

<!-- Speaker note: A **semicolon** (;) joins two **main clauses**, each of which could stand alone, when the ideas are closely related: - *The lights went out; the whole street went quiet.* A comma alone cannot join them. (*The lights went out, the whole street went quiet.* is two sentences stuck together.) A **dash** (–) works in two ways: - **A pair of dashes** sets off an aside, with more emphasis than brackets: *My cousin – the one from Perth – is visiting.* - **A single dash** adds a pause, or a surprise: *The door opened – and nobody was there.* -->

## Is it joined correctly?

$$\text{Read each side on its own}$$

1) $\text{If both could}$ — A semicolon can join them, if the ideas are closely related.
2) $\text{If one could not}$ — Use a comma, or no mark, instead.

## Which pieces can a semicolon join?

**Which two pieces could a semicolon join?**

- 'Because it rained' and 'we stayed in'
- 'When the bell rang' and 'the class lined up'

## Which join?

**Which sentence joins its two clauses correctly?**

- The wind rose; the boats came in.
- The wind rose, the boats came in.
- The wind rose; so the boats came in.

* Answer: The wind rose; the boats came in.

## Make: describe a place

In your own document, describe a place you know well, such as a beach, a playground or a street. Write at least four sentences. Use a semicolon between two related sentences, and a pair of dashes for an aside or a single dash for a surprise. Use a spaced en dash (–), or a spaced hyphen ( - ) if that is easier to type.

Here is one example: "The beach at dawn is quiet. The waves are small**;** the sand is cold and smooth. A single gull **–** the only one in sight **–** circles above the rocks. Then the sun rises **–** and everything glows."

Then swap with a partner. Check that each side of the semicolon could stand alone, and say what each dash does.
