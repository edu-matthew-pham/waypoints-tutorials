# Colons and brackets

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** What does a colon promise the reader that a comma does not?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn that a colon follows a complete main clause and introduces a list, an explanation or a quotation, and that brackets hold extra information a sentence could do without. They label colon uses, sort colons that belong from colons that split a clause, spot a misplaced colon, test bracketed parts, build a bracketed sentence and write an information note.

**Students should be able to say:** I can use a colon after a complete clause to introduce a list or an explanation. I can leave a colon out when the words before it are not a sentence. I can put extra information in brackets. I can test a colon or brackets in my own writing.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: does the colon belong?


:::keep-together

### Does the colon belong?

1. $\text{Cover the colon and what follows}$ — Is what is left a whole sentence?
2. $\text{If it is}$ — The colon can introduce a list, an explanation or a quotation.
3. $\text{If it is not}$ — A colon splits the clause. Leave it out.

**Does the colon in each sentence belong?**

Groups: The colon belongs, The colon does not belong

- Ravi had one goal: to finish the track.: {{field-15}}
- We stayed home because: it was raining.: {{field-15}}
- Ava bought three things: bread, milk and eggs.: {{field-15}}
- She said that: the bus was late.: {{field-15}}
- The result was clear: our team had won.: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Ravi had one goal: to finish the track. → The colon belongs; We stayed home because: it was raining. → The colon does not belong; Ava bought three things: bread, milk and eggs. → The colon belongs; She said that: the bus was late. → The colon does not belong; The result was clear: our team had won. → The colon belongs

:::


:::keep-together

### Spot the misplaced colon

**Here is Nib's excursion note, one sentence per line. One line has a colon in the middle of a clause. Click it.**

```
 1  Our class will visit the museum on Friday.
 2  Please bring one thing: a drink bottle.
 3  We are going to the museum because: it opens early on Fridays.
 4  Remember the rule: stay with your group.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3 has a colon after 'because', so the colon splits the clause. The words before it are not a sentence on their own.

:::


:::keep-together

### Take out the brackets

**Which sentence stops making sense when you take out the bracketed part?**

☐ The race (was cancelled) because of rain.

☐ Ava (our captain) won the toss.

☐ The cave (about two hours away) is huge.

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** The race (was cancelled) because of rain.

Without the bracketed part, 'The race because of rain.' is not a sentence. Brackets hold extra information, not words the sentence needs.

## Colons in a notice


## Make: an information note

In your own document, write a short note for families about an excursion or an event. Write at least four sentences. Use a colon to introduce what to bring, and brackets for extra detail such as a time or a place.

Here is one example: "Our class excursion is on Friday. Please bring three things**:** a hat, a drink bottle and sunscreen. The bus **(**booked by Mr Lee**)** leaves at nine. We will return by three."

Then swap with a partner. Cover the colon and its list, and check that a whole sentence comes before it. Then cover the bracketed part and check that what is left still makes sense.

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?
