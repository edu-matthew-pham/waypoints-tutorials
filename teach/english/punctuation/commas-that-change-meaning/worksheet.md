# Commas that change meaning

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** 'Let's eat Grandma.' One comma decides whether it is dinner or a disaster.


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn three jobs for a comma: separating items in a list, ending an opening clause, and introducing speech. They also learn that a comma is not a breath mark. They label each job, sort commas that belong from commas that do not, spot a comma that splits a subject from its verb, choose commas on boards and write a short recount.

**Students should be able to say:** I can name three jobs for a comma. I can put a comma after an opening clause. I can leave a comma out between a subject and its verb. I can check the commas in my own writing.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: does the comma belong?


:::keep-together

### Does the comma belong?

1. $\text{Find the structure}$ — Is it a list, an opening clause, or speech?
2. $\text{If it is one of those}$ — A comma belongs there.
3. $\text{If it is not}$ — A comma splits the sentence for no reason.

**Does the comma in each sentence belong?**

Groups: The comma belongs, The comma does not belong

- When the rain stopped, we climbed out of the tent.: {{field-15}}
- We knew, that the creek was high.: {{field-15}}
- Ben said, 'Follow me.': {{field-15}}
- We climbed the steep, hill.: {{field-15}}
- Mia packed a torch, a map and a rope.: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** When the rain stopped, we climbed out of the tent. → The comma belongs; We knew, that the creek was high. → The comma does not belong; Ben said, 'Follow me.' → The comma belongs; We climbed the steep, hill. → The comma does not belong; Mia packed a torch, a map and a rope. → The comma belongs

:::


:::keep-together

### Spot the stray comma

**Here is Nib's excursion diary, one sentence per line. One line has a comma that splits a sentence in the wrong place. Click it.**

```
 1  We arrived at the museum at ten.
 2  After a short talk, we split into groups.
 3  Our guide, pointed at the dinosaur skeleton.
 4  Ravi asked, 'How old is it?'
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3 has a comma between the subject, 'Our guide', and its verb, 'pointed'. A subject and its verb stay together. Nib put the comma where the voice pauses.

:::


:::keep-together

### What is a comma for?

**Which is the best rule for where a comma goes?**

☐ After every fourth or fifth word, so that no sentence runs on for too long

☐ Wherever you would pause to breathe when you read the sentence aloud to a friend

☐ Where the structure calls for one: list items, opening clauses and speech

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Where the structure calls for one: list items, opening clauses and speech

Commas follow the structure of the sentence. Pausing to breathe is not a reason, because people pause in different places.

## Commas in a recount


## Make: a recount of an excursion

In your own document, write a short recount of an excursion or an event at school. Write at least four sentences with at least three commas. Include something you packed or saw as a list, a sentence that starts with a clause like 'When we arrived', and something someone said, in speech marks.

Here is one example: "On Friday our class walked to the creek. When we arrived**,** Mia unpacked torches**,** maps and rope. 'Stay together**,**' said our teacher. After a short rest**,** we headed back to school."

Then swap with a partner. Find each comma and say which job it does: list, opening clause or speech.

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?
