# Commas that change meaning

Punctuation for meaning · Marks that change meaning

<!-- Speaker note: Students learn three jobs for a comma: separating items in a list, ending an opening clause, and introducing speech. They also learn that a comma is not a breath mark. They label each job, sort commas that belong from commas that do not, spot a comma that splits a subject from its verb, choose commas on boards and write a short recount. About 45 minutes (Predict and Try 8 min · Worked example 12 min · Vary 5 min · Practise 6 min · Make in their own document 14 min). -->

## Today

- I can name three jobs for a comma.
- I can put a comma after an opening clause.
- I can leave a comma out between a subject and its verb.
- I can check the commas in my own writing.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do the Try board yourself: six sentences, label the job. The one idea: a comma has a job in the structure of the sentence. It is not a breath mark. This lesson covers AC9E6LA09 nodes 1 and 2. The hook, 'Let's eat, Grandma', shows a fourth job: marking the person being spoken to (direct address). The lesson names three jobs; you can name the fourth in discussion. The lesson avoids the comma before the last 'and' in a list (the Oxford comma). Australian style usually leaves it out, but some writers use it. No board item depends on it. The comma after an opening clause is not usually needed when the clause comes last. The lesson sticks to the opening position, so there is no debate. The sentences are made up for the lesson. Teacher: Try: ask 'what would happen without the comma?' Teacher: Worked example: for each comma in the sort, ask 'what is the job?'. If nobody can say, it does not belong. Teacher: Vary: say each sentence aloud first, then name the structure. -->

---

## Why it matters

> 'Let's eat Grandma.' One comma decides whether it is dinner or a disaster.

<!-- Speaker note: A comma has a few clear jobs: - **In a list:** it stands in for *and* between items. *We saw emus, kangaroos and wombats.* - **After an opening clause:** a clause that cannot stand alone and starts the sentence is followed by a comma. *If it rains, the picnic moves inside.* - **With speech:** a comma comes between the words that introduce speech and the speech itself. *Ava said, 'Come this way.'* A comma is **not** a breath mark. You cannot put one wherever you would pause when reading aloud. A comma between the subject and its verb (*The bus, was late.*) splits a sentence in a place where it should stay whole. -->

## Does the comma belong?

$$\text{Find the structure}$$

## Does the comma belong?

**Does the comma in each sentence belong?**

Groups: The comma belongs · The comma does not belong

When the rain stopped, we climbed out of the tent. · We knew, that the creek was high. · Ben said, 'Follow me.' · We climbed the steep, hill. · Mia packed a torch, a map and a rope.

* Answer: When the rain stopped, we climbed out of the tent. → The comma belongs; We knew, that the creek was high. → The comma does not belong; Ben said, 'Follow me.' → The comma belongs; We climbed the steep, hill. → The comma does not belong; Mia packed a torch, a map and a rope. → The comma belongs


## What is a comma for?

**Which is the best rule for where a comma goes?**

- After every fourth or fifth word, so that no sentence runs on for too long
- Wherever you would pause to breathe when you read the sentence aloud to a friend
- Where the structure calls for one: list items, opening clauses and speech

* Answer: Where the structure calls for one: list items, opening clauses and speech

<!-- Speaker note: Commas follow the structure of the sentence. Pausing to breathe is not a reason, because people pause in different places. -->

## Make: a recount of an excursion

In your own document, write a short recount of an excursion or an event at school. Write at least four sentences with at least three commas. Include something you packed or saw as a list, a sentence that starts with a clause like 'When we arrived', and something someone said, in speech marks.

Here is one example: "On Friday our class walked to the creek. When we arrived**,** Mia unpacked torches**,** maps and rope. 'Stay together**,**' said our teacher. After a short rest**,** we headed back to school."

Then swap with a partner. Find each comma and say which job it does: list, opening clause or speech.
