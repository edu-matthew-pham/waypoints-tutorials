# Apostrophes and speech marks

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Same letters, different meaning: one dog or two? What can an apostrophe tell you?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn that an apostrophe either stands in for missing letters or shows who owns something, and that speech marks wrap spoken words with the closing punctuation inside. They choose words with apostrophes, match contractions, catch a stray apostrophe, choose speech punctuation, sort out its and it's and write a short scene with talk.

**Students should be able to say:** I can say what an apostrophe is doing in a word. I can put the apostrophe in the right place for one owner and for more than one. I can tell a plain plural from an owner. I can punctuate speech, including a said tag.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: what the apostrophe is doing


:::keep-together

### Two jobs for an apostrophe

1. $\text{Ask what the apostrophe is for}$ — Letters left out, or an owner?
2. $\text{Count the owners}$ — One owner, apostrophe before the s. More than one, after the s.
3. $\text{Check a plain plural}$ — A word that just means more than one has no apostrophe.

**Match each contraction to the words it stands in for.**

A. we will

B. let us

C. is not

D. they are

{{columns 60% 40%}}

{{rows 1}}

| Part | Letter |
|---|---|
| `they're` | |
| `we'll` | |
| `isn't` | |
| `let's` | |

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** they're → D. they are; we'll → A. we will; isn't → C. is not; let's → B. let us

In each one the apostrophe sits where letters were left out.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find the stray apostrophe

**Here is Nib's trip report, one sentence per line. One line has an apostrophe where it does not belong. Click it.**

```
 1  We visited the zoo on Friday.
 2  Max's group saw two giraffe's near the gate.
 3  The keepers' van was parked outside.
 4  It was a day we won't forget.
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 2

Line 2: 'giraffe's' has an apostrophe, but it just means more than one giraffe. Plain 'giraffes' is right.

:::


:::keep-together

### Where does the comma go?

**'We can start now,' said Mia. Where does the comma go, and why?**

☐ After said, because that is where the speaker is named in the sentence

☐ No comma is needed, because said Mia already tells the reader who spoke

☐ Outside the closing speech mark, because the whole sentence carries on after it

☐ Inside the closing speech mark, because it ends the spoken words

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Inside the closing speech mark, because it ends the spoken words

The comma ends the spoken words, so it sits inside the closing speech mark. Then the speaker is named in lower case.

:::

## Owners and missing letters


## Make: a scene with talk

In your own document, write a short scene from a story with two characters talking, at a bus stop or a campsite. Write at least four sentences. Put at least one piece of speech in speech marks, and use at least two apostrophes.

Here is one example: "The bus was late again. 'I **can't** wait any longer,' said Zoe. Her **brother's** bag sat on the bench beside her. '**We'll** walk,' he said, 'if it **doesn't** rain.'"

Then swap with a partner. Point to each apostrophe and say its job: letters left out, or an owner. Check that every spoken part ends inside its closing speech mark.

Work in the tutorial. Plan first: what will you change, and how will you test it?
