# Challenge: punctuate the newsletter

Punctuation for meaning · Challenge: punctuate the newsletter

<!-- Speaker note: Students put the punctuation back into Nib's school fair newsletter in four parts: apostrophes, commas, colons and semicolons, and then a whole paragraph. They finish by checking the commas in a piece of their own writing. About 50 minutes (Predict 3 min · Parts 1 to 3 18 min · Part 4 12 min · Own writing 10 min · Wider 7 min). -->

---

## Today

- I can use apostrophes for owners and short forms.
- I can use commas for lists, opening clauses and speech.
- I can use a colon or a semicolon where a whole clause stands on each side.
- I can find and fix a comma problem in my own writing.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: Do Parts 1 to 3 yourself and punctuate the Part 4 paragraph. > A newsletter with every fact right and not one mark of punctuation. Can you make it readable again?

## Think, pair, share

**How do you know a mark belongs?**

- Pick a mark in your paragraph. What is its job?
- What would the reader lose if it were missing?

<!-- Speaker note: Every mark has a job in the structure. If it has none, take it out. Give 30 seconds alone, 2 minutes in pairs, then take answers from both sides. There is no single right answer: ask for a reason, and let students change their minds. -->

## Predict

**Nib's newsletter has a list, an opening clause and a quotation. Which one mark do all three of those need?**

- The comma
- The apostrophe
- The semicolon
- The colon

<!-- Speaker note: What happens: The comma. It separates list items, follows an opening clause and comes before the quotation. The colon and the semicolon each do one of these jobs at most, and the apostrophe does none of them. Take two or three predictions before anyone runs it. -->

## Make: Part 4: punctuate a whole paragraph

Copy this paragraph into your own document. It has no punctuation and no capital letters after the first word, so you will add them.

> The school fair is on Saturday Mrs Tran our principal will open it at ten When the gates open the first stall to fill will be the cake stall Last year the cakes sold out by noon this year we are baking more Ravi said we will need three things flour eggs and sugar

Punctuate it. Put in the capital letters and full stops first. Then add at least four commas, a colon and speech marks around what Ravi said.

Here is one model, so you can compare after you have tried: "The school fair is on Saturday. Mrs Tran, our principal, will open it at ten. When the gates open, the first stall to fill will be the cake stall. Last year the cakes sold out by noon; this year we are baking more. Ravi said, 'We will need three things: flour, eggs and sugar.'"

Then swap with a partner. Point to every mark and say its job. Where you chose differently, talk about which version reads better.

<!-- Speaker note: What this step is for: Punctuating a whole paragraph makes students decide each mark in context, as they do in extended writing. Hints, in order: Where does each sentence end? Put the capital letters and full stops in first. / Read it aloud. Where does your voice stop, and where does a reader need help to follow the meaning? For students who need more support: Ask students to punctuate one sentence at a time and read it aloud before moving to the next. For early finishers: Swap your punctuated paragraph with a partner who used dashes where you used commas. Whose version reads better, and why? The hard part, on purpose: In Part 4 students add marks everywhere, or only full stops. Ask them to name each comma's job aloud. If they cannot, they take the comma out. When students get stuck: A student puts a comma after 'Ravi' in 'Ravi said we will need...' Ask: What is the comma for? Fix: The comma comes after 'said', before the speech. Between a subject and its verb there is none. / A student leaves out the colon after 'three things'. Ask: Is 'Ravi said we will need three things' a whole clause? What does it promise? Fix: The clause promises a list. A colon introduces it. -->

## Which is best?

**Which sentence is punctuated best?**

- Ravi said 'We need, flour, eggs and sugar.'
- Ravi said, 'We need flour, eggs and sugar.'
- Ravi said; 'We need flour eggs and sugar.'

* Answer: Ravi said, 'We need flour, eggs and sugar.'

<!-- Speaker note: A comma comes before the speech and between the first items of the list. Ask on mini whiteboards or by show of hands before the reveal. -->
