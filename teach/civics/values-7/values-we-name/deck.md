## Today

**We are learning to** identify values often named as Australian and explain how they are expressed in public life.

- I can name values often called Australian and say what they mean.
- I can give an example of how a value shows up in public life.
- I can explain why these values aren't Australia's alone.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: The values are taught as values *often named* as Australian (by ACARA and in the Department of Home Affairs' list of Australian values, which visa applicants agree to respect; the citizenship pledge names a smaller set: democratic beliefs, rights and liberties, and the law), not as values only Australians hold, and not as a claim that every Australian lives up to them. That second question is the next lesson's hinge. Nothing personal is asked. No step asks a student's background, beliefs or family, or how their family marks national days. Some students and families have different feelings about national symbols (the anthem, the flag, 26 January); the lesson states facts about the symbols and keeps views for discussion. Don't ask students to declare a stance on any symbol. Facts: Advance Australia Fair became the national anthem on 19 April 1984, and on 1 January 2021 one word changed ("young" to "one") in the second line (Parliamentary Education Office). The citizenship pledge has two versions, one with "under God" and one without (Department of Home Affairs). The student text says "one" is about unity and leaves the reasons for the change to you; if you discuss them, note the change was linked to recognising First Nations peoples' long history, and keep to what was said at the time. Wattle Creek's ceremony is made up; the pledge and anthem are real. Teacher: Pledge: read it aloud. Ask which words name a value. Teacher: Vary: the equal vote is a good example of a value shown through how an institution works. -->

## Why it matters

> If you had to name three values that hold Australia together, what would they be? Would people in other countries name the same ones?

<!-- Speaker note: A **value** is an idea about what matters and how people should treat each other. Some values are often named as Australian, by the Australian Curriculum and in the government's list of Australian values (from the Department of Home Affairs): - **freedom**: people can speak, meet, believe and live as they choose, within the law; - **respect**: treating others, and their views and beliefs, with consideration; - **fairness** and a **fair go**: everyone deserves a fair chance; - **equality of opportunity**: the same chances, whoever you are; - **compassion**: caring about people who are struggling; - **inclusion**: making sure people are part of things, not left out; - **religious freedom**: people can follow any religion, or none. These values are named as Australian, but they aren't Australia's alone. Many other democracies name similar ones. And naming a value isn't the same as everyone living up to it; the next lesson looks at that. In this lesson, Wattle Creek and its ceremony are made up. The pledge and the anthem are real. -->

## A value or a law?

**Is a value the same as a law?**

- No, a value is an idea; laws can reflect values
- Yes, you can be fined for not holding a value
- No, values and laws have nothing to do with each other
- Yes, every value is written in the Constitution

* Answer: No, a value is an idea; laws can reflect values
