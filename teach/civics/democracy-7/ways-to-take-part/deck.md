## Today

**We are learning to** compare formal and informal ways citizens participate in Australia's democracy and match them to civic goals.

- I can describe at least three different ways citizens can participate in Australia's democracy.
- I can distinguish between parliamentary/electoral channels and community/public action.
- I can explain why one method of participation is more effective than another for a specific goal.

<!-- Speaker note: Before class: This lesson builds on the five democratic freedoms (Lesson 1) and their legal bounds (Lesson 2). It shows how citizens put those freedoms to work in practice. The facts about House of Representatives petitions in the Typical step come from the Parliamentary Education Office and APH: a petition that meets the rules is considered by the Petitions Committee and presented to the House. If the Committee refers it to a minister, the minister is expected to respond within 90 days of presentation (House Standing Order 209; checked against the House's Guide to Procedures, 1 Nov 2026). Not every petition is referred, so the student text says "may be sent to a minister". Senate petitions follow different rules. The committee inquiry in the worked example (pool safety) is made up and labelled so; it avoids echoing any real recent inquiry. This lesson also covers Civics' skill standard AC9HC7S04 (methods and strategies for civic participation), nodes n1 (features of participation methods) and n3 (choosing a method for a goal). The standard asks students to explain methods, so it is taught here as knowledge. Taking real civic action is your call, outside the site: for example, a class petition or submission to the student council on a school issue the class chooses. Teacher: Try: pause on 'committee of inquiry' — many Year 7s have heard of protests and voting, but don't realise ordinary citizens and school classes can send submissions to parliamentary committees. ## Why it matters

> An election happens only once every few years. If a town problem needs fixing this month, what can citizens actually do?

<!-- Speaker note: Because Australia has the five democratic freedoms you met in Lesson 1, democratic participation is not limited to election day. Citizens can take part in two broad, connected ways: - **Through electoral and parliamentary channels:** **voting** in elections and referendums, **standing for parliament** (or council), signing or presenting a **petition** to Parliament, contacting a local member of parliament (MP), or writing a submission to a **parliamentary committee of inquiry**. - **Through community and public action:** joining **community organisations**, **volunteering** (such as bushcare, surf lifesaving or a food pantry), running a **social enterprise**, running **social media or public awareness campaigns**, or joining a **peaceful protest**. -->

## Choosing the right tool for the goal

**In a made-up example, a Senate committee is looking into a proposed law on safety at public swimming pools and asks the public for submissions. Why could a youth swimming club's submission be worth sending?**

- It only helps if the club also gets thousands of people to sign it
- It is worth sending, but only adults and experts are allowed to send one
- The senators read the evidence and can recommend changes before the vote
- Once the committee receives it, the club's ideas become part of the law

* Answer: The senators read the evidence and can recommend changes before the vote

<!-- Speaker note: When a committee is looking at a bill in detail, a clear submission puts evidence straight in front of the lawmakers before they vote. -->

## Comparing two methods

**An online campaign can quickly show that many people care. Why is it often weaker for fixing the detail of a law?**

- If enough people share it, Parliament has to vote on it
- Online campaigns are not a real way of taking part in democracy
- MPs do not pay attention to anything posted online
- Short posts rarely explain the evidence or exact changes a law needs

* Answer: Short posts rarely explain the evidence or exact changes a law needs

<!-- Speaker note: Each method has strengths and limits: online campaigns spread awareness fast, while submissions and local projects work on detailed solutions. -->

## Matching method to goal

**Which participation method puts detailed written evidence directly in front of MPs and senators who are examining a proposed law?**

- A submission to a parliamentary committee of inquiry
- A petition with thousands of signatures
- A peaceful rally outside Parliament House
- An online campaign that is shared widely

* Answer: A submission to a parliamentary committee of inquiry

## Combining two methods

**In a made-up town, residents want (1) help clearing storm branches from older neighbours' yards now, and (2) better state funding for storm drains next year. What is the best plan?**

- Start an online campaign about both and wait for a response
- Petition the state MP about both, since the government should clear the yards
- Volunteer for both, since a strong local effort will fix the drains too
- Volunteer for the clean-up now, and petition or meet the state MP about drains

* Answer: Volunteer for the clean-up now, and petition or meet the state MP about drains

<!-- Speaker note: Volunteering meets the need straight away, while a petition or meeting with the MP reaches the people who decide on funding. -->

## Participating before turning 18

**Can people under 18 participate in Australia's democracy before they are old enough to vote?**

- No: taking part in democracy starts when you can vote at 18
- Yes: they can vote in council and state elections from 16
- Yes: they can write to MPs, sign petitions, volunteer and join campaigns
- Only by volunteering, because writing to MPs is for voters

* Answer: Yes: they can write to MPs, sign petitions, volunteer and join campaigns

<!-- Speaker note: Voting starts at 18, but almost every other way of taking part in democracy is open to young people now. -->
