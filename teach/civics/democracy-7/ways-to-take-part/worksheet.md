# Ways to take part in democracy

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** An election happens only once every few years. If a town problem needs fixing this month, what can citizens actually do?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students identify and compare formal and informal ways citizens participate in Australia's democracy — including voting, standing for parliament, petitions, committee inquiries, volunteering, community organisations, social enterprise, campaigns and peaceful protest — and evaluate which methods fit different civic goals.

**Students should be able to say:** I can describe at least three different ways citizens can participate in Australia's democracy. I can distinguish between parliamentary/electoral channels and community/public action. I can explain why one method of participation is more effective than another for a specific goal.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: matching the method to the goal


:::keep-together

### Choosing the right tool for the goal

1. $\text{Goal: fix creek rubbish this Saturday}$ — Join a local bushcare working bee -> Volunteering & community organisation.
2. $\text{Goal: show Parliament 5,000 locals care}$ — Collect signatures on an official e-petition -> Petitioning Parliament.
3. $\text{Goal: improve the wording of a new bill}$ — Send evidence and recommendations to the inquiry -> Committee of inquiry submission.
4. $\text{Goal: choose who represents the electorate}$ — Cast a ballot on election day (or stand as a candidate) -> Voting & elections.

Now: why is a committee submission effective when a bill is being examined, where does Nib go wrong, and how do different methods compare?

**A Senate committee is holding an inquiry into a proposed law on teen online safety and has asked the public for submissions. Why is writing a submission to the committee an effective way for a youth organisation to participate here?**

☐ A committee submission automatically becomes a binding law as soon as the committee receives it

☐ The senators examining the bill read the evidence and can recommend changes to the law before Parliament votes on it

☐ Committee submissions are only open to members of Parliament, not to citizens or community groups

☐ Committees only investigate past elections, never proposed bills

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** The senators examining the bill read the evidence and can recommend changes to the law before Parliament votes on it

When Parliament is actively inquiring into the details of a bill, a well-argued committee submission puts evidence straight in front of the lawmakers.

:::


:::keep-together

```
 1  Next federal election day is still two years away.
 2  Residents in Wattle Creek want the government to improve flood warning signs along the river.
 3  Nib says: "Voting at an election is the only way citizens can participate in Australia's democracy."
 4  Nib says: "So the residents can do nothing at all for the next two years."
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: Nib thinks voting at an election is the only way citizens can participate in a democracy. Between elections, citizens can petition councils or parliaments, meet their local representative, contribute to inquiries, volunteer, and campaign.

### Comparing two methods

**Why might a social media campaign by itself be strong for raising quick awareness of an issue, but weaker than a committee submission or community project for actually fixing the detail of a law?**

☐ Members of Parliament are banned by law from reading anything posted online

☐ A social media post automatically forces Parliament to vote on a bill within 7 days

☐ Only a formal parliamentary petition is allowed to mention a community issue

☐ Online posts can reach thousands of people quickly, but a short post rarely explains the detailed evidence or practical changes a law needs

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Online posts can reach thousands of people quickly, but a short post rarely explains the detailed evidence or practical changes a law needs

Each method has strengths and limits: online campaigns spread awareness fast, while submissions and local projects dig into detailed solutions.

## Compare effectiveness for a goal


:::keep-together

### Combining two methods

**Residents in a made-up regional town want two things: (1) immediate help clearing fallen branches from elderly neighbours' yards after a storm, and (2) better state funding for storm drains next year. Why does combining two forms of participation work better than using only one?**

☐ Because parliamentary petitions can only be signed by people who have never volunteered

☐ Because a state petition is the only legal way neighbours are allowed to help clear fallen branches

☐ Volunteering through a community group solves the immediate yard cleanup today, while a petition and meeting with the state MP targets next year's state budget

☐ Because volunteering in a neighbour's yard automatically changes the state budget

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Volunteering through a community group solves the immediate yard cleanup today, while a petition and meeting with the state MP targets next year's state budget

Hands-on volunteering meets the immediate community need, while formal representation and petitions address the government funding decision.

:::


:::keep-together

### Participating before turning 18

**Can people under 18 participate in Australia's democracy before they are old enough to vote?**

☐ Yes: they can sign or start petitions, write to MPs, contribute to inquiries, volunteer, join community groups and take part in peaceful campaigns

☐ No: nobody under 18 is allowed to sign a parliamentary e-petition or write to an MP

☐ Only by voting in state and local council elections, since the voting age is 18 only for federal elections

☐ No: only enrolled voters aged 18 and over are allowed to join a community campaign or attend a public meeting

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Yes: they can sign or start petitions, write to MPs, contribute to inquiries, volunteer, join community groups and take part in peaceful campaigns

While formal voting starts at 18, almost every other form of democratic participation is open to young people right now.
