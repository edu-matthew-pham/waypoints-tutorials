# Ways to take part in democracy

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** An election happens only once every few years. If a town problem needs fixing this month, what can citizens actually do?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students identify and compare formal and informal ways citizens participate in Australia's democracy — including voting, standing for parliament, petitions, committee inquiries, volunteering, community organisations, social enterprise, campaigns and peaceful protest — and evaluate which methods fit different civic goals.

**Students should be able to say:** I can describe at least three different ways citizens can participate in Australia's democracy. I can distinguish between parliamentary/electoral channels and community/public action. I can explain why one method of participation is more effective than another for a specific goal.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

☐ Write to their federal member of Parliament

☐ Start an online campaign and wait for the council to notice

☐ Write to the council and collect signatures from library users

**What happens:** Bike racks at a library are a council decision, so writing to the council (with signatures to show support) reaches the people who decide. ## Worked example: matching the method to the goal


:::keep-together

### Choosing the right tool for the goal

1. $\text{Goal: fix creek rubbish this Saturday}$ — Join a local bushcare working bee -> Volunteering & community organisation.
2. $\text{Goal: show Parliament 5,000 locals care}$ — Collect signatures on an official e-petition -> Petitioning Parliament.
3. $\text{Goal: improve the wording of a new bill}$ — Send evidence and recommendations to the inquiry -> Committee of inquiry submission.
4. $\text{Goal: choose who represents the electorate}$ — Cast a ballot on election day (or stand as a candidate) -> Voting & elections.

Now: why is a committee submission effective when a bill is being examined, where does Nib go wrong, and how do different methods compare?

**In a made-up example, a Senate committee is looking into a proposed law on safety at public swimming pools and asks the public for submissions. Why could a youth swimming club's submission be worth sending?**

☐ It only helps if the club also gets thousands of people to sign it

☐ It is worth sending, but only adults and experts are allowed to send one

☐ The senators read the evidence and can recommend changes before the vote

☐ Once the committee receives it, the club's ideas become part of the law

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** The senators read the evidence and can recommend changes before the vote

When a committee is looking at a bill in detail, a clear submission puts evidence straight in front of the lawmakers before they vote.

:::


:::keep-together

### Find Nib's mistake

**Nib is advising residents of the made-up town of Wattle Creek who want better flood warning signs before the next wet season. Click the first line where Nib's thinking goes wrong.**

```
 1  Next federal election day is still two years away.
 2  Residents in Wattle Creek want the government to improve flood warning signs along the river.
 3  Nib says: "Voting at an election is the only way citizens can participate in Australia's democracy."
 4  Nib says: "So the residents can do nothing at all for the next two years."
```

Line: {{field-15}}

Why is it wrong?

{{lines-2}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: Nib thinks voting at an election is the only way citizens can participate in a democracy. Between elections, citizens can petition councils or parliaments, meet their local representative, contribute to inquiries, volunteer, and campaign.

:::


:::keep-together

### Comparing two methods

**An online campaign can quickly show that many people care. Why is it often weaker for fixing the detail of a law?**

☐ If enough people share it, Parliament has to vote on it

☐ Online campaigns are not a real way of taking part in democracy

☐ MPs do not pay attention to anything posted online

☐ Short posts rarely explain the evidence or exact changes a law needs

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Short posts rarely explain the evidence or exact changes a law needs

Each method has strengths and limits: online campaigns spread awareness fast, while submissions and local projects work on detailed solutions.

## Compare effectiveness for a goal


:::keep-together

### Combining two methods

**In a made-up town, residents want (1) help clearing storm branches from older neighbours' yards now, and (2) better state funding for storm drains next year. What is the best plan?**

☐ Start an online campaign about both and wait for a response

☐ Petition the state MP about both, since the government should clear the yards

☐ Volunteer for both, since a strong local effort will fix the drains too

☐ Volunteer for the clean-up now, and petition or meet the state MP about drains

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Volunteer for the clean-up now, and petition or meet the state MP about drains

Volunteering meets the need straight away, while a petition or meeting with the MP reaches the people who decide on funding.

:::


:::keep-together

### Participating before turning 18

**Can people under 18 participate in Australia's democracy before they are old enough to vote?**

☐ No: taking part in democracy starts when you can vote at 18

☐ Yes: they can vote in council and state elections from 16

☐ Yes: they can write to MPs, sign petitions, volunteer and join campaigns

☐ Only by volunteering, because writing to MPs is for voters

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Yes: they can write to MPs, sign petitions, volunteer and join campaigns

Voting starts at 18, but almost every other way of taking part in democracy is open to young people now.

## Exit ticket

:::keep-together

**Name three ways citizens can participate in Australia's democracy, and explain which one you would choose if Parliament was reviewing a new bill that affected young people.**

{{lines-3}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Examples: voting, petitions, contributing to a parliamentary committee of inquiry, volunteering, peaceful protest, social media campaigns. If Parliament is reviewing a bill, contributing a submission to the committee of inquiry (alongside contacting your MP or petitioning) is effective because it puts evidence directly in front of the lawmakers examining the bill.
