# The five democratic freedoms

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** Imagine holding an election where nobody is allowed to criticise the government, join a group, or hold a public meeting beforehand. Would that vote still be a real democracy?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students learn the five key democratic freedoms in Australia — freedom of speech, association, assembly, religion and movement — and explain how they enable citizens to participate in democracy. This is the hinge lesson for AC9HC7K02: without a clear grasp of what each freedom is and why it matters, legal limits (lesson 2) and active participation (lessons 3–4) have no foundation.

**Students should be able to say:** I can name the five democratic freedoms: speech, association, assembly, religion and movement. I can explain how each freedom helps people take part in Australia's democracy. I can give a concrete example of citizens using these freedoms to participate.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: how freedoms support participation


:::keep-together

### The Wattle Creek bridge campaign

1. $\text{Join a group}$ — Residents form the Wattle Creek Safe Crossing Group -> Freedom of association.
2. $\text{Meet in public}$ — 200 people gather peacefully in the park on Saturday -> Freedom of assembly.
3. $\text{Argue the case}$ — Spokespeople explain the danger on local radio -> Freedom of speech.
4. $\text{Travel to Parliament}$ — Two members travel to the state capital to hand in their report -> Freedom of movement.

Now: which freedoms did the residents use, where did Nib go wrong, and how do you tell association from assembly?

**In the made-up town of Wattle Creek, residents want the state government to build a pedestrian bridge over a busy road near the high school. They form the 'Wattle Creek Safe Crossing Group', hold a Saturday rally in the park, and speak to local reporters. Which three freedoms are they using?**

☐ Freedom of association (forming the group), freedom of assembly (the peaceful park rally), and freedom of speech (speaking out to reporters)

☐ Only freedom of speech, because forming a group and holding a rally are just forms of speaking

☐ Freedom of assembly (forming the ongoing group) and freedom of association (gathering in the park on Saturday)

☐ Freedom of movement (forming the group) and freedom of religion (holding the rally)

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Freedom of association (forming the group), freedom of assembly (the peaceful park rally), and freedom of speech (speaking out to reporters)

Active participation usually combines several freedoms at once: joining together (association), meeting in public (assembly), and making the case (speech).

:::


:::keep-together

**Answer:** line 3

Line 3: Nib thinks democracy only happens at the ballot box on election day. Without the five freedoms to debate ideas, form groups and gather peacefully between elections, voters could not make an informed choice or hold governments to account.

### Association vs assembly

**What is the difference between freedom of association and freedom of assembly?**

☐ Association is joining or forming an ongoing group; assembly is gathering together peacefully in a place

☐ Association is gathering in a public park for a rally; assembly is joining an ongoing club or union

☐ There is no difference: both mean meeting in the same place at the same time

☐ Association only applies to political parties in Parliament; assembly applies to any group outside Parliament

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Association is joining or forming an ongoing group; assembly is gathering together peacefully in a place

You use freedom of association when you join a club or union; you use freedom of assembly when people gather at a peaceful meeting or march.

## Explain how a freedom supports participation


:::keep-together

### Freedom of religion

**How does freedom of religion support a peaceful, inclusive democracy?**

☐ It means the federal government chooses one official national religion that everyone must respect

☐ It means religious beliefs place a citizen above ordinary laws passed by Parliament

☐ It protects people who belong to a religion, but does not protect people who hold no religious belief

☐ People of different faiths and of no faith can live, worship and take part in public life as equal citizens without the government forcing one belief on everyone

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** People of different faiths and of no faith can live, worship and take part in public life as equal citizens without the government forcing one belief on everyone

Freedom of religion ensures people are not excluded from civic life because of their faith or lack of faith.

:::


:::keep-together

### Freedom of movement

**Why does Australia's Constitution require that trade and movement between the states be free, and how does freedom of movement help democracy?**

☐ It means state governments must get federal permission before building local roads

☐ It only protects goods and freight trucks crossing state borders, not ordinary citizens travelling

☐ People can travel across Australia to work, live, meet others, or attend national events and inquiries without state border passports

☐ It only applies to international travel with a passport, not moving between Australian states

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** People can travel across Australia to work, live, meet others, or attend national events and inquiries without state border passports

Being one country means people can move freely between states and travel to take part in national life.

:::
