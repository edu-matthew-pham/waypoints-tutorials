# Within the bounds of law

Freedoms and taking part · Democratic freedoms and their limits

## Today

**We are learning to** explain how democratic freedoms operate within the bounds of law and why legal limits exist.

- I can explain what it means for democratic freedoms to operate 'within the bounds of law'.
- I can give at least one example of a legal limit on a democratic freedom (such as defamation law on speech or safety rules on assembly).
- I can explain why legal limits exist in a democracy (to protect other people's rights, safety and reputation).

## Why it matters

> You have freedom of speech and assembly in Australia. Does that mean you can post a false story that your neighbour is a thief, or hold a protest that blocks an ambulance on its way to hospital?

<!-- Speaker note: In Lesson 1 you met the five democratic freedoms: speech, association, assembly, religion and movement. Can you do anything at all in the name of those freedoms? No. In a democracy, freedoms work **within the bounds of law**. That means the law sets public limits where one person's use of a freedom would seriously harm others: - **other people's reputation**: harmful false claims about a person can break **defamation** law (the law that protects a person's good name); - **public safety**: a protest must stay peaceful and must not block ambulances; - **the safety of the community**: urging people to be violent is against the law. -->

## Inside and outside the bounds of law

**In the made-up town of Wattle Creek, Jordan writes a newspaper letter saying: 'The council's new parking fees are too high and will hurt local shops.' Meanwhile, Alex posts a false story online saying that a named shopkeeper steals from customers. How does the law treat these two posts?**

- Jordan's opinion is fine; Alex's false claim could break defamation law
- Both could break defamation law, because both make someone look bad
- Both are fine, because freedom of speech covers anything you say
- Jordan's letter could be illegal for criticising the council; Alex's is fine

* Answer: Jordan's opinion is fine; Alex's false claim could break defamation law

## What makes a limit fair?

**What makes a legal limit on a freedom reasonable in a democracy, rather than an excuse to silence people?**

- It applies only to groups the government disagrees with
- A minister decides it on the day, depending on the protest
- It is in a public law, applies to everyone and protects others from harm
- It is popular with most voters at the time

* Answer: It is in a public law, applies to everyone and protects others from harm

<!-- Speaker note: A fair limit is written in a public law, applies to everyone, and protects safety or rights without shutting down peaceful debate. People can still disagree about where a particular limit should sit. -->

## Within the bounds of law

**Why are democratic freedoms in Australia subject to legal limits such as defamation law and peaceful-assembly rules?**

- To protect other people's rights, reputation and safety where one person's action would harm others
- To stop people criticising the government too much
- Because freedoms only count on election day
- Because most people would misuse their freedoms

## Sam's claim about speech

**Sam (made up) says: 'If the law can stop me spreading false rumours about someone, we don't have free speech at all.' What is the best reply?**

- The limit is fine because it only covers posts, not what you say out loud
- The limit is fine because free speech mainly protects politicians
- You can still share opinions; the limit is on harmful false claims about people
- Sam is right: any limit at all means the freedom is gone

* Answer: You can still share opinions; the limit is on harmful false claims about people

## Who checks the boundaries?

**A citizen argues that a law goes further than the Constitution allows. Who decides?**

- The courts, with the High Court at the top
- The Prime Minister and Cabinet, who run the government
- The Senate, by voting on the law again
- The Governor-General, who signs every new law

* Answer: The courts, with the High Court at the top

<!-- Speaker note: Independent courts, with the High Court at the top for questions about the Constitution, decide whether a law stays within its limits. -->
