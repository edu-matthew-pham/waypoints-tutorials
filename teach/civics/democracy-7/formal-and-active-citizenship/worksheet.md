# Formal and active citizenship

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** In a made-up example, Orla and Dev are both Australian citizens. Orla only thinks about it when she renews her passport. Dev votes, volunteers with the local bushfire brigade and helps run a youth group. What is the difference between them?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students distinguish formal citizenship (legal status, rights and responsibilities) from active citizenship (taking part in civic and community life), and identify that not all people in Australia have always had equal access to citizenship rights.

**Students should be able to say:** I can explain the difference between formal citizenship and active citizenship. I can name examples of legal rights and responsibilities of formal citizenship, and examples of active citizenship. I can give one way access to citizenship rights in Australia has changed over time.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: rights have changed over time


:::keep-together

### Formal status and taking part

1. $\text{Formal citizenship}$ — Legal membership of Australia -> Rights (passport, vote, stand for parliament) + Duties (obey laws, vote once 18, jury service).
2. $\text{Active citizenship}$ — Taking part -> Volunteering, petitions, writing to council, joining groups, staying informed.
3. $\text{Change over time}$ — Laws about who can vote have changed; the AEC records that from 1962 all First Nations people could enrol and vote in federal elections.

Now: how do formal and active citizenship differ, where did Nib go wrong, and how can people without a vote still take part?

**Have all adults in Australia always had the same right to vote in federal elections?**

☐ Yes: every adult has had the same vote since 1901

☐ No: the law has changed over time about who could enrol and vote

☐ Yes: the Constitution gave every person a vote in 1901

☐ No, but it changed only once, at the 1967 referendum

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** No: the law has changed over time about who could enrol and vote

Who can vote in federal elections is set by laws Parliament passes, and those laws have changed over time. The AEC records the changes on its milestones page.

:::


:::keep-together

### Taking part without a vote

**In a made-up town, students aged 14 want a pedestrian crossing near their school. None of them can vote yet. What can they do?**

☐ Wait until they turn 18, because petitions need voters to sign

☐ Nothing: only people on the electoral roll can ask for change

☐ Collect signatures, write to the council and speak at a council meeting

☐ Ask the High Court to order the council to build it

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Collect signatures, write to the council and speak at a council meeting

You don't need a vote to be an active citizen. Petitions, letters and public meetings are open to anyone.

## Explain formal and active citizenship


:::keep-together

**Explain the difference between formal citizenship and active citizenship, and give one way access to citizenship rights in Australia has changed over time.**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** A strong answer: Formal citizenship is your legal status as a member of Australia. It gives you rights, like getting a passport and voting, and duties, like obeying the law and jury service. Active citizenship is how you take part to help your community and democracy, like volunteering or starting a petition. Not everyone has always had the same rights: the AEC records that all First Nations people could enrol and vote in federal elections from 1962.; A different answer that also works: Formal citizenship is the legal side of being a citizen: the status, the rights and the legal duties. Active citizenship is choosing to take part, and you can do it before you can vote, like writing to the council. Who could vote in federal elections has changed over time, for example the law change in 1962 recorded by the AEC.

:::
