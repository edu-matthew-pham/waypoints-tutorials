# Formal and active citizenship

**Name:** {{field-24}} **Date:** {{field-15}} **Class:** {{field-15}}

> **Key question:** In a made-up example, two people are both legal Australian citizens. Orla only ever thinks about it when she renews her passport. Dev votes carefully, volunteers at the local bushfire brigade, and helps run a town youth group. What is the difference between the two?


:::teacher-note

**In one sentence:** Students distinguish between formal citizenship (legal status, rights and responsibilities) and active citizenship (engaged participation in civic and community life), and identify that not all people in Australia have historically had equal access to citizenship rights, particularly in relation to First Nations Australians.

**Students should be able to say:** I can explain the difference between formal citizenship and active citizenship. I can name examples of legal rights and responsibilities of formal citizenship, and examples of active citizenship. I can identify that First Nations Australians were historically excluded from full citizenship and voting rights, and that active citizenship helped change the law.

**Width:** the unmarked parts are the floor every student does (predict, run, investigate and make). Typical adds the modify practice; wider adds the real-world contexts. They add contexts, not difficulty. Students work in the online tutorial alongside this sheet.

:::

## Worked example: inclusion, exclusion and changing the law


:::keep-together

### Formal status, active participation, and historical change

1. $\text{Formal citizenship}$ — Legal membership of Australia -> Rights (vote, passport, stand for parliament) + Duties (obey laws, compulsory voting, jury service).
2. $\text{Active citizenship}$ — Engaged participation -> Volunteering, community leadership, petitions, campaigns, informed debate.
3. $\text{Historical exclusion}$ — From 1901/1902, federal law excluded most First Nations Australians from the federal vote until 1962 (with compulsory enrolment extended in 1984), and s 127 of the Constitution excluded them from the national census count until the 1967 referendum.

Now: have citizenship rights always been equal in Australia, where did Nib go wrong, and how did active citizenship drive change?

**Have all people in Australia always had equal access to the formal rights of citizenship, such as voting in federal elections?**

☐ No: First Nations Australians gained the right to vote in federal elections at the 1967 referendum, not in 1962

☐ No: for decades after Federation in 1901, Commonwealth law excluded most First Nations Australians from enrolling and voting in federal elections until the Commonwealth Electoral Act was amended in 1962

☐ Yes: the 1901 Constitution gave every person in Australia an automatic passport and vote

☐ Yes: every adult in Australia has had the exact same voting rights since 1 January 1901

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** No: for decades after Federation in 1901, Commonwealth law excluded most First Nations Australians from enrolling and voting in federal elections until the Commonwealth Electoral Act was amended in 1962

Under the Commonwealth Franchise Act 1902, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples were barred from enrolling to vote in federal elections (unless already entitled under state law before 1902, with a partial exemption for defence service in 1949) until the Commonwealth Electoral Act 1962 guaranteed the right to enrol and vote.

:::

### How active citizenship drove change

**How did active citizenship help change the laws that excluded First Nations Australians from full constitutional and voting rights?**

☐ Over many decades, First Nations leaders and community organisations used petitions, public meetings, speeches and campaigns to persuade Parliament and Australian voters to change the laws and the Constitution

☐ Only people who were already on the federal electoral roll were legally allowed to organise petitions or public meetings

☐ The laws changed automatically on a fixed date written into the 1901 Constitution

☐ The High Court changed the Constitution by itself in 1967 without a public campaign or referendum

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** Over many decades, First Nations leaders and community organisations used petitions, public meetings, speeches and campaigns to persuade Parliament and Australian voters to change the laws and the Constitution

Even when people were excluded from formal voting rights, they used active citizenship — petitions, associations, assemblies and speech — to campaign for equal rights.

:::

## Explain formal and active citizenship


:::keep-together

**Explain the difference between formal citizenship and active citizenship, and explain one way that access to citizenship rights in Australia has changed over time.**

Answer: {{field-15}}

:::


:::teacher-note

**Answer:** A strong answer: Formal citizenship is a person's legal status as a member of Australia, which carries legal rights like voting and getting a passport, and duties like obeying the law and jury service. Active citizenship is how people get involved to help their community and democracy, such as volunteering, petitioning or campaigning. Historically, not everyone had equal formal rights: most First Nations Australians were excluded from voting in federal elections until the law was changed in 1962.; A different answer that also works: Formal citizenship is the legal side of being a citizen (status, rights and legal obligations), whereas active citizenship is participating in public and community life to make a positive difference. Even when First Nations Australians were excluded by law from full formal citizenship rights after 1901, First Nations campaigners practised active citizenship through petitions and public advocacy to win the federal vote in 1962 and constitutional change in the 1967 referendum.

:::
