Year 9 Chemistry

What's inside an atom?

Try firstOxygen has atomic number 8 and mass number 16. How many protons, neutrons and electrons does a neutral oxygen atom have?

Check your answer

8 protons, 8 neutrons (16 − 8), 8 electrons.

An atom has a tiny, dense nucleus of protons (+1, mass 1) and neutrons (0, mass 1), with electrons (−1, almost no mass) around it.

Atomic number = protons, and it decides the element. Mass number = protons + neutrons. A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons.

Same element, different atoms?

Try firstUranium-235 and uranium-238 both have 92 protons. How many neutrons does each have, and are they the same element?

Check your answer

143 and 146 neutrons. Both are uranium (92 protons): they are isotopes.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same number of protons) with different numbers of neutrons, so they have different mass numbers: carbon-12, carbon-13 and carbon-14.

They have the same electrons, so they react in the same way. Some isotopes are stable; others are unstable (radioactive) and their nuclei decay.

What comes out of a decaying nucleus?

Try firstCobalt-60 (27 protons) decays by beta decay and gives out gamma rays. What is it now, and which of the radiation would get through 1 cm of aluminium?

Check your answer

Nickel-60 (28 protons, mass number still 60). Only the gamma rays get through the aluminium.

An unstable nucleus decays by giving out alpha (2 protons and 2 neutrons), beta (a fast electron made when a neutron turns into a proton) or gamma (energy only).

Alpha: atomic number −2, mass number −4. Beta: atomic number +1, mass number the same. Gamma: no change. Alpha is stopped by paper, beta by a few millimetres of aluminium, and gamma is only reduced by thick lead.

How long does radioactivity last?

Try firstIodine-131 has a half-life of 8 days. A hospital receives 40 g. How much is left after 24 days?

Check your answer

5 g (40 → 20 → 10 → 5: three half-lives).

Each unstable nucleus decays at random, but a large sample halves in a fixed time: its half-life. After 1 half-life, 1/2 is left; after 2, 1/4; after 3, 1/8.

Half-life doesn't change with heat or chemistry. Short half-lives suit medicine (fluorine-18, about 2 hours); long ones suit dating (carbon-14, 5730 years).

From uranium to lead

Try firstRadium-226 (atomic number 88) decays by alpha emission. What are the new atomic and mass numbers?

Check your answer

Atomic number 86, mass number 222: radon-222.

A decay chain ends only at a stable nucleus. Alpha decay lowers the atomic number by 2 and the mass number by 4; beta decay raises the atomic number by 1 and leaves the mass number unchanged.

What goes in, what comes out?

Try firstIron reacts with oxygen and water to make rust (hydrated iron oxide). Write the word equation and label the reactants and products.

Check your answer

iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron oxide (rust). Reactants: iron, oxygen, water. Product: hydrated iron oxide.

In a chemical reaction the reactants are used up and new substances, the products, are made. A word equation names them: reactants → products ("react to make").

Include every reactant and product: methane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water. A word equation shows no amounts and no atoms.

Where do the atoms go?

Try firstWhen charcoal burns in a sealed jar, does the total mass change? Why?

Check your answer

No: the carbon and oxygen atoms are rearranged into carbon dioxide, which stays in the jar. Same atoms, same mass.

In a chemical reaction the atoms are rearranged into new particles; none are made or destroyed. The same atoms means the same mass: conservation of mass.

A balanced equation shows the same number of each type of atom on both sides. Balance by changing the numbers in front, never the formulas.

How do you balance an equation?

Try firstBalance: H₂ + Cl₂ → HCl. How do you know it's balanced?

Check your answer

H₂ + Cl₂ → 2 HCl. Two hydrogen and two chlorine atoms on each side.

A balanced equation has the same number of each kind of atom on both sides, because atoms are only rearranged.

Change only the coefficients (numbers in front); the subscripts define the substance. Same atoms on both sides means the same mass: conservation of mass.

Where does the mass go?

Try firstA student weighs a candle before and after burning it in an open dish and finds it lighter. Is mass conserved?

Check your answer

Yes. The missing mass left as carbon dioxide and water vapour. Weighing the candle and the oxygen used against the gases would show the same total.

Atoms are rearranged, not made or destroyed, so total mass is conserved. In an open system the mass can seem to change when a gas escapes or enters. A balanced equation has the same atoms on both sides.

Year 9 Biology

What counts as a stimulus?

Try firstGive one internal stimulus and name its receptor and effector.

Check your answer

For example: body temperature rising; temperature receptors in the brain; sweat glands.

Stimulus: any change the body detects, inside or outside it. Pathway: stimulus → receptor (detects) → command centre (decides) → effector (muscle or gland) → response.

Who decides?

Try firstName the command centre in a reflex and say why reflexes are fast.

Check your answer

The spinal cord; the impulse doesn't have to travel to the brain and wait for a decision.

The CNS (brain and spinal cord) is the command centre. Sensory neurons carry impulses in from receptors; motor neurons carry them out to effectors (muscles or glands). A reflex is automatic and fast because no decision is made: most go through the spinal cord, and reflexes in the head (blinking, pupils narrowing) go through the brain stem.

Nerves or hormones?

Try firstGive one difference between how a nerve signal and a hormone travel, and one difference in how long their effects last.

Check your answer

Nerve signals are electrical, along nerves; hormones are chemicals in the blood. Nerve effects are brief; hormone effects last longer.

Both systems run stimulus → receptor → command centre → effector → response. The nervous system sends electrical signals along nerves: very fast, brief, to one place.

The endocrine system uses glands that release hormones into the blood: slower, longer-lasting, reaching target organs anywhere. Many responses use both.

Which system runs it?

Try firstWhich system would you give blinking to, and which growing, and why?

Check your answer

How does your body stay at 37 °C?

Try firstDescribe what happens when your body temperature rises above 37 °C.

Check your answer

Temperature receptors detect it; the hypothalamus sends impulses to the sweat glands and skin blood vessels; sweating and widened vessels cool the body; as it comes back near 37 °C they ease off, and they stop once the heat has gone.

Homeostasis: keeping internal conditions near a set point. Negative feedback: a change is detected and the response reverses it, then eases off as conditions return to the set point (in steady heat a smaller response keeps going to hold it there; it stops once the stimulus has gone). Body temperature: sweating and flushed skin when too hot, shivering and pale skin when too cold.

What makes a sex cell special?

Try firstWhy do sperm and egg cells have 23 chromosomes, not 46?

Check your answer

So that when they fuse at fertilisation, the new cell has 46, the full number, again.

Gametes (sperm and egg) have half the usual number of chromosomes (23 in humans), so fertilisation restores the full number (46). Sperm swim (tail, mitochondria) and digest their way in (acrosome); the egg is large with a food store and lets only one sperm in.

Why don't brothers and sisters look the same?

Try firstWhy do two offspring of the same parents look different?

Check your answer

Each gets a random one of each parent's two copies of each gene, so each gets a different combination.

Sexual reproduction: two parents; each offspring gets a random half of each parent's genes, so offspring vary. Asexual reproduction: one parent makes clones. Variation helps a population survive change; asexual reproduction is fast and needs no partner.

Year 9 Physics

How does heat get through?

Try firstWhy can convection happen in water but not in a metal spoon?

Check your answer

Convection needs the material to flow so heated parts can rise. Water's particles can move; a spoon's are held in place, so it can only conduct.

Conduction: particles vibrate harder and pass energy to their neighbours by collisions. The particles stay in place. Mostly in solids.

Convection: heated fluid becomes less dense and rises; cooler fluid sinks. The particles carry the energy. Only in liquids and gases.

How does sound travel?

Try firstA loud, high note is played. Say what is large or fast about the wave, and what the particles of air in between are doing.

Check your answer

Large amplitude (loud) and high frequency (high pitch). The particles vibrate to and fro in place while the squashed and spread-out patches travel.

A sound wave is a line of compressions (particles squashed together) and rarefactions (spread out) that travels through a medium. The particles only vibrate to and fro; the pattern moves.

Frequency sets the pitch and amplitude sets the loudness. With no particles, as in a vacuum, there is no sound.

Where does static charge come from?

Try firstA plastic ruler is rubbed on a cloth and becomes negative. What happened to the cloth, and why?

Check your answer

The cloth became positive. Electrons moved from the cloth to the ruler, so the cloth has more + than −.

Rubbing moves electrons from one material to the other. The material that gains them becomes negative; the one that loses them becomes positive, by the same amount. Protons never move.

Unlike charges attract; like charges repel. A spark is the extra electrons jumping across to neutralise the imbalance. On the board, Join with metal connects the balloon and the wool, so the electrons go back to the wool; touching an earthed tap instead sends them into the ground and leaves the wool positive.

How does current flow?

Try firstA student says 'the bulb uses up the electrons so the wire after it is empty'. What would you say?

Check your answer

The same number of electrons leave the bulb as enter it. They carry on round the loop. The bulb transfers some of the energy they carry as light and heat.

A current is a flow of free electrons pushed round the circuit by the battery. A conductor has free electrons; an insulator has almost none because its electrons are tied to their atoms.

The electrons are not used up. More volts means a stronger push and a bigger current.

What is the electromagnetic spectrum?

Try firstA student says X-rays are dangerous because they are faster than light. Correct them in a sentence.

Check your answer

X-rays travel at the same speed as light. They are dangerous because they have a high frequency, so each packet carries a lot of energy, enough to damage cells.

Light is a transverse wave and part of the electromagnetic spectrum, which runs from radio to gamma. All travel at the same speed in a vacuum and need no medium.

Going from radio to gamma, the wavelength gets shorter, the frequency higher and the energy in each packet (photon) greater. That decides what each wave is used for and how careful we must be.

How does light bend?

Try firstA ray of light goes from air into water and bends towards the normal. What does that tell you about the speed of light in water?

Check your answer

It is slower in water than in air. The change of speed turns the wave fronts and so the ray.

Reflection: the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence.

Refraction: light slows down in an optically denser material (one where it travels more slowly), so the wave fronts bunch up and the direction bends towards the normal. Going straight in, it slows without bending.

Wave or particle?

Try firstGive one phenomenon where the wave model is more useful than the particle model, and one where it is the other way round.

Check your answer

Wave: sound, reflection or refraction of light. Particle: conduction of heat, static charge. Any one of each, with a reason.

The particle model explains conduction, convection and static charge. The wave model explains sound, reflection, refraction and the electromagnetic spectrum. Light also behaves as photons when it gives energy to single electrons.

A model is a tool with a domain of usefulness, not a literal description.

Where does the energy go?

Try firstA skateboarder has 2000 J at the top of a ramp and 1700 J of kinetic energy at the bottom. Where are the other 300 J?

Check your answer

Transferred as heat and sound because of friction and air resistance. The total is still 2000 J.

Conservation of energy: energy is never made or destroyed, only transferred or transformed. The total in a system always stays the same.

Energy that seems lost or used up has been transferred to the surroundings, usually as heat and sound.

How much is useful?

Try firstA phone charger takes in 20 J and stores 15 J in the battery. What is its efficiency, and where did the rest go?

Check your answer

15 ÷ 20 × 100% = 75%. The other 5 J became heat (the charger gets warm).

Efficiency = useful energy out ÷ total energy in × 100%. It is a proportion, so a device that gives out more useful energy can still be less efficient.

No real device is 100% efficient: some energy always becomes by-products, usually heat.

What does a Sankey diagram show?

Try firstA wind turbine takes in 500 J of kinetic energy and gives out 200 J of electricity. What is its efficiency, and how would its Sankey diagram show the rest?

Check your answer

200 ÷ 500 × 100% = 40%. The other 300 J would bend away as wasted bands (heat and sound), together as wide as 300 J.

A Sankey diagram shows energy as bands whose widths are proportional to the amounts. The input splits into the useful output (straight on) and wasted outputs (bending away), which always add up to the input.

Read the efficiency as useful ÷ input, and find the biggest waste by the widest wasted band.

Year 9 Earth and space

What are Earth's spheres?

Try firstName the four spheres and give one example of something where two of them meet.

Check your answer

Atmosphere, biosphere, hydrosphere, geosphere. For example, soil (geosphere and biosphere) or a cloud (atmosphere and hydrosphere).

Earth's spheres: atmosphere (air), biosphere (living things), hydrosphere (water), geosphere (rock, soil, magma). They overlap and interact as one system.

Where is all the carbon?

Try firstName a carbon store in the hydrosphere and one in the geosphere, and one flow that moves carbon into the atmosphere.

Check your answer

Hydrosphere: carbon dioxide dissolved in the oceans. Geosphere: fossil fuels or limestone. Flow into the air: respiration, decay, burning or release from the oceans.

Carbon is stored in every sphere: carbon dioxide in the air, dissolved carbon in the oceans, living things and soil in the biosphere, and fossil fuels and rocks in the geosphere.

Carbon is never made or destroyed; it flows between stores. The carbon cycle is the map of those flows.

Where does the carbon in a tree go?

Try firstDescribe how carbon moves between the air and living things.

Check your answer

Photosynthesis takes CO₂ from the air into plants; respiration by plants, animals and decomposers returns it to the air as CO₂.

Photosynthesis moves carbon from the atmosphere into the biosphere; respiration and decomposition move it back. When these flows balance, the air's CO₂ stays steady.

What does burning fossil fuels do to the carbon cycle?

Try firstPlants and the oceans take up about half of the CO₂ we add each year. Why does CO₂ in the air still keep rising?

Check your answer

Burning moves carbon stored in the geosphere over millions of years into the air in years. The natural flows were balanced, so the half that plants and oceans don't take up builds up, year after year.

Combustion of fossil fuels moves carbon from the geosphere, where it was stored over millions of years, to the atmosphere in years. Oceans and plants take up part of it; the rest builds up, so CO₂ in the air rises.
