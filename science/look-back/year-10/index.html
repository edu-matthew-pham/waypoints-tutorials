Year 10 Chemistry

How are the electrons arranged?

Try firstWrite the electron arrangement of aluminium (13 electrons). How many outer electrons does it have?

Check your answer

2, 8, 3: three outer electrons.

Electrons fill shells in order: 2, then 8, then 8. The outermost occupied shell is the outer shell, and its electrons decide how the element reacts.

Why do elements in a group behave alike?

Try firstBromine is in group 17. Predict how many outer electrons it has and how it reacts. Explain.

Check your answer

Seven outer electrons; it gains one easily, so it is a reactive non-metal, like chlorine.

Elements in the same group have the same number of outer-shell electrons, so they react in similar ways.

Group 1: one outer electron, easily lost: very reactive metals. Group 17: seven, one short of full: reactive non-metals. Group 18: full outer shell: hardly react.

Why does the periodic table repeat?

Try firstElement X has electrons 2, 8, 3. Which period and group is it in, and is it more likely a metal or a non-metal?

Check your answer

Period 3 (three shells), group 13 (three outer electrons): aluminium, a metal.

Each period fills one shell, so the period number is the number of shells. Across a period the outer electrons go from 1 to 8; then a new shell starts and the count goes back to 1.

Because the outer electrons repeat, so do the properties: that is why elements in a group behave alike. Across a period, elements change from metals to non-metals.

Group gives the outer electrons; period gives the shells. Outer electrons decide reactivity and bonding, so the table is a predictive model, not a list.

What kind of reaction is it?

Try firstClassify magnesium + zinc sulfate → magnesium sulfate + zinc, and say why it happens.

Check your answer

Displacement: magnesium is more reactive than zinc, so it takes zinc's place.

Synthesis: substances join into one (A + B → AB). Decomposition: one substance breaks into simpler ones (AB → A + B). Displacement: a more reactive element takes a less reactive one's place (A + BC → AC + B).

Spot the pattern to classify a reaction and predict its products. Displacement only goes from more reactive to less reactive.

Why does heat speed up reactions?

Try firstWhy does crushing a tablet make it dissolve and react faster in water?

Check your answer

Crushing increases the surface area, so more particles are exposed and collisions happen more often.

Particles react only when they collide with at least the activation energy.

  • Temperature: particles collide more often and harder (the biggest effect).
  • Concentration and surface area: more collisions each second.
  • Catalyst: lowers the activation energy; not used up.

Year 10 Biology

Where are the instructions?

Try firstWhat's the difference between a gene and an allele?

Check your answer

A gene is a section of DNA coding for a protein; an allele is one version of that gene.

DNA carries the instructions; a chromosome is one long coiled DNA molecule; a gene is a section of DNA coding for a protein; alleles are versions of a gene. Chromosomes come in pairs, one from each parent.

Why is every sperm and egg different?

Try firstWhy does meiosis make every gamete different?

Check your answer

Each gamete gets one chromosome from each pair, and which one is random, so the combination differs.

Meiosis makes gametes with half the chromosomes: one from each pair, chosen at random, so almost every gamete is a different combination (over 8 million possible in humans). Mitosis makes identical cells for growth and repair.

Can you predict the offspring?

Try firstComplete Bb × Bb. What fraction of offspring are expected to show the recessive trait?

Check your answer

BB, Bb, Bb, bb: 1 in 4 (25%).

Punnett square: each parent's possible gametes along the edges, every combination in the grid. It gives probabilities: Pp × Pp gives 1 PP : 2 Pp : 1 pp, so 3 dominant : 1 recessive phenotype. Genotype is the alleles; phenotype is the characteristic seen.

How different are individuals?

Try firstGive an example of inherited variation and one of environmental variation in humans.

Check your answer

For example: blood group (inherited); a scar or a language spoken (environmental).

Variation: individuals in a population differ. Continuous (any value, such as height) or discontinuous (groups, such as blood group); inherited, environmental, or both. Only inherited variation is passed on, so only it drives evolution.

Why did the beetles change?

Try firstExplain why the beetle population got paler on pale ground.

Check your answer

Beetles varied in colour; birds ate the darker, more visible ones; paler beetles survived and bred, passing on their colour; over generations, the average colour got paler.

Natural selection: individuals vary; those best suited to the environment are more likely to survive and reproduce, passing on their alleles; over generations the population changes. Individuals don't change: the population does. "Fittest" depends on the environment.

Over millions of years this branches into a tree of life: all living things share ancestors, and DNA shows how closely they are related.

Year 10 Physics

What is the net force?

Try firstA cyclist pedals with a forward force of 120 N. Air resistance is 70 N and friction 20 N. Find the net force and say what happens to her speed.

Check your answer

120 − (70 + 20) = 30 N forward: she speeds up.

A force is a vector: it has a size and a direction. Along one line, forces pointing the same way add, and forces pointing opposite ways subtract.

What is left is the net force, in the direction of the bigger side. A net force changes motion; a net force of zero leaves it unchanged.

Does a moving thing need a push?

Try firstA spacecraft coasts through empty space with its engines off. Describe its motion and explain.

Check your answer

It keeps moving at a constant velocity: there is no net force (no friction or air resistance), so by the first law its motion doesn't change.

Newton's first law: an object stays at rest or keeps moving at a constant velocity unless a net force acts on it.

Moving things don't need a push to keep going; they need a force to change their motion. Friction and air resistance are why everyday things slow down.

What is acceleration, exactly?

Try firstA sprinter goes from 0 to 9 m/s in 3 s. What is her acceleration?

Check your answer

a = (9 − 0) ÷ 3 = 3 m/s², forwards.

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity: a = Δv ÷ Δt, in m/s². It points the same way as the net force; slowing down is acceleration backwards.

On a speed–time graph, acceleration is the gradient. A steady speed means zero acceleration, however fast.

How hard do you need to push?

Try firstWhat acceleration does a net force of 30 N give a 6 kg trolley?

Check your answer

a = F ÷ m = 30 ÷ 6 = 5 m/s², in the direction of the net force.

Newton's second law: F = ma. F is the net force (N), m the mass (kg), a the acceleration (m/s²), in the direction of the net force.

Double the net force, double the acceleration; double the mass, half the acceleration.

Push the wall, and it pushes you?

Try firstA swimmer pushes the water backwards. Name the other force in the pair, and say which way it acts and on what.

Check your answer

The water pushes the swimmer forwards, on the swimmer, with a force of the same size.

Newton's third law: when A pushes or pulls B, B pushes or pulls A with a force of the same size in the opposite direction.

The two forces act on different objects, so they never cancel. Balanced forces (weight and support on a book) act on the same object and are not a third-law pair.

What forces act on it?

Try firstA 1000 kg car has 3000 N of driving force and 1000 N of drag. Find the net force and the acceleration.

Check your answer

2000 N forward; a = 2000 ÷ 1000 = 2 m/s².

A free body diagram shows one object and every force acting on it, as labelled arrows from the object.

Work out the net force in each direction separately. Zero in every direction: no change in motion. A net force: the object accelerates that way, a = F ÷ m.

Solving force and motion problems

Try firstA 50 kg skateboarder is pushed with a net force of 100 N from rest. How fast is she moving after 4 s?

Check your answer

a = 100 ÷ 50 = 2 m/s². After 4 s: 2 × 4 = 8 m/s.

A routine: net force, then F = ma, then a = Δv ÷ t, then check the answer and the units. On a speed–time graph the gradient is the acceleration.

Newton's laws in the real world

Try firstA car stops from 20 m/s in 0.5 s with one crumple zone, and in 1.0 s with a better one. By what factor does the force on the occupants change?

Check your answer

It halves. Twice the time means half the acceleration, so half the force.

Newton's laws explain design: seat belts (first law), airbags and crumple zones (second law: a longer stopping time means a smaller force), rockets (third law), and a spearthrower, which lengthens the push on the spear so it leaves faster.

How did the universe begin?

Try firstOutline three stages of the universe after the big bang.

Check your answer

For example: the first nuclei of hydrogen and helium (3 minutes), atoms forming and light set free (380 000 years), the first stars (about 200 million years), galaxies (about 1 billion years).

The big bang theory: space, time, matter and energy began about 13.8 billion years ago in an extremely hot, dense state; the universe has expanded and cooled since, forming nuclei, atoms, stars and galaxies. Heavier elements were made in stars.

How do we know the universe is expanding?

Try firstWhat does redshift show, and how does it support the big bang?

Check your answer

Galaxies are moving away, farther ones faster: the universe is expanding. Run backwards, everything was once together in a hot, dense state.

Redshift: light from distant galaxies is stretched to longer wavelengths, so they are moving away. Farther galaxies move faster (Hubble's law): an expanding universe with no centre. Run backwards, it began together: the big bang.

Where does the sunlight go?

Try firstWhy does replacing sea ice with open ocean warm the area?

Check your answer

Ice reflects most sunlight (about 80%); dark ocean absorbs most (reflects about 6%), so more energy is absorbed.

Sunlight is Earth's energy input. Some is reflected (the albedo, about 30% overall) and the rest absorbed. Bright surfaces reflect most; dark surfaces absorb most.

What keeps Earth's temperature steady?

Try firstWhat is energy balance, and what happens if more energy comes in than goes out?

Check your answer

Energy in equals energy out over time. If more comes in, Earth warms until it gives off enough extra infrared to balance again.

Energy balance: absorbed sunlight = infrared given off to space. If in exceeds out, Earth warms until out catches up. Energy moves between spheres by radiation, conduction and convection; the oceans store and carry heat.

How do greenhouse gases warm the Earth?

Try firstExplain how adding CO₂ warms the Earth.

Check your answer

CO₂ absorbs infrared from the surface and sends some back down, so less energy escapes; energy in exceeds energy out, and Earth warms until it gives off enough to balance again.

Greenhouse gases absorb outgoing infrared and send some back down, slowing energy loss. The natural effect keeps Earth about 15 °C (not −18 °C); the enhanced effect from extra greenhouse gases upsets the balance, so Earth warms. Not the ozone hole.
