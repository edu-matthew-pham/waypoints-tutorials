# English master plan (27 Oct 2026)

**Status:** draft for Matthew's review. One course is built (Holding writing together). Everything else here is planned, not built. The plan covers every English curriculum item in Kit. The node-by-node data is in the tutorials repo at `data/curriculum/english-triage.yml`, so a threads page and future course chats can be built straight from it. A fresh-eyes review checked the sort against Kit's data; its fixes are folded in.

This plan sits on top of the earlier decisions in `claude/english-pathway-plan.md`: no AI marking, texts written for the site or public domain, Nib as the novice, flexible speaking, and bring-your-own-text designed in. It is aligned to ACARA only, as decided for the first iteration of the tutorials; an MYP Language and Literature mapping can come later.

## How English differs from maths and science

- **Mostly drop-in skills, not a ladder.** A teacher picks the skill their class needs during a unit and teaches it. Most skills stand alone.
- **Short chains only where they're real.** A few skills need an earlier one first, such as topic sentences before strong paragraphs. Those lessons say "do this first" and link to it.
- **Progress means the same skill at a higher level.** A Year 4 and a Year 10 student both build paragraphs and argue points. What grows is subtlety, length and independence. So the curriculum is organised below as **threads**, each with what ACARA expects at each stage.
- **Teachers plan by unit** (a novel study, a persuasive unit), not by standard. So each course is a set of drop-in lessons, and a "Which lessons fit your unit?" table maps unit types to courses.
- **Grammar is taught in use.** Every Building sentences and Punctuation lesson ends with the move used in a short piece of a real text type, such as a paragraph of a review or a step of an explanation, not in isolated sentences. Plan, draft and edit comes early (wave 2) so the moves meet real writing.

## Threads: how English skills grow

These are the threads for a future teacher page, "How English skills grow". The page would show what ACARA intends at each stage, in plain words, with links to lessons where they exist. It is not a sequence to follow, and lessons aren't tied to it. Each descriptor was placed by its content. ACARA's code numbers mostly line up with a thread from about Year 5 (the LA04s are cohesion, the LA05s sentences), but numbers are reused for different topics in the early years and shift in places later, so the numbers weren't relied on.

**Language for people and situations** (Language). How we change our language for who we are talking to: formality, roles, identity, and including or excluding people.
- F–2: Notice that we talk differently at home and at school, and with friends and teachers.
- 3–4: Take turns and cooperate; spot formal and informal language and inclusive words.
- 5–6: See how language signals roles; move along a scale from slang to formal and choose the right level.
- 7–8: Language shows and builds who we are, and puts people in roles: leader, peer, outsider.
- 9–10: Language can include or exclude, empower or disempower; spot the devices that do it.

**Judging, persuading and positioning** (Language and Literacy). The words that carry opinions and values, from "I like it because" to bias, evidence and the hidden values in word choice.
- F–2: Say what you like and why, and move beyond "I liked it".
- 3–4: Words that show certainty and feeling; opinion words versus factual reporting.
- 5–6: Back up a claim instead of just asserting it; tell objective from subjective; spot bias.
- 7–8: The vocabulary of judgement; back a judgement with the text; irony and hyperbole; language that shifts with audience.
- 9–10: Judgement hidden in word choice, metaphor and allusion; the values behind the words; how fairly a perspective is shown.

**How texts are built for a purpose** (Language and Literacy). Each kind of text has stages that do a job: recount, report, explanation, argument, review, and the hybrids and twists writers make.
- F–2: Texts have purposes and shapes: stories, information, instructions, persuasion.
- 3–4: Stages of recounts, reports, explanations and arguments, and the language each uses.
- 5–6: Stages and phases; features that serve the purpose; how structure and language work together on a reader.
- 7–8: Purpose drives structure; idea patterns such as cause and effect; hybrid texts.
- 9–10: How and why writers bend or break the usual structure, and how well a structure works.

**Holding writing together** (Language). What makes writing easy to follow: pointing words, joining words, sentence starts, word chains, paragraphs, overviews and conclusions.
- F–2: Pronouns that point back to someone already named.
- 3–4: Paragraphs group ideas; joining words link sentences and order events.
- 5–6: Sentence starts guide the reader; repetition and word chains hold a text together.
- 7–8: Topic sentences, overviews and conclusions; claim, evidence and explanation inside a paragraph.
- 9–10: Nominalisation and connectives as a system; varying paragraph shape; joining paragraphs and images.

**Building sentences** (Language). From one complete idea to clauses, noun groups, complex and compound-complex sentences, and sentences shaped for effect.
- F–2: A sentence is a complete idea; word classes; simple and compound sentences.
- 3–4: Clauses, subject-verb agreement, verb types and tense, complex sentences, phrases that add detail.
- 5–6: Dependent clauses and their order, expanded noun groups, embedded clauses, precise verbs.
- 7–8: Compound-complex sentences, tense consistency, sentence shape as a deliberate choice.
- 9–10: Fragments and breaks for effect; judging which sentence structures work; syntax and style.

**Writing like a subject expert** (Language). Specialist and academic words, nominalisation, quoting and referencing: the voice of writing in science, history and English essays.
- 7–8: Technical words, words with everyday and technical meanings, academic vocabulary, nominalisation.
- 9–10: Nouns that sum up and carry an argument; quoting and referencing; precise academic vocabulary.

**Choosing words** (Language). Building vocabulary and choosing the word that fits: topic words, synonyms, word history, figurative language, tone and mood.
- F–2: Topics have their own words; choose words on purpose.
- 3–4: Technical words, words with more than one meaning, synonyms and antonyms.
- 5–6: Specialist words, Greek and Latin roots, figurative and emotive language, words to talk about texts.
- 7–8: Technical and academic words: see Writing like a subject expert.
- 9–10: How word choice builds tone, mood and style.

**Punctuation for meaning** (Language). From full stops to commas, apostrophes, colons, semicolons and dashes, and how authors bend the rules.
- F–2: Capital letters and full stops, question marks, commas in lists.
- 3–4: Apostrophes, and punctuating dialogue.
- 5–6: Commas after an opening phrase and between clauses, apostrophes for plural and joint ownership.
- 7–8: Colons, brackets, semicolons and dashes to support meaning.
- 9–10: How authors use and bend punctuation for effect.

**Images and multimodal texts** (Language). How pictures, diagrams, framing, angle, symbols and film choices make meaning alongside words.
- F–2: Pictures tell us things the words do not.
- 3–4: Gaze, angle, distance and framing set up a relationship with the viewer.
- 5–6: Image sequence and juxtaposition; diagrams, tables, maps and graphs; cultural sensitivity of images.
- 7–8: Vectors, angle and social distance build a perspective; visual allusion in adverts.
- 9–10: Symbols in still and moving images; evaluating visual choices in representations.

**Texts and their contexts** (Literature and Literacy). How a text reflects when, where and by whom it was made, and how people and places are represented, including First Nations and world literature.
- F–2: Stories from many places and peoples, including First Nations Australian stories, connected to our own lives.
- 3–4: The same story told differently; texts from different times; themes.
- 5–6: What a text shows about its time and place; how context shapes a reader's response.
- 7–8: Ideas, issues and values in texts from different contexts; how technology changes reading and writing.
- 9–10: Representations of people and places as constructed, and judged against their context.

**Responding to literature** (Literature). Forming, justifying and comparing opinions and interpretations of literature, and judging its value.
- F–2: Share feelings about a story and connect it to your own life.
- 3–4: Opinions grounded in experience; the effect of structure and language on a reader.
- 5–6: An opinion backed with literary terms and evidence; comparing two texts; other people's views.
- 7–8: Form and justify an opinion, weigh others' opinions; aesthetic and social value; opinions about style.
- 9–10: From first response to whole-text analysis; extending others' interpretations; ethical positions and world views.

**How authors craft literature** (Literature). Character, setting, plot, point of view, style, literary devices and voice, and the effects they create.
- F–2: Characters, settings and plots, and the words that create them.
- 3–4: Character techniques, setting and mood, plot tension, figurative language and word play.
- 5–6: Point of view and what it hides; an author's individual style.
- 7–8: Devices that create character and layers of meaning; how elements combine; tone through sentence patterns; positioning readers.
- 9–10: Extended metaphor, allegory, symbolism and intertextuality; voice as a device.

**Sound, rhythm and imagery** (Literature). Rhyme, rhythm, sound devices and imagery in poems, songs and stories.
- F–2: Rhythm, rhyme and alliteration; invent your own sound patterns.
- 3–4: Imagery, sound devices, figurative language and word play, and their effect on the reader.
- 5–6: Simile, metaphor, personification, assonance and onomatopoeia; ballad rhythm.
- 7–10: Poetry continues inside How authors craft literature: layers of meaning, imagery, voice.

**Creating literary texts** (Literature). Retelling, adapting and writing stories and poems, experimenting with devices, and editing for effect.
- F–2: Retell and adapt a familiar story.
- 3–4: Plan and write stories using techniques from what you have read; edit with feedback.
- 5–6: Read as a writer, experiment with figurative language, adapt a source.
- 7–8: Experiment with devices for a purpose, then edit for effect.
- 9–10: Experimental and hybrid texts; a sustained voice.

**Reading and understanding** (Literacy). Comprehension strategies: predicting, inferring, summarising, evaluating and comparing ideas, and finding your way around a text.
- F–2: Predict, connect, retell and question while reading together.
- 3–4: Main idea and detail, inference, monitoring; navigating print and online texts.
- 5–6: Skim and scan, summarise, infer with evidence, judge sources, compare sources.
- 7–8: Infer beyond the literal, summarise extended texts, interpret and evaluate ideas and credibility.
- 9–10: Compare ideas between texts; interpret complex and abstract ideas.

**Planning, writing and editing** (Literacy). The writing process for any purpose: plan for purpose and audience, draft with deliberate choices, edit and publish.
- F–2: Write short texts with growing control, guided in class.
- 3–4: Plan, write, edit and publish with paragraphs, complex sentences and linked ideas.
- 5–6: Plan for purpose and audience; develop ideas with connectives and noun groups; edit against criteria.
- 7–8: Choose structures, language and devices on purpose; revise purposefully; publish for an audience.
- 9–10: Critical and analytical texts; experimenting with structure; reflecting on choices.

**Talking and listening** (Literacy). Taking part in discussion: listening, building on others, questioning, and supporting or challenging what is said.
- F–2: Listen, take turns, speak clearly, respond to others.
- 3–4: Build on others' ideas and acknowledge other opinions.
- 5–6: Paraphrase and question to clarify; present and justify an opinion; formality in talk.
- 7–8: Evaluate spoken texts; support or challenge stated and implied meanings.
- 9–10: Analyse how a speaker positions listeners; discuss and present opinions on spoken texts.

**Speaking to an audience** (Literacy). Planning, rehearsing and delivering spoken and multimodal presentations, with voice and visual features chosen for the audience.
- F–2: Short talks with an opening, middle and end, and a voice the room can hear.
- 3–4: Plan, rehearse with feedback, and deliver structured presentations.
- 5–6: Elaborated ideas, technical vocabulary, voice and visual features chosen on purpose.
- 7–8: Presentations for a purpose and audience, crafted and rehearsed for effect.
- 9–10: Analytical presentations; rhetorical devices with a rationale.

**Spelling and word parts** (Literacy). Spelling through word parts, roots, generalisations and patterns, and spelling bent for effect.
- 3–4: Prefixes, suffixes, word families, homophones and spelling generalisations.
- 5–6: Base words, Greek and Latin roots, less common plurals, spelling generalisations.
- 7–8: Word parts and roots as a spelling tool; accurate spelling of complex words; correct sources for First Nations words.
- 9–10: Spelling bent for characterisation, humour and effect.

**Early reading, phonics and handwriting** (Literacy). Foundation to Year 2 decoding, phonological awareness, letter-sound knowledge, high-frequency words and handwriting.
- F–2: Phonological awareness, phonics, decoding, high-frequency words and handwriting.

What each course covers:
- **Building sentences:** clauses and how they join, clause order and emphasis, noun groups, precise verbs and adverb groups, embedded clauses, compound-complex sentences, tense consistency, choosing a sentence shape on purpose.
- **Punctuation for meaning:** commas that change meaning, colons, brackets, semicolons and dashes, each tried on sentences where the mark matters.
- **Language for people and situations:** formality and register, language and identity, language that builds group roles, and words with shades of meaning (formal or casual, strong or mild, positive or negative). (Renamed from Choosing words after review: its nodes are mostly about register and identity. Figurative language moved to How authors craft stories.)
- **Judging and persuading:** fact or opinion, emotive language and bias, the vocabulary of judgement, backing up a judgement, irony and hyperbole, language that shifts with audience.
- **Writing like a subject expert:** technical and academic words, nominalisation, quoting and referencing. Cross-linked from Science and humanities.
- **How texts are built:** stages of reviews, discussions, explanations, recounts and arguments; idea structures (cause and effect, compare and contrast, problem and solution); hybrids and twists. Uses the genre cycle: take a model apart, build one together, then write.
- **Reading closely:** main idea and summary, inference, skimming and scanning, signposts, comparing sources, judging credibility. Strategies on short texts, never test drill; links to ICT's judging-information lessons.
- **Images and multimodal texts:** diagrams, tables and maps; vectors, angle and social distance; image sequence; visual allusion in made-up adverts.
- **Plan, draft and edit:** the writing process as drop-in moves: plan for purpose and audience, then edit Nib's draft and your own against a checklist. It brings in a **peer feedback card** pattern (a short, specific feedback structure students use on each other's drafts in class), reused by every writing and speaking course. Long drafting stays in class.
- **How authors craft stories:** figurative and emotive language and the words to talk about it, character techniques, setting and mood, point of view, style, devices that build character and layers of meaning, tone, positioning. Extracts written for the site or public domain. Poetry for Years 7–10 lives here (layers of meaning, imagery, voice).
- **Poetry:** imagery and sound devices, and ballad rhythm on public-domain Australian ballads.
- **Writing stories and poems:** reading as a writer, experimenting with devices, adapting a source, editing for effect, in short pieces.
- **Responding to literature:** forming and justifying opinions, engaging with others' views, context and values. Works on the class text; the site scaffolds the talk (class-plan workshops) and the write-up (the existing unmarked `reasoning` check: write, then compare with model answers and a self-check). Built in wave 2 with what exists; the model-range board and highlighting come later.
- **Speaking to an audience:** the speech shape (purpose and audience, plan, script, cue cards, rehearse, deliver, self-check), plus judging spoken texts from transcripts.
- **Discussion moves:** paraphrasing, questioning, building on and challenging others, as talk-move cards practised in class workshops.
- **Spelling through word parts:** base words, prefixes, suffixes, Greek and Latin roots, and spelling generalisations, with endless practice pages.

### Start here: a first-year set

Seventeen courses is too many to start with. For a school trying the site, the plan recommends five: **Holding writing together** (built), **How texts are built**, **Building sentences**, **Judging and persuading** and **How authors craft stories**, plus **Speaking to an audience** for a speech unit. These match the first two waves, and the English teacher page will say so.

My path (`/my-path/`) already lets a teacher save a unit's lessons as one shareable link. A later step could ship these unit lists as ready-made paths.

## Boards

Reuse first. The cohesion board already does much more than cohesion:
- **Chain mode:** colours words by category, so it can mark opinion words, sound devices or evidence.
- **Link mode:** traces what a word points back to, and can also show what a symbol stands for.
- **Parts mode:** labels and orders sentences or paragraphs, so it handles stages of a text and summaries.
- **Gaps:** offer a choice of word in a gap.
- **Write mode:** students write their own text and mark it up themselves.

New boards, one per need:

1. **Sentence board** (Building sentences, Punctuation, Writing like a subject expert). Students drag clause chunks to combine and reorder them, switch active and passive, and drop punctuation into slots. Checks are choose-and-justify plus a few planted structures.
2. **Word choice board** (Language for people and situations, Judging and persuading). Students swap a word for alternatives and place each on a scale: formal to informal, certain to unsure, positive to negative. Scales are rough, not exact.
3. **Image board** (Images and multimodal texts). Drawn scenes, no photos, where students change angle, distance, gaze and framing, plus comic-panel sequences.
4. **Word builder** (Spelling). Students build words from base words, prefixes, suffixes and roots, with practice pages using the maths practice engine.
5. **Cue mode** on the cohesion board (Speaking): already agreed.

**Only one chat changes the cohesion board's code at a time.** Others use its existing modes and note what they need in `roadmap/english.md`.

## Explain it to me: still pictures, not clips

English explanations are mostly *look at this and notice that*, so a marked-up still picture works better than a clip. Students can take their time with it, it needs no sound, and it works on a slide or a printout.
- **Every English lesson gets an Explain-it-to-me picture by default:** the example with its arrows, colours or labels already on it, drawn by the board at build time as maths and science boards are. This needs one small board addition: a **show-the-answer option** on the cohesion board, so the picture shows the finished markings. It is added in wave 1 and given to the nine built cohesion lessons too.
- **Diagrams** (the existing Mermaid feature) where a structure helps: the stages of a text type, or how claim, evidence and explanation link.
- **Small drawn graphics** (inline SVG, like the coding ideas page): a formal-to-informal scale, or clause blocks joining into a sentence.
- **Clips only by exception,** where something really changes over time, such as an image sequence. Speaking models need audio and headphones aren't guaranteed, so they'd be captions-first and optional, or left out.

## Things the site deliberately doesn't do

- **Foundation to Year 2 early literacy** (phonics, decoding, handwriting): schools use dedicated programmes.
- **First Nations content:** where the curriculum names First Nations Australian ways of knowing, cultural protocols, authors or language spellings, the site states the principle and points to the right sources: local community guidance, AIATSIS, and established teaching resources such as Reading Australia (which includes First Nations texts, partly with Magabala Books). The class text and local guidance do the teaching; the site never supplies protocols, spellings or interpretations itself, and never picks the texts. Every literature-context node and every node naming First Nations content carries this note in the data file.
- **Whole-text and multi-text work:** interpreting a novel, summarising an extended text, or comparing an author's style across books needs the real texts. The site scaffolds the discussion and the write-up, and bring-your-own-text (moved up to wave 2) lets a teacher paste a passage from the class text.
- **Test drill:** comprehension is taught as strategies, not exam practice.

## Build order and handoffs

Each wave can run as separate chats in parallel, with the rules below. Each chat proposes its plan for Matthew to approve before working unsupervised, and gets a fresh-eyes review agent at the end, like the cohesion course did.

**Wave 1 (start now, no shared code conflicts):**
- **A. Threads page** "How English skills grow", built from the data file. Shared build code, so one chat, modelled on the coding ideas page.
- **B. Building sentences + Punctuation for meaning.** Builds the new sentence board. Each lesson's Make step uses the move in a short piece of a real text type.
- **C. Language for people and situations + Judging and persuading.** Builds the new word choice board and uses the existing cohesion chain mode.
- **D. How texts are built,** plus the cohesion board's show-the-answer option and Explain-it-to-me pictures for the nine built lessons. D is the only chat that edits the cohesion board in wave 1.

**Wave 2 (after wave 1 settles):**
- **E. Speech shape + Speaking to an audience + cue mode.** Touches Create and the cohesion board, so nothing else may edit those at the same time.
- **F. Plan, draft and edit + the peer feedback card pattern,** then **bring-your-own-text** on the cohesion board (after E, since both touch the board).
- **G. How authors craft stories + Poetry.** Uses public-domain extracts, with Australian copyright status checked (author's death date, not US rules).
- **H. Responding to literature,** with the existing unmarked reasoning check and class-plan workshops.

**Wave 3:**
- **I. Reading closely + Images and multimodal texts.** Builds the new image board.
- **J. Writing stories and poems + Writing like a subject expert,** the latter cross-linked from Science.
- **K. Spelling through word parts,** with practice pages.

**Later (after the workshop format settles):**
- **L. Open-answer upgrades:** a board where students place their answer among model answers, and highlighting the evidence in their own answer.
- **M. Discussion moves.**

**Rules for every English chat:**
- Pull before starting and before every push, and never force-push.
- Notes go in `roadmap/english.md`.
- Tag every lesson with `node:` from the data file, and update the data file's `course:` if a lesson lands somewhere new.
- Run `npm run check-cohesion` and `check-lesson-boards` if you use the cohesion board, and build the matching check for any new board.

## Parked: AI ideas for later (speculative, not in any wave)

The English plan works fully without AI. These ideas, some from tools like MagicSchool and some from the earlier "teach a novice" thinking, are parked:
- **Debate sparring partner:** the AI argues the other side of a student's claim, so they practise rebuttal.
- **Nib as a real AI novice:** the student explains one skill and the AI plays a learner who misunderstands it.
- **Question-only coach:** the AI asks the student questions about their own paragraph and never rewrites it.
- **Character hot seat:** students question a character from the class text. This needs care with accuracy and copyright.

If any of these are picked up, they follow the existing rules:
- optional prompt cards with a no-AI alternative and "only if your teacher says you can";
- AI never marks;
- one skill at a time;
- possibly teacher-led through Waypoints Kit rather than run by students.
