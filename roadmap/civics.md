## Plan (proposed and approved 30 Oct 2026): course `civics/government-7`, *How Australia is governed*

AC9HC7K01 first: it is the gateway (the humanities plan's "Civics: How Australia is governed opens Freedoms and Law and justice"), K02 to K05 all list it as prior knowledge, and it has the Year 6 catch-up already planned for it. One lesson per waypoint, in the node file's order, one unit (`government`). Usually Year 7 (teacher note only). Lesson shape: copy `economics/scarcity-7/needs-and-wants` (`language: maths`, predict, try, worked example with Nib's planted slip, vary, practise, Make in the student's own book with a `reflect` tick, then typical and wider steps; teacher.yml and a class plan; no clips, no board).

**What is real and what is made up.** Civics can't be all made up the way Economics is: the Constitution and the institutions are the content. So: real institutions and stable constitutional facts only (the Constitution, Parliament's two houses, the Governor-General, the High Court, the double majority); everything else made up (people, proposals, election results, a club). No current office holders, no parties, no seat counts or other numbers that change, no live policy debates. Nothing dates and nothing leans.

### Lessons

1. **`origins-and-common-law`** (n1, hinge): *Where our law comes from*
   - **Insight:** Australia's law has two sources: Acts that Parliament passes, and common law built up from judges' past decisions (precedent), applied by judges who don't take sides and can't be told what to decide. The idea that no one is above the law goes back to how people came to read Magna Carta.
   - **Planted slip (`only-parliament-makes-law`):** Nib says "Judges just read out the laws Parliament wrote. They never make law." `smaller:` "A higher court decided a question last year. Does a lower court have to follow it in a new case like it?"
   - **Made-up case:** Lin's case against Wattle Creek Hardware over a ladder that broke: the magistrate uses an Act about product safety and a higher court's past decision.
   - **Distractors:** common law is "law Parliament passed for everyone"; Magna Carta "gave everyone in England the vote"; a judge "can ignore an Act if an older court decision disagrees"; the Prime Minister "can tell a judge how to decide".
   - **Steps:** predict (who decides what the law means in a dispute nobody's law covers exactly?), match (Magna Carta, Act of Parliament, common law, independent judges), worked example and bug, sort (made by Parliament / built by courts / not allowed), Make (two sources of law and one reason judges must be independent). Typical: lower courts follow higher ones (one parsons). Wider: since 1986 the High Court has the final say (no appeals to London).
2. **`rule-of-law-and-justice`** (n2, hinge): *The same law for everyone*
   - **Insight:** under the rule of law the law is public, known in advance and applies to everyone, ministers and police included. In a criminal trial the person charged is presumed innocent, and the prosecution has to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt.
   - **Planted slip (`accused-must-prove-innocence`):** Nib says "Morgan was charged, so Morgan has to prove they didn't do it." `smaller:` "In a criminal trial, who brings the charge, and so who has to prove it?"
   - **Made-up case:** Morgan, an adult in Wattle Creek, is charged with taking a watch from a market stall.
   - **Distractors:** presumption of innocence "means police can't charge anyone"; the accused "must bring an alibi to prove innocence"; rule of law "means strict laws, even if ministers are exempt".
   - **Steps:** predict, match (rule of law, presumption of innocence, burden of proof, right to a lawyer, equal before the law), worked example and bug, sort (fair / unfair steps in the made-up case), Make (two principles and why each protects anyone charged). Typical: a made-up minister breaks a road rule: does the law apply? Wider: a made-up council ignores a court's ruling; what does the rule of law say? Teacher note: Kit's First Nations wider item (above), the civil standard (balance of probabilities) for teachers only.
3. **`courts-and-fair-trials`** (n3): *Courts and a fair trial*
   - **Insight:** courts form a ladder, so cases start at the level that fits and a higher court can check a decision on appeal. In a trial the judge runs it fairly and explains the law, a jury (in most serious criminal trials) decides whether guilt is proved, witnesses give evidence and each side can have a lawyer.
   - **Planted slip (`jury-decides-law-and-sentence`):** Nib says "The jury found Morgan guilty, so now the jury picks the punishment." `smaller:` "In a jury trial, who decides the verdict, and who decides the sentence?"
   - **Made-up case:** a Wattle Creek case that goes to the Magistrates Court, then on appeal to a higher court. One Mermaid `flowchart TD`: state or territory courts and federal courts, both leading to the High Court.
   - **Distractors:** a serious criminal trial "starts in the High Court"; an appeal "asks the Prime Minister to change the verdict"; the judge "tells the jury what verdict to give".
   - **Steps:** predict, parsons (a case moving up on appeal), match (judge, jury, witness, prosecution, defence lawyer), worked example and bug, Make (draw the ladder and one fair-trial element at each step, checked against the diagram). Typical: why ask ordinary citizens to be jurors (question). Wider: federal and state courts side by side (sort).
4. **`access-to-justice-and-democracy`** (n4, integration): *Fair courts and democracy*
   - **Insight:** equal laws only help if people can use them. Legal aid, community legal centres, interpreters and fair steps help people get justice in practice, and independent courts check that governments obey the law and the Constitution. Both support democracy.
   - **Planted slip (`equal-laws-guarantee-equal-access`):** Nib says "The law is the same for everyone, so everyone gets the same chance in court." `smaller:` "One side has a team of lawyers and the other can't afford advice. Is the contest equal?"
   - **Made-up case:** Tarn, an apprentice in Wattle Creek who can't afford a lawyer for a dispute with a landlord, and what help Tarn could look for.
   - **Distractors:** legal aid "gives a free lawyer to anyone who asks"; courts support democracy "by agreeing with whatever the government wants"; access to justice "only matters in criminal trials".
   - **Steps:** predict, match (legal aid, community legal centre, interpreter, independent court), worked example and bug, sort (helps access / doesn't), `reasoning` on paper connecting a fair legal system to one democratic principle (unmarked view, self-check), Make. Typical: courts checking a made-up government action. Wider: discussion only: which barrier matters most where you live (income, distance, language), never marked.

### Plan (1 Nov 2026; Matthew said go, built 1 Nov): the House-majority board in `government-7/representative-and-responsible`
- **Why:** the lesson's hard idea (Kit flags it as counter-intuitive, hinge n2) is that whoever has **majority support in the House** can form government, not simply the biggest side. Today students only read that and answer one question. A board lets them act on it: give or take support and see when a side crosses the line. Light on purpose, like the Economics trade-off board: one row of seats, a count and one line of result; fits a phone.
- **What students see:** a made-up, smaller House of 15 seats (labelled once: "a made-up House; the real one is bigger, but the rule is the same: more than half the members"). Seats are chips in three groups: Side A, Side B and the crossbench ("members in neither side"). Side seats are fixed; each crossbench chip is a button that cycles Undecided → Backs Side A → Backs Side B. Under the row: "Side A 7 + 1 backing = 8 of 15. Majority is 8. Side A can form government." or "No side has a majority yet." A dashed majority line on a bar shows how close each side is. Start again button.
- **Neutral by design:** no party names, colours or logos that read as real parties (Sides A and B get letters and patterns in neutral colours, not red, blue, green or teal); no real seat numbers or elections. Supporting a side is shown as "backs", not "joins" (outside support is in the teacher notes already).
- **Widget:** `playground-board/widgets/house.js` (DOM, about 150 lines, like `consent.js`), one `<script>` line in `playground-board/index.html`, an AUTHORING › `widget: house` entry, a line in `roadmap/shared.md`. Setup: `widget: house`, `seats: 15`, `side A: 7`, `side B: 6`, `crossbench: 2`, optional `backs A: 1` (crossbench backing A at the start). Values for `when:`: a and b (seats backing each side, crossbench included), u (undecided), n (seats), m (majority needed), g (0 none, 1 A, 2 B). Keyboard and screen reader: chips are buttons with `aria-label` ("Crossbench member 1: backs Side A"), the result line is `aria-live`.
- **Where in the lesson (required path):** a new step after the worked example, *Practise: who has the numbers?* (`stage: practise`), board starter `boards.yml#hung` (A 7, B 6, 2 crossbench), two `board` checks:
  1. "Side A has the most seats. Can it form government yet? Give it just enough support to get a majority." `when: "a = 8"` (exactly one backer, so it's the smallest majority, not everyone). `smaller:` a 5-seat House (`boards.yml#hung-5`: A 2, B 2, 1 crossbench), "How many is more than half of 5? Make Side A a majority." `when: "a = 3"`.
  2. "Start again. Could Side B form government instead, with fewer seats than Side A?" `when: "b = 8"`. Explain: the biggest side doesn't automatically govern; majority support does.
  The existing Practise step (two questions) stays; this keeps each step at about three checks.
- **Wider (optional):** a second task in *Wider: how scrutiny works*: starter `boards.yml#governing` (A 7 + 1 backing), "One crossbench member stops backing Side A. Show it, then read what normally happens next." `when: "a < 8"`, followed by the existing note on losing the House's support.

Real example, teacher notes only (Matthew, 1 Nov 2026): the 2010 federal election, the first hung parliament since 1940, where neither major side won a majority of House seats and a minority government formed with the backing of crossbench members. Written factually (who backed whom, no judgement of anyone), checked against the AEC and APH at build, and no seat counts in student text.

## Plan (1 Nov 2026; Matthew said go, built 1 Nov): course `civics/perspectives-7`, *Whose view is it?* (AC9HC7S03)

Per `roadmap/checklist.md` › "Plan first, then build". Tagline *Whose view is this, and whose is missing?* One unit (`perspectives`), Usually Year 7, after `diversity-7` (it can run after any Civics course; `after:` keeps the trail order). Descriptor: "analyse information, data and ideas about political, legal or civic issues to identify and explain differences in perspectives and potential challenges". Prior: AC9HS6S03, AC9HS6S04 (Year 6 HASS skills).

**One made-up issue across all three lessons.** Wattle Creek council (made up, labelled once per lesson) is deciding whether to close Main Street to cars on Saturday mornings for a market. It is local, low-stakes and has no party or national-politics echo, yet real people would genuinely disagree. Six short made-up sources, written for the course (nothing copied, no real outlets, no real people):
- a council notice (the proposal, times, a trial period);
- an editorial in the made-up *Wattle Creek Gazette* (for the market);
- a post from the made-up Main Street Traders group (worried about car access and deliveries, though some traders want the crowds);
- a letter from a made-up resident who uses a wheelchair and parks on Main Street (access);
- a made-up youth council survey of 200 residents (a small table: support, against, unsure, by age group), standing in for the polls and reports ACARA's elaborations mention;
- a bus company timetable note (a route change), a source with a perspective that hardly looks like one.

**Why one issue:** students learn the skill, not the topic, and each lesson builds on sources they already know. The issue is a model: teacher notes suggest using a real local issue in class only if it is not party-political or a live debate in the school community.

**Overlap, said plainly:** English *Who says so?* and *Spot the bias*, ICT *Who made it and why?* and History *Source origin and purpose* teach nearby moves. Lessons mention them in one line ("you may have met this in…"), never assume them (rules: lessons don't assume other pathways). What is Civics' own: perspectives on a **civic decision**, why they differ (values, interests, information, role), and why the decision is hard.

**Real and made up:** all sources, people, groups, the survey and the town are made up. Real only: the general idea that councils consult the public. No current issues, parties, outlets, polls or office holders.

### Lessons

1. **`whose-view`** (n1, hinge): *Whose view is it?*
   - **Insight:** every civic source comes from someone with a position. Ask who made it, what they want to happen, and how that shapes what they put in and leave out. Official sources have a perspective too.
   - **Planted slip (`official-source-is-neutral`):** Nib says "The council notice is from the government, so it's just facts, not a view." `smaller:` "The council notice says the market will 'bring the town together'. Is that a fact, or what the council hopes?"
   - **Distractors:** a perspective is "just someone's opinion, so it's wrong"; the longest source "must be the most balanced"; a survey "has no perspective because it's numbers" (who asked, what question, who answered).
   - **Steps:** predict (which source is neutral?), match each source to who made it and what they want, worked example on the notice and bug, sort (what the Traders' post includes / leaves out), practise (whose view is missing from the editorial?), Make (pick one source: who, what they want, one thing left out). Typical: the bus timetable note as a hidden perspective. Wider: why a source can be accurate and still one-sided.
   - **Shape:** default worked-example: who made it and what it wants have defensible planted answers.
2. **`comparing-views`** (n2): *Why views differ*
   - **Insight:** compare two sources by where they agree and disagree, then explain why: different values (community vs convenience), interests (sales, access), information (one saw the survey, one didn't) or roles (a council must weigh everyone).
   - **Planted slip (`disagreement-means-lying`):** Nib says "The editorial and the traders disagree, so one of them must be lying." `smaller:` "Can two people look at the same street and want different things for good reasons?"
   - **Distractors:** they disagree "because one has the facts wrong" (sometimes, but not here); the side with more people "has the better view"; reasons are "just feelings".
   - **Board (light, existing tool):** the `cohesion` board in parts mode on two short made-up sources, marking the claim each makes and one word that shows its values (planted answers only, as in English *Spot the bias*). Reuses a built widget; no new code. If the parts mode doesn't fit civic sources cleanly at build time, drop the board and use a `sort` (say so in the roadmap).
   - **Steps:** predict, board Try (mark claim and values words in each source), worked example (one point of agreement, one of disagreement, one reason) and bug, sort reasons (values / interests / information / role), practise on the resident's letter vs the survey, Make (compare two sources: agree, disagree, why). Typical: the survey by age group (what younger and older residents said). Wider: a factual disagreement vs a values disagreement, and which more evidence could settle.
   - **Shape:** default.
3. **`hard-to-settle`** (n3, integration): *Why it's hard to decide*
   - **Insight:** a civic decision is hard when good values pull in different directions (a lively street vs access for people who need to park), interests compete, or evidence is missing. Counting supporters doesn't settle a values trade-off on its own; a council can trial, adjust, or find a middle way, and some people may still lose out.
   - **Planted slip (`majority-settles-it`):** Nib says "More people in the survey support the market, so there's no problem left to solve." `smaller:` "If most people want it, what happens to the resident who can't reach the shops?"
   - **Distractors:** a challenge is hard "because people don't understand the facts"; the council should "do whatever the loudest group wants"; a compromise "means everyone gets everything".
   - **Steps:** predict, match each challenge to what makes it hard (competing values, competing interests, missing evidence), worked example on the access problem and bug, sort possible council responses (trial, accessible parking zone, delivery window, doing nothing) by which challenge each addresses, practise, Make (one challenge, why it's hard, one way a council might respond and who might still be unhappy). Typical: what a trial period can and can't tell you. Wider: discussion only, never marked: "Should the market go ahead?" with reasons on more than one side; the teacher keeps it about Wattle Creek.
   - **Shape:** default for the required path (the challenges and responses have planted answers); the decision itself stays unmarked discussion.

## Plan (1 Nov 2026; Matthew said go, built 1 Nov): course `civics/values-7`, *Values and living together* (AC9HC7K05)

Per `roadmap/checklist.md` › "Plan first, then build". Tagline *What shared values hold a diverse society together?* One unit (`values`), Usually Year 7, after `diversity-7`. Descriptor: "how values based on freedom, respect, fairness and equality of opportunity can support social cohesion and democracy within Australian society". Prior: K02, K04.

**This is the most values-heavy course, so the ground rules from `diversity-7` apply, plus:**
- **"Australian values" are labelled as values often named as Australian** (by ACARA and in government documents such as the citizenship pledge), not as values only Australians hold or that every Australian lives up to (T8). Many are shared by other democracies; the lessons say so.
- **The hinge (n2) is evaluation:** whether stated values are lived. Students judge with evidence, and the site marks only the evidence-reading (what a figure shows, what would count as a gap), never a student's overall verdict.
- **No current debates in student text:** no immigration levels, offshore processing, Voice referendum, flag or anthem disputes, or named groups being blamed. Settled facts only (dates of the anthem's adoption and its 2021 wording change; what the citizenship pledge says; legal definitions).
- **Nothing personal asked (T4, firm):** no step asks a student's background, migration story, religion or family. Make steps use the lesson's sources.
- **No made-up hardship stories:** made-up people are fine for everyday situations, but we don't invent a refugee's trauma. Reasons people come are taught as general categories; teacher notes link real first-person accounts for teachers to choose from.

### Lessons

1. **`values-we-name`** (n1): *Values we name*
   - **Insight:** values often named as Australian include freedom, respect, fairness, equality of opportunity, a "fair go", compassion, inclusion and religious freedom. They show up in public life through customs and protocols (the national anthem, flag ceremonies, citizenship ceremonies) and through how institutions work (equal votes, the rule of law). Many democracies name similar values.
   - **Planted slip (`values-are-uniquely-australian`):** Nib says "A fair go is an Australian value, so people in other countries don't care about fairness." `smaller:` "Could people in another country also want everyone to have a fair chance?"
   - **Made-up case:** a made-up Wattle Creek citizenship ceremony (the pledge is real public text, quoted in part).
   - **Distractors:** a value is "a law you can be fined for breaking"; showing a value means "singing the anthem, nothing more"; a fair go means "everyone ends up with the same".
   - **Steps:** predict, match values to plain meanings, worked example on the ceremony (which values does the pledge name?) and bug, sort (a value / how it's expressed), practise, Make (three values and one way each shows up in public life). Typical: the anthem was adopted in 1984 and one line changed on 1 January 2021 ("young and free" to "one and free"); what the change was meant to express (settled facts, checked at build). Wider: is a "fair go" a description or an aim? Unmarked discussion.
2. **`values-and-cohesion`** (n2, hinge): *Do we live our values?*
   - **Insight:** social cohesion is people from different backgrounds getting along, trusting each other and feeling they belong. Shared values can support it, but a stated value isn't proof it's lived: to judge, look for evidence of where values are and aren't reflected.
   - **Planted slip (`stated-value-means-lived`):** Nib says "Australia says everyone gets a fair go, so everyone does." `smaller:` "If a town says its library is open to everyone, what would you check to see if it really is?"
   - **Evidence (your call, below):** either real, dated figures from a public survey of social cohesion (the Scanlon Institute's *Mapping Social Cohesion*, on the ICT `sheet` board, checked at build) or a made-up Wattle Creek survey in the same shape.
   - **Distractors:** one person's story "proves it for everyone"; a gap "means the value is fake"; cohesion "means everyone agrees".
   - **Steps:** predict, match (cohesion terms: belonging, trust, inclusion), worked example (what does this figure show, and what doesn't it?) and bug, sort (evidence that a value is lived / evidence of a gap / not evidence either way), practise, Make (one value, one piece of evidence it is lived, one of a gap). Typical: aspiration vs reality in one made-up Wattle Creek case. Wider: unmarked discussion, "To what extent are these values reflected in Australian society?" with reasons on more than one side.
   - **First Nations:** reconciliation (in the elaborations and Kit's typical width) gets one transparency line in student text, as in `diversity-7`: "Reconciliation between First Nations and other Australians is part of this; your teacher may take it up with local material." Teacher notes quote the elaboration and point to First Nations-led sources once a First Nations educator has looked.
   - **Shape:** default; the hinge check is on reading evidence, which has defensible planted answers.
3. **`values-across-traditions`** (n3): *Values across traditions*
   - **Insight:** Christian traditions shaped many of Australia's institutions (met in *A diverse democracy*; one recap line, no repeat). Other religions practised in Australia, such as Judaism, Buddhism, Islam and Hinduism, have their own values and beliefs, and many share values with each other and with Australian civic life, such as giving to people in need and caring for others. Religious freedom lets them all be practised.
   - **Depth (your call, below):** one checked, plain line per tradition about one shared value (giving), using the tradition's own term (for example tzedakah, dana, zakat, seva, and Christian charity), sourced from the communities' own educational material. No summaries of whole belief systems.
   - **Planted slip (`traditions-share-nothing`):** Nib says "Different religions have nothing in common, so they can't share any values." `smaller:` "Do people in many traditions think it's good to help someone in need?"
   - **Distractors:** religious freedom "means you can do anything if it's religious" (limits from *Within the bounds of law*); "only one religion shaped Australian law" (Western and other sources too); "shared values mean the beliefs are the same".
   - **Steps:** predict, match each tradition's term to "giving to people in need" (and one to a different value, so it isn't a pattern), worked example and bug, sort (shared value / belief particular to a tradition / civic value), practise, Make. Typical: religious freedom and its limits (links to K02). Wider: unmarked: where shared values meet different beliefs, how does a pluralist society live with the difference?
   - **First Nations:** the elaborations name Christianity's impacts on First Nations communities; covered by the one line already in `diversity-7` lesson 2; teacher notes point there.
4. **`why-people-come`** (n4, integration): *Why people come to Australia*
   - **Insight:** people migrate to Australia for many reasons: work, family, study, safety, freedom. A **refugee** is someone who has had to flee their country because of a well-founded fear of persecution (the Refugee Convention's definition, in plain words); a **migrant** chooses to move. Becoming a citizen, people pledge loyalty to Australia and its people, and to share its democratic beliefs, respect its rights and liberties, and uphold and obey its laws (the pledge, checked at build), connecting back to lesson 1.
   - **Planted slip (`migrant-same-as-refugee`):** Nib says "Migrants and refugees are the same: they all just decided to move." `smaller:` "Did a person who fled a war choose to leave in the same way as someone who moved for a job?"
   - **Distractors:** people come "only for money"; becoming a citizen "means giving up your culture" (pluralism, from K04); refugees "aren't allowed to become citizens".
   - **Steps:** predict, sort reasons (pushed to leave / drawn to Australia), worked example on the pledge and bug, match (migrant, refugee, permanent resident, citizen), practise, Make (two reasons people come, one value from the pledge and how it connects). Typical: the difference between a permanent resident and a citizen (voting). Wider: unmarked: how well might life in Australia match what newcomers hope for? What would help? (no policy positions; teacher-led).
   - **Shape:** default; definitions and reasons have defensible planted answers, the matching-to-reality question is discussion.
