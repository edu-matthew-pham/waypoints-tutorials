# Plan: *Teaching when AI is everywhere* (a staff course) (19 Oct 2026)

**Draft for Matthew. Design only; nothing built.** Grew out of the staff-training gap in *AI at our school* (`roadmap/ai-committee-plan.md` › Update 19 Oct). The committee course now teaches students and staff to *notice* know-how gaps (lesson 10: rule problem vs know-how problem; lesson 11: "who needs to know, and how"). This course is where staff *close* them.

## What it is
A short, self-paced course for teachers under the **Teachers** menu, beside *Make a lesson* and *Choosing AI tools*. Same format as *Make a lesson*: **do first, then the why**. Each teacher brings a task they're setting in the next fortnight, and leaves with it redesigned, plus a task instruction card students can read.

- **About the task, not the tool.** No product training. Products appear only in dated teacher notes.
- **No AI required.** One optional step lets teachers try their own task in a school-approved tool. Every activity works without it.
- **Any subject.** Examples rotate across subjects (an English essay, a science report, a maths problem set, a history source task, a design folio).
- **Runs in a staff meeting.** Facilitator notes give a 50-minute session for lessons 1–2 and one for lessons 3–4.

## Lessons (one move each; about 20–30 minutes)
1. **What thinking is this task for?** Name the thinking a task exists to build, then sort its parts into "keep the thinking", "AI can help" and "depends on the goal" (the student course's lesson 1, for staff). *Checks:* sort parts of an example task; reasoning: name the thinking your own task is for.
2. **Could AI do it now?** Test the task against what AI can do *now*, not what it couldn't do last year: write fluent prose, solve and check maths by running code, find and link sources, read images and diagrams, act (open a quiz, fill it in, submit). Optional: try it in a school-approved tool. *Checks:* sort example tasks by which parts AI could finish alone; reflect on your own.
3. **Make the thinking visible.** Process evidence instead of detection: drafts, checkpoints in class, a short in-class piece, an explanation in the student's own words, sources and notes. Why detection fails (unreliable; wrongly flags second-language writers). *Checks:* sort redesigns (makes thinking visible / relies on catching people / just adds work); reasoning: redesign one part of your task.
4. **Set the AI level and say it clearly.** The task-instructions layer: how much AI this task allows, what students should acknowledge, and that owning up isn't the offence. Assessed work follows outside rules first (the IB academic integrity policy and its AI appendix; the BSSS guide for ACT senior students). *Checks:* match instruction cards to tasks; produce: a task instruction card for your task.
5. **Your own use.** AI for planning, feedback drafts and report comments: a person decides and owns what goes out, student work only into school-checked tools, and say when AI helped. Points to the school's staff conduct and privacy lines (the map). *Checks:* sort staff uses (fine / fine with conditions / not with student data); reflect.
6. **Judge a tool** (optional). A short route through *Choosing AI tools* (the trial checklist and "Can we trust it?"), not a repeat of it.

## Teacher layer
`teacher.yml` notes are written for a **PD facilitator** (a head of department or the AI committee's staff lead): what to bring (a task set in the next fortnight), pairs swapping tasks for lesson 2's test, and how to collect know-how gaps for the committee (they feed lesson 10's rule vs know-how sort in *AI at our school*).

## Decisions for Matthew
1. **Title:** *Teaching when AI is everywhere* (working). Alternatives: *Tasks that keep the thinking*, *AI and your tasks*.
2. **Where it lives:** a course at `content/teachers/ai-and-your-tasks/` in the Teachers menu (recommended), or a section added to *Choosing AI tools*.
3. **AI levels:** use generic levels in examples (no AI; AI for ideas; AI for feedback; AI throughout, acknowledged) and tell schools to use their own scale if they have one (recommended), or adopt a published scale. If a published scale is named, check it at its source and date it.
4. **Lesson 2's optional try-it step:** keep it (school-approved tool only, teacher's own account, no student data), or drop it so the course never touches an AI tool.
5. **Assessment depth:** generic plus dated IB and BSSS pointers in teacher notes (recommended), or a CGGS-specific version.
6. **Order with *Make a lesson*:** separate courses in the same menu; neither depends on the other.

## Before building

- Check the capability examples in lesson 2 against vendors' own pages and date them; keep names out of the course text.
